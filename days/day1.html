Who studies what? Gender, language, and origin in university departments

Walk through any university campus and you will quickly notice that different academic disciplines attract very different kinds of students. A lecture hall in the humanities faculty — full of students studying English literature or Modern Languages — tends to look quite different from one in the sciences, where Physics, Chemistry, or Mathematics are taught. These differences are not just about subject matter; they extend to gender, linguistic background, and national origin.

Data from universities around the world consistently show that arts and language-based courses enrol a significantly higher proportion of female students than STEM subjects. In language departments such as English or Modern Languages, women account for over two-thirds of the student population in many institutions. By contrast, Mathematics and Chemistry tend to attract a much smaller share of female enrolments — sometimes as low as 15–20%.

Another dimension worth examining is students' first language. What stands out is that science and mathematics programmes tend to have a noticeably higher percentage of students whose first language is not English, often approaching or exceeding half of the cohort. The English department, perhaps unsurprisingly, typically has the lowest proportion of non-native English speakers — usually around 10–15%.

Closely linked to language background is where students were born. Physics and Mathematics show a markedly higher figure — more than half of their students may have been born outside the host country. Literary and linguistic fields record the lowest figures, with only around one in five students born abroad.

Overall, the picture that emerges is one of sharp variation across faculties. It is also worth noting that History and Physics sit somewhere in the middle across all three measures, making them interdisciplinary bridge subjects in demographic terms.

Sample answer

Model response, with useful comparison language marked.

The table shows the percentages of students in six academic departments at an Australian university in 2011, by gender, first language, and birthplace. Overall, the humanities — particularly English and Modern Languages — had large female majorities and comparatively low international representation, whereas History was an exception. In contrast, the science-based departments, especially Mathematics and Chemistry, were predominantly male and showed substantially higher shares of overseas-born students and of those whose first language was not English.

In the humanities, females formed clear majorities: 72% in English and 68% in Modern Languages. These two departments also recorded the lowest shares of students whose first language was not English (12% and 16%, respectively) and comparatively modest overseas-born figures (20% and 23%). However, History was more gender-balanced, with women constituting a small majority (52%). At the same time, linguistic diversity was considerably higher than in the other humanities, with 45% having a first language other than English and nearly one-third (31%) overseas-born.

The science-based departments showed contrasting gender and international profiles. Mathematics (17% female) and Chemistry (15% female) showed very low female representation but strong international profiles: 49% and 42% had a first language other than English, 56% and 48% were overseas-born, respectively. By contrast, Physics had a female majority (56%), yet it also showed one of the most international profiles, with 38% whose first language was not English and 56% overseas-born.
