The Infinite Career: What Happens When Retirement Becomes Optional?

In 1937, when social security frameworks were first drafted, life expectancy sat comfortably below seventy. Setting a mandatory retirement age around sixty-five was simple arithmetic. Nearly a century later, that arithmetic has collapsed: statutory age limits are disappearing from the workforce across several major economies.

The case for the senior workforce is straightforward: as birth rates plunge, older workers keep contributing to the tax base rather than drawing immediately from pension funds. Beyond public balance sheets, decades of institutional memory and crisis-management skill mean senior employees often outperform entry-level recruits — there are human dividends as well, since work provides social structure and identity that sudden retirement abruptly severs.

Between 2000 and today, the share of the workforce aged 65+ has risen from roughly 12% to 31% in several economies — yet the elimination of retirement boundaries is not without friction. The primary concern is intergenerational equity: when senior staff remain in specialised posts late into their seventies, promotional pathways for younger cohorts freeze, delaying pay rises and milestones like homeownership.

Managing an ageing staff also poses delicate operational hurdles — evaluating a long-serving employee objectively, without age discrimination, is a legal minefield. The shift toward open-ended working lives represents more than a legislative tweak; the classic three-stage life of education, work, and retirement is giving way to a far more fluid trajectory.

Sample answer

Model essay, with useful argument language marked.

Some countries allow people to work for as long as they choose. Although this approach can strengthen public finances, this essay argues that the disadvantages — especially safety and productivity risks in physically demanding jobs — outweigh the benefits.

Allowing seniors to work as long as they wish can shore up public finances: they continue paying income tax and social-insurance contributions while drawing pensions later, which eases pressure on state budgets. For example, Japan and Singapore have extended re-employment frameworks to around 70, keeping healthy retirees in part-time municipal, retail, and advisory roles. Governments benefit from steady tax receipts, and employers retain reliable, low-turnover staff who can mentor juniors. However, I disagree with prioritizing this benefit because indefinite tenure can block promotion pipelines. In sectors with rigid hierarchies — such as the civil service or universities — senior posts remain occupied longer, so fewer vacancies open for graduates and mid-career staff. This, in turn, slows salary growth, prompts talented youth to seek opportunities elsewhere, and ultimately undercuts long-term productivity.

However, implementing this policy is unsafe in physically demanding sectors, where peak reflexes, balance, and strength are non-negotiable. As workers age, slower reaction and longer recovery times raise accident risks on ladders, scaffolds, and night highways. This, in turn, inflates insurance premiums, disrupts the schedule, and drags down team productivity as tasks are reallocated or extra staff hired. For example, in construction and logistics, firms in France and South Korea have introduced "adapted duties" for older staff — lighter shifts, mentor/supervisor roles, and daytime routing — because full-duty work past 65 proved high-risk and costly. Therefore, I agree with the view that unlimited work ages are problematic in manual jobs: a blanket rule ignores sector-specific physiology and externalizes hidden costs onto co-workers, employers, and the public.

To conclude, despite budgetary gains, unrestricted employment at any age compromises safety and productivity in physically taxing roles, so the disadvantages outweigh the benefits.
