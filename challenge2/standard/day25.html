What Happens to Your Body When You Exercise?

Health · Science · 316 words

Imagine you have just started running for a bus. Within a few seconds, your heart is beating faster and you are breathing more quickly. This is your body responding to a simple message from your muscles: we need more oxygen. Muscles use oxygen to turn the food we eat into energy, so the harder they work, the more oxygen they need, and the heart and lungs must work harder to deliver it.

If you keep going, other changes follow. Your body temperature rises, because working muscles produce heat. To stop you from overheating, your skin starts to sweat: as the sweat dries, it carries heat away from the body. This is why it is important to drink water during and after exercise, especially on a hot day.

Exercise does not only affect the body. Many people say they feel calmer and happier after a game of football or a long walk, and scientists believe that this is because exercise changes the chemicals in the brain. For some people, a short run is the best way to stop worrying about an exam.

The biggest changes, however, come with regular exercise over weeks and months. The heart is a muscle, and like other muscles it becomes stronger when it is used. A stronger heart can pump more blood with each beat, so it does not need to beat as often. This is why fit people often have a lower heart rate when they are resting.

There is one more surprise. Muscles do not actually become stronger during exercise — they become stronger while they recover afterwards. Hard training causes tiny amounts of damage, and the body repairs the muscle so that it is a little stronger than before. For this reason, coaches say that rest days are as important as training days. Doing too much, too often, is one of the most common reasons why people get injured.

The Park Run Phenomenon
How a small Saturday-morning run in one London park became a weekly event for people all over the world.

Reading Passage 1 · Sport and community · 577 words

AEvery Saturday morning, in parks across the world, crowds of people gather to run five kilometres together. Nobody pays to take part, nobody wins a medal, and many of the participants do not even run — they walk, jog or push a baby in a buggy. A few hours later, each finisher receives a message with their time. The event is called parkrun, and in about twenty years it has grown from a small gathering in a single park into one of the largest regular sporting events on the planet.

BThe story began in October 2004 in Bushy Park, in south-west London. Its founder was an experienced club runner who had been injured and could no longer race. Missing both running and his running friends, he decided to organise a weekly timed run that anyone could join. Only thirteen people took part in the first event. The times were recorded with a stopwatch, and the results were typed into a computer afterwards and sent out to the runners.

CAt first, the idea spread slowly, mainly because people who had enjoyed the run told their friends about it. There was no advertising campaign. Gradually, runners in other parts of Britain asked whether they could start an event in their own local park, and later the idea travelled abroad. Today there are events in more than twenty countries, from Australia and South Africa to Japan. A shorter version, a two-kilometre run for children aged four to fourteen, now takes place on Sunday mornings.

DThe rules are deliberately simple. Before your first run, you register once on the website and print a personal barcode. At the finish, a volunteer scans this barcode together with a token that shows your finishing position, and that is how your time is calculated. The organisers insist that taking part will always be free; the costs are covered by sponsors and donations. They also insist that it is 'a run, not a race'. Every event has a volunteer called the tail walker, who stays at the back, so no participant ever finishes in last place.

EVolunteers are, in fact, at the heart of the whole idea. There are no paid staff at the events themselves: marshals stand at corners to point the way, timekeepers record the finishing times, and other helpers set out the course and pack it away again. Many regular runners volunteer several times a year, and some people help every week without ever running at all. For them, the attraction is the friendly atmosphere and the feeling of belonging to a community.

FParkrun also seems to have health benefits, and not only for keen runners. When researchers surveyed participants, a large number described themselves as inactive before they started, and many said that their fitness and mood had improved since then. Because of results like these, some doctors in Britain now encourage patients to try their local parkrun, either by running or by volunteering.

GNot everyone has welcomed the events. In some busy parks, people have complained about crowds of runners on narrow paths or about damage to the grass. In one well-known case, a local council proposed charging a fee for the use of its park, and the event closed rather than give up its free principle. For most people, however, the event is something to look forward to at the end of the week — and the coffee and conversation afterwards are, for many, almost as important as the run.

PE Lessons: More Than Just Games?

Education · Sport · 304 words

In many schools, physical education — PE — is treated as a less serious subject than maths, science or languages. There are no difficult exams, students rarely get homework, and when the timetable is full, PE is often the first lesson to lose time. Some parents even ask for their children to miss PE so that they can study for other subjects.

Supporters of PE say this is a serious mistake. A good PE programme does far more than let children run around. It teaches basic movement skills, such as throwing, catching, balancing and swimming, which children need if they are going to enjoy sport later in life. It also teaches them how to work in a team, how to follow rules, and how to win and lose with dignity.

There is a health argument too. For some children, especially those whose families cannot pay for sports clubs, PE lessons may be the only regular exercise they get. Doctors in many countries are worried that children today are less active than children in the past, and a weekly PE lesson is one of the few times when every child moves.

However, PE has its critics among students themselves. Many adults remember PE with horror: being chosen last for a team, feeling embarrassed in front of classmates, or standing in the cold on a football pitch for an hour. Students who are not naturally sporty can come to believe that exercise is simply not for them.

For this reason, some schools are changing the way they teach PE. Instead of focusing only on competitive team games, they offer a wider choice, such as dance, yoga, climbing or cycling. The idea is that every student should find at least one activity they enjoy — because, as the next passage shows, many teenagers stop doing sport altogether.

Why Do Teenagers Give Up Sport?
Children love to play — so why do so many stop when they reach their teens? A youth coach looks at the evidence.

Reading Passage 2 · Sport and young people · 500 words

AWatch any primary school playground and you will see children running, jumping and chasing balls with enormous energy. Visit the same children six or seven years later, and the picture is often very different. Surveys carried out in several countries suggest that participation in sport falls sharply between the ages of about eleven and sixteen, and that the fall is often steeper among girls than among boys. For a coach like me, this is one of the most worrying trends in youth sport.

BThe first reason is simple: for many young people, sport stops being fun. As children get older, sport becomes more organised and more serious. Adults start keeping league tables, coaches pick their strongest players for important matches, and weaker players spend more and more time on the bench. I believe that adults are often responsible for this. When winning becomes more important than enjoyment, the children who are not winning soon find something else to do.

CA second reason is time. Teenagers have more homework, more exams and, often, part-time jobs. They also have a busier social life, much of it on their phones. Sport has to compete with all of these things, and it often loses. Some students tell me that they have stopped training 'just until the exams are over' — but very few come back. In my view, exams are not a good reason to give up exercise completely. Even a short run can help students to concentrate, and they need that more than ever when they are revising.

DFor many teenagers, and especially for girls, the problem is also how they feel about their bodies. At an age when young people are very aware of how they look, wearing tight sports clothes or getting changed in front of others can be extremely uncomfortable. Some schools have reported that more students took part in PE after they were allowed to choose their own sports clothes.

EA fourth reason may surprise some parents. Children who are talented at a sport are sometimes pushed to focus on that one sport from a very young age, training several times a week all year round. This can lead to injuries caused by repeating the same movements, and to 'burnout' — a feeling of being so tired of a sport that the young person never wants to play it again. Many experts now recommend that children should try a range of different sports before they specialise, and I strongly agree.

FSo what can be done? Clubs and schools can offer activities that are not competitive, such as dance, climbing or cycling, for teenagers who do not want to compete. They can give teenagers a say in what they do and when they do it. And they can train coaches to praise effort rather than results, so that every player feels valued. The aim of youth sport should not be to produce a few champions; it should be to produce adults who are still active at forty, sixty and eighty.

Speaking Part 2 — A Sport or Activity You Enjoy

In Part 2 you have 1 minute to prepare and should speak for 1–2 minutes. First read a model answer and notice how it is organised, then do the task yourself.

Part 2 task card

Describe a sport or physical activity that you enjoy doing.

You should say:
  what the sport or activity is
  when and where you do it
  who you do it with
and explain why you enjoy it.

Model answer — Aziz

I’d like to talk about swimming, which is something I’ve been doing since I was about eight years old. My father took me to the pool near our house one summer, and at first I was terrified of the water, but after a few lessons I was hooked.

These days I go swimming two or three times a week, usually early in the morning before university, because the pool is almost empty at that time. It’s an indoor pool, so I can go all year round, even in January when it’s freezing outside.

I normally go with my cousin, Sardor. We push each other to swim a little further each week — at the moment we’re trying to swim two kilometres without stopping, and we’re nearly there.

As for why I enjoy it so much, I think there are two main reasons. First, it’s great for my fitness — it works almost every muscle in the body, and it doesn’t hurt my knees like running does. But the main reason is that it clears my head. When I’m in the water, I can’t look at my phone or think about exams, so after half an hour I feel completely relaxed. To be honest, I can’t imagine my week without it.

Analyse the model. Answer the questions about Aziz’s talk.

1. How did Aziz feel about water when he first started?

2. Why does he go swimming early in the morning?

3. What is Aziz’s current goal?

4. What is the MAIN reason he enjoys swimming?

Useful language. Find a phrase in the model answer that means…

1. very enthusiastic about something, unable to stop → I was

2. extremely cold → it’s outside

3. encourage each other to try harder → we each other

4. stops you thinking about your worries → it my head

5. introducing the reasons part of the talk → As why I enjoy it…

Speaking Part 3 — Sport and Society

Part 3 questions are more general and more abstract: you talk about people and society, not just yourself. Strong answers give an opinion, a reason and an example, and often look at both sides.

Model Part 3 answer

Examiner: Why do you think some people don’t do any exercise?

Candidate: I think the main reason is lack of time. Many adults work long hours, and when they get home they’re simply too tired to go to a gym. Another reason might be money — in my city, for example, gym memberships are quite expensive. On the other hand, I’d say some people just haven’t found an activity they enjoy. If they tried something like dancing or hiking instead of running on a machine, they might actually like it.

Pronunciation — Word Stress in Sport and Health Words

In words with more than one syllable, one syllable is stressed more strongly than the others. Wrong stress is a big reason why listeners misunderstand a speaker, even when every sound is correct. Say each word aloud, clapping on the stressed syllable.

Rules of thumb (not perfect, but useful): most two-syllable nouns and adjectives are stressed on the 1st syllable (FOOT·ball, HEAL·thy); words ending in -tion / -sion are stressed just before the ending (com·pe·TI·tion); words ending in -ic are stressed just before -ic (a·THLE·tic).
