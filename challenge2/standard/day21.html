The Trap Lab — What Sunday's Mock Taught Us

Day 21 is a reading day — and a hard one. Before we start, look at four questions from your Day 20 mock that many Standard students got wrong. Each one is a classic IELTS trap. Learn the trap, then avoid it all day today.

Standard group, Day 20 reading: only 46% got Question 2 right, 36% got Question 20 right, and 38% got Question 22 right. These were not "difficult English" questions — they were trap questions.

1. Mock Question 2. Statement: "Soap has enjoyed widespread popularity in Europe for over a thousand years." The text said: "the benefits of soap finally managed to appeal to a wide European population in the 17th century." The answer is FALSE. Why?

2. Mock Question 20. Paragraph H was about the government advertising Canada abroad: agents, pamphlets and letters home. Many students chose the heading "Attracting urban environment". What was the trap?

3. Mock Question 22. Instructions: NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS. Gap: "...the West could increase ______ of Canada." Which answer gets the mark?

4. The NOT GIVEN rule. Text: "The metro opened in 1977." Statement: "The metro was the most expensive building project in the city."

Today's three rules

  • Numbers and time words (over, only, first, all, never, before) decide TRUE/FALSE. Check them word by word.
  • Same word ≠ same meaning. If a heading or option repeats a word from the text, be suspicious, not happy.
  • Count the words in every gap answer. "the" is a word.

Task 3 of 9

Reading 1 — The Sea That Walked Away

Suggested time: 20 minutes for Tasks 3–6. Read the whole passage once quickly for the main idea of each paragraph. Then choose the correct heading for paragraphs B–G. There are more headings than paragraphs — and three of them are traps.

The Sea That Walked Away

Environment · Central Asia · 633 words

AIn 1960, a fisherman in the town of Muynak, in the north-west of Uzbekistan, could walk out of his front door and see the water. The Aral Sea, which was actually a giant lake, covered about 68,000 square kilometres and was the fourth-largest lake on the planet. Two great rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, carried water from the mountains far to the south-east and kept it full. Its fish fed people across the Soviet Union, and Muynak had a busy port and a large fish factory.

BThen the planners arrived. In the 1960s, the Soviet government decided that Central Asia should become one of the world's biggest producers of cotton, a crop that needs enormous amounts of water in a hot, dry climate. Thousands of kilometres of irrigation canals were dug, and water from both rivers was taken away to the fields. Many of the canals were simply channels cut into the sand without any lining, so a large share of the water soaked into the ground or evaporated before it ever reached a cotton plant. By the 1980s, in some years almost no river water reached the sea at all.

CThe result was one of the worst environmental disasters of the twentieth century. The sea began to shrink, slowly at first and then at an alarming speed. By 1987 it had divided into two separate parts: a small northern sea in Kazakhstan and a larger southern sea, most of it in Uzbekistan. By the late 2000s, the Aral had lost about ninety per cent of its water, and in 2014 satellite photographs showed that the eastern part of the southern sea had dried up completely for the first time in modern history.

DAs the water disappeared, what was left became saltier and saltier, until most of the native fish could no longer survive. The fishing industry, which had once provided tens of thousands of jobs, collapsed. Today Muynak lies more than a hundred kilometres from the shore, and the rusting fishing boats that still sit on the sand have become a strange kind of tourist attraction. Visitors take photographs of them, but for the people who remember the port, the ships are a painful reminder of how quickly a way of life can vanish.

EThe damage did not stop at the old shoreline. The dry seabed, now a new desert known as the Aralkum, is covered in salt mixed with chemicals that once ran off the cotton fields. Strong winds lift this dust and carry it hundreds of kilometres, and doctors in the region have linked it to breathing problems and other illnesses. Without the sea to soften the weather, summers in the area have become hotter and winters colder.

FHowever, the story is not only one of loss. In 2005, Kazakhstan finished building a dam about thirteen kilometres long that separates the northern sea from the southern one. Water from the Syr Darya now stays in the north, and the level there has risen by several metres. Fish have returned, and some local fishermen are working again. The southern sea, which depends on the Amu Darya, has not been saved in the same way, and most experts agree that it will never return to its old size.

GInstead, Uzbekistan has chosen a different goal: rather than trying to bring back the water, it is trying to hold down the land. In recent years, workers have planted saxaul, a tough desert shrub with very deep roots, across large areas of the dry seabed. The plants help to stop the salty dust from blowing away, although many young plants die before they become established. Nobody believes the new forests will replace the sea, but supporters argue that they may at least make the region safer for the people who stayed.

Reading 2 — Palaces Under the City

Suggested time: 15 minutes for Tasks 7–8. A shorter passage about a place many of you use. Answer the questions with NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER from the passage.

Palaces Under the City

Cities · Tashkent · 399 words

AOn 26 April 1966, a powerful earthquake struck the centre of Tashkent. It was not the strongest earthquake the region had ever known, but it hit directly under the city, and thousands of buildings — many of them old houses made of mud brick — were destroyed or badly damaged. Hundreds of thousands of people lost their homes. The city was rebuilt with help from across the Soviet Union, and when planners later decided to give Tashkent an underground railway, they knew it would have to survive the next big earthquake.

BThe Tashkent Metro opened on 6 November 1977 and was the first underground railway in Central Asia. Its tunnels and stations were designed with earthquakes in mind, using strengthened structures and special joints that allow the tunnels to move a little without breaking.

CYet visitors rarely talk about the engineering. What they remember is the decoration. Each station was designed around its own theme. Kosmonavtlar station, built in honour of space exploration, is decorated in deep blue, with round portraits of cosmonauts, including Yuri Gagarin and Vladimir Dzhanibekov, who was born in the Tashkent region. Alisher Navoi station has domes and ceramic panels showing scenes from the great poet's works. Other stations use marble, granite, carved plaster and huge chandeliers.

DFor decades, however, very few outsiders saw these underground "palaces" in photographs. The metro was also designed to serve as a shelter in case of nuclear war, and taking pictures inside it was strictly forbidden; police officers regularly stopped tourists who tried. The ban was finally lifted in 2018, and within months images of the stations were being shared all over the internet, turning the metro into one of the city's most popular attractions.

EThe system has continued to grow. A third line, the Yunusobod line, opened in 2001, and in the 2020s a new ring line — much of it running above the ground rather than below it — began to connect districts on the edge of the expanding city. The newer stations are lighter and simpler than the old ones, which some residents welcome as modern and others criticise as ordinary.

FToday the metro carries hundreds of thousands of passengers every day. For most of them it is simply the cheapest and quickest way across town. For visitors, however, the stations remain proof that a public building does not have to be plain to be practical.

1. In which year did the Tashkent Metro open?

2. What were many of the old damaged houses made of?

3. Which colour is used most in Kosmonavtlar station?

4. Apart from transport, what was the metro also designed to be?

5. Who used to stop tourists taking photographs?

6. Where does much of the newest line run?
