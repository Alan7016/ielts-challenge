Creatures of Habit

Psychology · Everyday life · 295 words

Think about how your day started. You probably woke up at a similar time to yesterday, did the same things in the same order, and perhaps took the same route to school or work. Psychologists say that a large part of what we do each day is not really decided at all — it is habit.

Habits are useful. When an action becomes automatic, the brain does not have to think about it, so it can save its energy for new problems. This is why an experienced driver can hold a conversation while driving, but a learner cannot. Our love of the familiar has a cost, however: once a habit is in place, it is very hard to change, even when we know it is bad for us.

This explains why so many New Year resolutions fail. People decide to change a lot at once — to exercise every day, stop eating sugar and learn a language — and depend on willpower alone. After a few stressful days, the old routine returns, because it is easier than the new one.

Experts who study behaviour suggest a different approach. First, make the change very small: five minutes of exercise is better than an hour you never do. Second, connect the new habit to something you already do every day, for example reading ten pages straight after dinner. Third, repeat it until it feels automatic, which can take weeks rather than days.

Interestingly, people often find it easier to change at moments when their life is already changing — when they move house, start a new job or go to university. Old routines are broken anyway, so there is space for new ones. A new start, it seems, is the best time to make a new start.

Ready for Something New?
Why do some of us love change while others avoid it — and does age make a difference?

Reading Passage 1 · Psychology · 476 words

AEvery January, millions of people promise themselves a new start: a healthier diet, a new language, a different job. By February, many of these promises have been forgotten. Change, it seems, is something we talk about far more often than we actually do. Yet the desire for something new is a basic part of being human, and it is strongest at one particular stage of life.

BStudies of personality suggest that people are most open to new experiences in their late teens and early twenties. This is the age when many young people leave home, travel, try unusual jobs or move to a city where they know nobody. Psychologists believe this makes sense: young adults are building an identity, and new experiences help them to discover what they enjoy and what they are good at. In my view, this period of exploration is one of the most valuable times in a person's life, and adults should encourage it rather than worry about it.

CAs people get older, however, their openness to novelty tends to fall gradually. The main reason is not that the brain loses its curiosity but that life becomes fuller. A person with a family, a mortgage and a steady job has less time and less freedom to experiment, and the cost of a mistake is higher. Routines also become more comfortable. When you have found a café you like or a quick route to work, there is little reason to look for another one.

DThis does not mean that older people cannot change. Many people start new careers in their forties or fifties, and some discover hobbies such as painting or running only after they retire. What seems to matter more than age is personality. Some people find routine reassuring, while others quickly become bored when every day looks the same. These differences can often be seen in early childhood: some toddlers happily play with the same toy for hours, while others want something different every few minutes.

EInterestingly, changes that we choose ourselves usually feel very different from changes that are forced on us. Moving to a new city for an exciting job can feel thrilling, while moving because a factory has closed can feel frightening, even if the two moves are similar in practical terms. People who feel in control of a change tend to cope with it far better.

FSo what is the best way to make a change? I believe that small steps are more effective than dramatic decisions. Someone who wants to get fit is more likely to succeed by walking for ten minutes a day than by joining an expensive gym and planning to go every evening. Waiting for the perfect moment is usually a mistake, because that moment rarely arrives. If there is something you want to change, the best time to begin is probably now.

Remembering Things That Never Happened

Psychology · Memory · 281 words

Most of us think of memory as a kind of video camera: something happens, the brain records it, and later we press play. Psychologists have shown that this picture is wrong. Every time we remember an event, the brain rebuilds it from small pieces, and while it rebuilds, it can add details that were never there.

A famous experiment shows how easily this happens. People are asked to listen to a list of related words, such as bed, rest, tired, dream, pillow and night. Later, they are asked which words were on the list. Many people say, very confidently, that they heard the word sleep — but it was never on the list. The brain noticed the topic of the list and filled in the most likely word.

These 'false memories' are completely normal, and most of the time they do no harm. However, they can matter a great deal. A witness to a crime may be certain about the colour of a car or the face of a stranger and still be wrong. Feeling sure about a memory, it turns out, is not the same as being right.

False memories can also cause problems in daily life. You may be sure you locked the door or took your medicine, simply because you have done it so many times before. This is why some people keep a written list or use an alarm for important tasks — they do not trust the memory alone.

Scientists are still trying to understand why some people make more of these mistakes than others, and what can help to reduce them. As the next passage shows, one surprising answer may be a good night's sleep.

Sleep on It
A study suggests that a night's sleep may help us to notice the mistakes in our memories.

Reading Passage 2 · Science · 408 words

AScientists have known for many years that sleep helps us to remember what we have learned. A study led by Kimberly Fenn, a psychologist at Michigan State University in the USA, looked at a different question: can sleep also help us to avoid remembering things wrongly? According to the researchers, it was the first study to focus on errors in memory rather than on memory in general. Its findings were published in 2009 in the journal Learning & Memory.

BFenn worked with colleagues from the University of Chicago and Washington University in St Louis. The participants in the research were college students. First, they were shown or played lists of words. Twelve hours later, they were given a test: they saw individual words and had to say which ones had been on the earlier lists. Some of the test words had not been on any list but were closely related to the words that had.

CThe students were divided into two groups. The first group learned the words at ten o'clock in the morning and took the test at ten in the evening, after a normal day without any sleep. The second group learned the words in the evening and were tested twelve hours later, the next morning, after about six hours of sleep.

DThe researchers conducted three experiments. Each time, the result was the same: the students who had slept between learning and testing made fewer false-memory mistakes, choosing fewer words that had not really been on the lists. In other words, sleep seemed to help them to pick out the parts of their memory that were incorrect.

EWhy this happens is not yet known. One possibility is that sleep strengthens the memory of the source of information — where and when we first saw or heard something. If you can remember where a piece of information came from, it is easier to decide whether it is true. Another possibility is that the students who stayed awake took in so much other information during the day that it interfered with their memory.

FThe researchers believe the work could have practical uses, from students preparing for multiple-choice tests to older people who sometimes confuse their medicines. Older adults generally have weaker memories than younger people and are more likely to have false memories, so the team hoped to study this group next. If sleep can help them to reject false information, it might improve their quality of life.
