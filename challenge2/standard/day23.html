The Choyxona: Uzbekistan's Tea-House

Culture · Uzbekistan · 300 words

Walk through almost any town or mahalla in Uzbekistan and you will find a choyxona. The word means 'tea-house', but a choyxona is much more than a place to drink tea. For centuries it has been the place where neighbours meet, share news, discuss business and celebrate family events. Traditionally, most visitors were men, although today many choyxonas also welcome families.

A classic choyxona is often built beside a canal or a small stream, in the shade of tall plane trees. Instead of sitting on chairs, guests sit on a tapchan — a wide wooden platform covered with carpets and cushions, with a low table in the middle. On hot summer afternoons, the sound of running water and the shade of the trees make it one of the coolest places in town.

Tea is served in a round teapot and drunk from small bowls called piyola. Green tea is the most common choice in many regions, although black tea is also popular. Before serving, the host often pours a bowl of tea and returns it to the pot two or three times. This helps the leaves to brew evenly.

Good manners matter. The host usually fills a guest's bowl only partly. A full bowl can suggest that the guest should drink quickly and leave, while a small amount shows that the host wants to keep refilling it — a sign of respect. Guests often take the bowl with the right hand while placing the left hand on the chest.

The choyxona is also famous for its food. Many serve plov cooked in a huge kazan, as well as bread and soup. Before a wedding, men may gather early in the morning at a choyxona to share a plov meal. In this way, the tea-house remains a centre of community life.

The Story of Tea

Reading Passage 1 · History · 643 words

AToday tea is the most popular drink in the world after water, yet for most of history it was known only in one corner of Asia. All true tea comes from a single plant, Camellia sinensis. A famous Chinese legend says that tea was discovered nearly five thousand years ago, when some leaves fell into a pot of water that was being boiled for the emperor Shennong. Nobody knows whether this story is true, but historians agree that tea was first used in China as a medicine, not as an everyday drink.

BThis changed during the Tang dynasty, which ruled China from 618 to 907. Tea became popular with ordinary people as well as with scholars and monks, and in the eighth century a writer called Lu Yu produced The Classic of Tea, the first book entirely about growing, preparing and drinking it. At this time, tea leaves were usually steamed and pressed into hard cakes or bricks. These were easy to store and carry, and pieces could be broken off and boiled when they were needed.

CBecause tea bricks travelled well, they soon became an important trade product. Buddhist monks carried tea from China to Japan, where it later became part of a famous ceremony. Along mountain paths to the west, Chinese merchants exchanged tea for horses from Tibet. Camel caravans also took tea along the Silk Road routes into Central Asia, and later across Siberia to Russia. In some places tea was so valuable that bricks of it were even used as money.

DEuropeans did not taste tea until the early seventeenth century, when Portuguese and Dutch ships began to bring it home from Asia. In Britain, tea became fashionable after 1662, when the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza married King Charles II and brought her love of tea to the royal court. For many years, however, it remained a luxury. The government placed a high tax on tea, so a great deal of it was smuggled into the country illegally. When the tax was cut sharply in 1784, tea became cheap enough for working families, and it soon became the national drink.

EThere was still one problem for the British: almost all their tea came from China, and they had to pay for it with silver. In the 1830s they began to grow tea in Assam, in north-east India, where a local variety of the plant had been found. Between 1848 and 1851 a Scottish plant collector, Robert Fortune, secretly took thousands of tea plants and seeds out of China, together with knowledge of how to process them. By the end of the nineteenth century, most of the tea drunk in Britain came from India and Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, rather than from China.

FIn Central Asia, tea arrived by land rather than by sea. Merchants brought it from China along the old caravan routes, and by the nineteenth century it had become part of daily life in cities such as Bukhara and Samarkand. The British usually added milk and sugar to strong black tea, but in many parts of Central Asia, especially in what is now Uzbekistan, people preferred green tea without milk, served in small bowls.

GAlthough there are thousands of kinds of tea, the differences between them come mainly from how the leaves are processed after picking, not from different plants. To make green tea, the fresh leaves are heated soon after they are picked, usually by steaming them or by frying them in a hot pan. This stops a natural process called oxidation, so the leaves stay green and the taste remains light. To make black tea, the leaves are first left to wither, then rolled to break them, and finally allowed to oxidise fully, which turns them dark brown before they are dried. Oolong tea is only partly oxidised, so it sits between the two.

What Happens When We Sleep?

Science · The brain · 298 words

We spend about a third of our lives asleep, but sleep is not a time when the brain simply switches off. Scientists who record brain activity at night have found that sleep has a clear structure, and that this structure repeats several times every night.

A night's sleep is made up of cycles that last roughly 90 minutes. Each cycle begins with light sleep, when we can be woken easily. Next comes deep sleep, when breathing slows down, the muscles relax and it is very hard to wake someone up. Deep sleep is thought to be important for repairing the body and helping children grow.

The last stage of each cycle is called REM sleep. REM stands for 'rapid eye movement', because the eyes move quickly from side to side under the closed eyelids. During REM sleep the brain is almost as active as it is when we are awake, but most of the body's muscles are temporarily unable to move. This is probably a form of protection: it stops us from acting out our dreams.

Most of our vivid, story-like dreams happen during REM sleep. Nobody is completely sure why we dream. Some scientists think that dreams help us deal with our emotions; others believe that they are simply a side effect of the brain's activity at night. Most dreams are forgotten within minutes of waking up, which is why many people believe that they do not dream at all.

Early in the night, the cycles contain more deep sleep; towards the morning, the REM periods become longer. This is why we often wake up in the middle of a dream. It also means that a short night does not remove all kinds of sleep equally — it takes away a large part of our REM sleep.

Why Teenagers Need More Sleep

Reading Passage 2 · Health and science · 575 words

AEvery parent of a teenager knows the scene: a son or daughter who cannot fall asleep before midnight and then cannot get out of bed in the morning. For a long time, adults explained this as laziness or bad habits. Scientists now believe that the main cause is biological. During adolescence, the body's internal clock changes, and teenagers' natural sleeping pattern moves later.

BEvery human has a 'body clock', a small group of cells in the brain that controls when we feel sleepy and when we feel awake over a cycle of about 24 hours. Light is its most important signal. When it gets dark in the evening, the brain starts to release a hormone called melatonin, which makes us feel sleepy. In the morning, daylight stops the production of melatonin, and we wake up more easily.

CResearch shows that in teenagers, melatonin is released later at night than in children or adults — often up to two hours later. This means that a fifteen-year-old who goes to bed at ten o'clock may simply lie awake, unable to sleep. The shift usually begins around puberty and is strongest in the late teens. Interestingly, it seems to reverse in the early twenties, and some scientists have even suggested using it as a biological sign that adolescence has ended.

DExperts recommend that teenagers sleep between eight and ten hours a night — more than most adults need. Surveys in many countries, however, show that a large number of teenagers sleep less than eight hours on school nights. Because they still have to get up early for school, their sleep is cut short at the end of the night. Many try to 'catch up' by sleeping late at weekends, but this can make the problem worse: the body clock moves even later, and Monday morning feels like arriving in a new time zone. Researchers call this 'social jet lag'.

ELack of sleep affects more than mood. Sleep plays an important role in memory. While we sleep, the brain replays and organises what we learned during the day, moving important information into long-term memory. Some of this work happens in the later part of the night, which is exactly the sleep that early-rising teenagers miss. Studies have found that students who are short of sleep find it harder to concentrate, remember less of what they study, and are more likely to feel anxious or low. Tiredness is also dangerous for young drivers.

FFor these reasons, many doctors argue that schools should start later. In 2014 the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended that secondary schools should not begin before 8:30 in the morning. Several schools that changed their timetables reported that students slept longer, arrived late less often and were less sleepy in class. Some also reported better grades, although the evidence on grades is less clear.

GNot everyone agrees that later start times are a good idea. Critics point out that the change is expensive and complicated. School buses may need new routes, parents may have to change their working hours, and sports and other after-school activities would finish later in the evening. Some worry that teenagers would simply go to bed even later. However, studies so far suggest that this mostly does not happen: when school starts later, most students use the extra time to sleep. Scientists also advise teenagers to help themselves by getting bright daylight in the morning and avoiding bright light late in the evening.

Caravans and Caravanserais

History · The Silk Road · 297 words

For more than a thousand years, the Silk Road linked China with Central Asia, Persia and the Mediterranean. It was not one road but a network of routes, and most goods did not travel the whole distance with one person. Instead, they were bought and sold many times along the way, passing from one merchant to the next.

Merchants usually travelled together in groups called caravans. Travelling in a group was safer, because robbers were a constant danger, and it allowed merchants to share guides and costs. A large caravan might include hundreds of animals. In the deserts and mountains of Central Asia, the most important of these was the camel, which could carry heavy loads over long distances with little water.

Caravans moved slowly, covering perhaps 30 to 40 kilometres a day. At the end of each day's journey, travellers needed somewhere safe to stop, so caravanserais were built along the main routes. A caravanserai was a kind of roadside inn: a strong building with thick walls and a single large gate, which was closed at night. Inside was an open courtyard where the animals could rest, surrounded by rooms for merchants and storerooms for goods. Many had a well or a water tank.

Caravanserais were more than hotels. Merchants exchanged goods and news there, and ideas, languages and religions travelled along with silk and spices. Rulers often paid for caravanserais to be built, because safe trade brought taxes and wealth to their cities.

Uzbekistan still has reminders of this time. The ruins of Rabati Malik, an eleventh-century caravanserai on the old road between Samarkand and Bukhara, show how grand some of these buildings were, and the covered trading domes of Bukhara remind visitors that the city was once a busy market on the caravan routes.

The Camel: Built for the Desert

Reading Passage 3 · Nature · 641 words

AFor thousands of years, people have crossed the world's driest places with the help of camels. Few large animals can survive where camels live: daytime temperatures can rise above 45°C, there may be no rain for months, and food is often scarce. The camel's success comes from many adaptations working together.

BThere are two domesticated species of camel. The dromedary, or Arabian camel, has one hump and lives mainly in the hot deserts of North Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. The Bactrian camel has two humps and comes from the cold deserts and dry grasslands of Central Asia, including Mongolia. In the past, both kinds carried goods for traders on the Silk Road. Bactrians grow a thick, woolly coat to survive winters that can fall to -30°C, and they lose it in large pieces when spring arrives. Today, dromedaries are by far the more common of the two, making up roughly nine out of every ten camels in the world.

CMany people believe that a camel's hump is full of water. In fact, it is mostly fat. When food is scarce, the camel can break down this fat for energy, and the hump becomes smaller and may fall over to one side. Storing fat in one place also has a second advantage. If the fat were spread under the skin all over the body, as it is in many other animals, it would hold heat in like a blanket. Keeping it on the back allows heat to escape more easily from the rest of the body.

DThe camel's most famous ability is to go for a long time without drinking. In cool weather, and when it can eat green plants that contain water, a camel may not need to drink for weeks. It can lose about a quarter of its body weight in water and survive, while most mammals would be in serious danger after losing much less. When it does find water, it can drink very quickly — around 100 litres in about ten minutes. Its red blood cells are oval rather than round, which helps them to keep flowing when dehydration makes the blood thick, and protects them from bursting when the animal suddenly drinks so much.

EMost mammals keep their body temperature almost constant, but a camel's temperature can change by about six degrees during a single day. It falls at night and then rises slowly during the day, and the animal only starts to sweat when its temperature becomes high. As a result, it loses far less water through sweating. Its thick coat, which may seem strange in a hot desert, actually acts as insulation and keeps out the heat of the sun.

FThe camel's body is also protected against sand and hot ground. It can close its nostrils during sandstorms, and two rows of long eyelashes, together with a thin third eyelid, keep sand out of its eyes. Its wide feet have two toes joined by a soft, leathery pad that spreads out on sand, so the camel does not sink. Thick, hard patches of skin on its chest and knees allow it to kneel and rest on burning ground. Even its nose helps: as the camel breathes out, the inside of the nose catches some of the water, so less moisture is lost.

GAlthough there are millions of domestic camels, their wild relatives are in trouble. The wild Bactrian camel, which scientists now consider a separate species from the domestic Bactrian, survives only in remote parts of the Gobi Desert in Mongolia and north-west China. Only about 1,000 are thought to remain, and the species is listed as critically endangered. The main threats include hunting, mining, competition with farm animals for water, and breeding with domestic camels. Remarkably, some wild camels are reported to drink salty water that most other animals cannot use.
