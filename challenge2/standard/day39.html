Reading Passage 1 — The Busy Life of the Honeybee

The test starts now. About 20 minutes for Questions 1–13.

The Busy Life of the Honeybee
Small insects with a big job.

Reading Passage 1 · Nature · 313 words

AA honeybee colony is like a small city. In summer, one hive can contain up to 60,000 bees, but only one of them is the queen. Her job is to lay eggs — up to 1,500 a day in the busiest season. Almost all the other bees are female workers. The male bees, called drones, do no work in the hive.

BWorker bees have many different jobs, and these change as they get older. Young workers clean the hive and feed the baby bees. Later they build wax cells and guard the entrance. In the last part of their lives, they fly out to collect nectar and pollen from flowers. In summer, a worker bee lives for only about six weeks.

CHow do bees tell each other where to find flowers? In the 1940s, the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch discovered that bees ‘dance’. A bee that has found good flowers moves in a figure-of-eight pattern. The direction of the dance shows the direction of the flowers, and the length of the dance shows how far away they are. Von Frisch received the Nobel Prize for this work in 1973.

DMaking honey is slow work. Bees turn nectar into honey by drying it and adding special substances. In her whole life, one worker bee makes only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey.

EBees are even more important for another reason: pollination. When bees move from flower to flower, they carry pollen, which helps plants to produce fruit and seeds. Many crops, such as apples, almonds and strawberries, depend on bees, and experts say that around one in every three mouthfuls of food we eat depends on pollinators.

FUnfortunately, bees are in danger. Since 2006, beekeepers in the United States and Europe have reported large losses of bees. Scientists believe there are several causes, including pesticides, disease and the loss of wild flowers.

About 20 minutes for Questions 14–26.

The Story of Chocolate
From a bitter drink to the world’s favourite sweet.

Reading Passage 2 · History · 289 words

AChocolate comes from the seeds of the cacao tree, which grows in hot, wet regions near the equator. People in Central America were using cacao more than 3,000 years ago. The ancient Maya made a drink from roasted cacao seeds, water and spices. It was bitter, not sweet, and it was often drunk at important ceremonies.

BFor the Aztecs, cacao was so valuable that its seeds were used as money. A rabbit, for example, could be bought for about ten seeds. The Aztec ruler Moctezuma was said to drink large amounts of chocolate every day.

CIn the sixteenth century, Spanish explorers brought cacao back to Europe. The Spanish added sugar to the drink, and it soon became fashionable among rich people across Europe. For many years, however, chocolate was still only a drink, and it was too expensive for ordinary people.

DThis changed in the nineteenth century. In 1828, the Dutch chemist Coenraad van Houten invented a machine that pressed out the fat from cacao, leaving a powder that was easier to use. In 1847, a British company, J. S. Fry & Sons, made the first chocolate bar, and in 1875 the Swiss chocolate maker Daniel Peter added milk to create milk chocolate.

EToday, chocolate is a huge global business. About 60% of the world’s cocoa comes from just two West African countries, Côte d’Ivoire (Ivory Coast) and Ghana. However, many cocoa farmers earn very little money, and some companies have been criticised for using children as workers on cocoa farms.

FIs chocolate good for you? Dark chocolate contains substances that may be good for the heart, but most chocolate also contains a lot of sugar and fat. Doctors therefore say that it should be enjoyed in small amounts.

About 20 minutes for Questions 27–40.

Should Children Have Smartphones?
Parents, schools and governments are all asking the same question.

Reading Passage 3 · Society · 299 words

AIn many countries, children now get their first smartphone at a young age. In the United Kingdom, according to the regulator Ofcom, around nine in ten children own a phone by the age of eleven. Many parents say they buy one so that they can contact their children and know where they are.

BHowever, more and more people are worried. In 2024, a group of British parents started a campaign called ‘Smartphone Free Childhood’, and within weeks thousands of parents had joined. They promised each other not to give their children smartphones before the age of fourteen, so that no child would feel left out.

CSchools have also taken action. Many schools now ban phones during the school day, and in 2024 the Netherlands stopped students from using phones in most classrooms. Teachers report that students concentrate better and talk to each other more at break times.

DSome researchers believe that smartphones are harming young people’s mental health. The American psychologist Jonathan Haidt argues that a rise in anxiety among teenagers in the 2010s happened at the same time as smartphones became common. Other researchers, however, say that the evidence is not clear, and that other causes may be more important.

EThere are also benefits. Smartphones can help children to learn, to stay in touch with friends and family, and to feel safe when they travel alone. For children who live far away from relatives, video calls can be very important.

FIn my view, the answer is not to ban phones completely but to delay them. A young child can have a simple phone for calls and messages, and a smartphone can come later, with clear family rules about when and where it can be used. What matters most is not the phone itself, but how it is used.

AFor most of the past century, the standard working week in many countries has been five days. In recent years, however, a growing number of employers have experimented with a four-day week, in which employees work fewer hours for the same pay.

Their working week was reduced from 40 hours to 35 or 36 hours without any loss of pay. Researchers reported that productivity stayed the same or improved in most workplaces, while workers’ wellbeing increased considerably.

Some also warn that squeezing five days of work into four could make each day more intense. Nevertheless, supporters argue that the trials show a long-held assumption — that more hours mean more output — deserves to be questioned.

AIn warehouses in cities from Singapore to New Jersey, lettuces, herbs and strawberries grow in stacked trays under pink and blue LED lights. BSupporters list several advantages. Because conditions are controlled, crops can be grown all year round, without pesticides, and close to the people who will eat them, which reduces transport. Vertical farms can also use up to 95% less water than traditional fields, since water is recycled.

CThe main problem is energy. Replacing the sun with electric lights is expensive, and after energy prices rose sharply in 2022, several well-funded vertical farming companies in Europe and the United States went out of business. For this reason, most vertical farms grow high-value, fast-growing crops such as salad leaves and herbs, rather than staple foods such as wheat or rice, which need large areas and would be far too costly to grow under lights.

DMost experts therefore see vertical farming as a useful addition to traditional agriculture rather than a replacement for it — particularly in crowded cities and in countries with little farmland or water.

A Short History of the Umbrella

Speed reading · 280 words · 195 words

AUmbrellas were first used not against rain but against the sun. More than three thousand years ago, sunshades appear in art from ancient Egypt, Assyria and China, where they were often a sign of rank: only kings and nobles were shaded by them. In China, oiled paper was used to make shades that could also keep off rain, and Chinese craftsmen were also among the first to make umbrellas that could fold.

BIn Europe, umbrellas were long seen as something for women. When the traveller Jonas Hanway began carrying one in London in the 1750s, he was laughed at in the street, and coach drivers, who earned money from people trying to stay dry, shouted insults at him. Within thirty years, however, umbrellas had become common among men too, and some were still called ‘Hanways’.

CEarly umbrellas were heavy, with frames of wood or whalebone. In 1852 the English manufacturer Samuel Fox introduced a frame with steel ribs, which was lighter and stronger, and the basic design has changed little since. The folding pocket umbrella followed in the twentieth century. Today, around one billion umbrellas are sold every year, and most are made in China.

Nowadays, many children get a smartphone at a very young age. Some people believe that children under fourteen should not have one. I partly agree with this view: young children should have a phone, but not a smartphone.

On the one hand, there are good reasons to keep smartphones away from young children. Firstly, smartphones can be very addictive. Many children spend hours on games and social media instead of doing homework, playing sports or sleeping. Secondly, smartphones can make it difficult to concentrate at school. For example, teachers in schools that ban phones report that students focus better in lessons and talk to each other more at break times.

On the other hand, children do need a way to contact their parents. Many children travel to school alone, and parents want to know that they are safe. However, a simple phone that can only make calls and send messages is enough for this. It gives parents and children the safety they need without the problems of the internet and social media.

In addition, when children are older, they can learn to use a smartphone responsibly. Parents can set clear rules, for example no phones at the dinner table or in the bedroom at night.

In conclusion, I agree that children under fourteen should not have smartphones, but I believe they should have a simple phone for safety. When they are older, they can have a smartphone with clear family rules.
