Why Do We Laugh?
Laughter has surprisingly little to do with jokes.

Reading Passage 1 · Psychology · 328 words

ALaughter is one of the most common human sounds. Most people laugh many times a day, and babies begin to laugh at around four months old, long before they can speak. But scientists have discovered that laughter has surprisingly little to do with jokes.

BIn the 1990s, the American psychologist Robert Provine and his students listened to more than 1,200 conversations in public places, such as shopping centres and university campuses, and noted every time someone laughed. They found that only about one in five laughs came after anything that could be called a joke. Most laughter followed ordinary comments such as ‘See you later’ or ‘I know’.

CProvine also found that speakers laughed more than their listeners — about 46 per cent more — and that people were around thirty times more likely to laugh when they were with others than when they were alone. These results suggest that laughter is mainly a social signal: a way of showing friendliness and agreement.

DHumans are not the only animals that laugh. Chimpanzees, gorillas and other apes make breathy, panting sounds when they play and tickle each other. Scientists believe that human laughter developed from this kind of ‘play panting’ millions of years ago. Even rats make high-pitched sounds when they are tickled, although humans cannot hear them without special equipment.

ELaughter may also be good for health. When we laugh, the body releases chemicals called endorphins, which can reduce pain and make us feel relaxed. In one study at the University of Oxford, people who had watched a comedy video together could hold their arm in an uncomfortable position for longer than those who had watched a boring documentary.

FLaughter is also contagious. Hearing other people laugh makes us more likely to laugh ourselves, which is why some television comedies add recorded laughter. Interestingly, people can usually tell the difference between real laughter and laughter that is put on, even when they hear it only for a second.

Cities of the Silk Road
For over 1,500 years, trade routes linked China and the Mediterranean — and Central Asia was at their heart.

Reading Passage 2 · History · 261 words

AFor more than 1,500 years, a network of trade routes known as the Silk Road connected China with the Mediterranean. The name was invented only in 1877 by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen; the merchants who used the routes never called them by a single name.

BSilk was only one of many goods carried along the routes. Merchants also traded spices, paper, glass, horses, gold and precious stones. Few traders travelled the whole distance; instead, goods passed from one merchant to another, changing hands many times between China and Europe.

CSome of the greatest cities of the routes grew up in Central Asia. Samarkand, in present-day Uzbekistan, was famous for its paper, which was considered among the best in the world, while Bukhara became a major centre of learning, with hundreds of schools and libraries.

DThe routes carried ideas as well as goods. Religions such as Buddhism and Islam spread along them, together with technologies including papermaking, printing and gunpowder. Scientists, poets and doctors travelled between cities, sharing knowledge.

ETravel was slow and dangerous. Caravans of camels could take months to cross deserts and mountains, and travellers faced extreme heat and cold, as well as attacks by robbers. Along the way, caravanserais — roadside inns with space for animals and goods — offered a safe place to rest.

FFrom the fifteenth century, the land routes became less important as European ships began to sail directly to Asia. Today, however, the Silk Road is enjoying new attention, as historic cities such as Samarkand and Bukhara attract tourists from around the world.

Should Homework Be Banned?
Is homework useful practice or an unnecessary burden?

Reading Passage 3 · Education · 236 words

AHomework is a familiar part of school life in most countries, but its value has long been debated. In some places, pupils spend more than ten hours a week on homework; in others, especially in primary schools, homework has been reduced or abolished.

BResearch suggests that the effect of homework depends greatly on age. A well-known review by the American researcher Harris Cooper found that homework had a clear positive link with achievement for older secondary students, but only a very small effect for primary school children.

CSupporters argue that homework helps pupils to practise skills, prepares them for independent study and keeps parents informed about what their children are learning. Some teachers also say that without homework there is not enough time to cover the curriculum.

DCritics point out that too much homework can cause stress, reduce sleep and leave little time for play, sport and family life. Homework may also increase inequality, because some children have a quiet place to work and parents who can help, while others do not.

EIn my view, homework should not be banned, but it should be used more carefully. Young children benefit more from reading for pleasure than from worksheets, and for older students, a small amount of well-designed homework is more useful than long hours of repetitive exercises. Schools should also provide places where pupils can study after lessons, so that every child has the same opportunity.

Sleeping in Space

Extra reading 1 · Science · 195 words

AAstronauts on the International Space Station see around sixteen sunrises and sunsets every day, because the station travels around the Earth every ninety minutes. This makes it difficult for the body to know when it is time to sleep.

BWithout gravity, there is no ‘up’ or ‘down’, so astronauts do not lie in beds. Instead, they sleep in small cabins, inside sleeping bags fixed to the wall so that they do not float around and hit something. Many wear eye masks to block out light and earplugs, because the station is noisy, with fans running all the time.

CStudies show that astronauts often sleep only about six hours a night, less than the eight and a half hours planned in their schedules. To help, the station has special lights that can change colour: blue-white light in the morning helps astronauts to wake up, while warmer, orange light in the evening helps them to feel sleepy.

DSome astronauts say that sleeping in space is very comfortable, because there is no pressure on the body. Others find that their arms float up in front of them while they sleep, so they put them inside the sleeping bag.

The Story of the Pencil

Speed reading · 280 words · 186 words

AFor most of history, people wrote with brushes, quills or pieces of charcoal. The modern pencil became possible after 1564, when a large deposit of unusually pure graphite was discovered in Borrowdale, in the north of England. Local shepherds first used it to mark their sheep. Because graphite is soft and breaks easily, people wrapped it in string or sheepskin, and later in wood.

BThe Borrowdale mine was so valuable that it was guarded, and graphite was sometimes smuggled. When supplies ran short in France during wars with Britain, the French engineer Nicolas-Jacques Conté found a solution in 1795: he mixed powdered graphite with clay and baked it. By changing the amount of clay, he could make pencils harder or softer — the basis of the grading system still used today.

CIn the nineteenth century, pencil factories grew in Germany and the United States. The famous yellow colour was introduced in the 1890s, when some manufacturers painted their best pencils yellow to suggest that the graphite came from China, where the colour was associated with royalty. Today, more than fourteen billion pencils are produced every year.

In many countries, young children are given homework almost every day. Some people believe that it should be banned in primary schools. I partly agree with this view: long homework tasks should be stopped, but a small amount of reading at home is still useful.

There are good reasons to stop giving young children traditional homework. First, research has shown that homework has only a very small effect on the achievement of primary school pupils. Second, too much homework can make children stressed and tired, and it leaves them less time for play, sport and family life, which are also important for their development. Finally, homework can be unfair, because some children have parents who can help them and a quiet place to work, while others do not.

However, I do not think that all homework should disappear. Reading for pleasure at home is one of the best ways for young children to improve their language and imagination, and it only takes fifteen or twenty minutes a day. In addition, simple tasks such as talking to parents about what they learnt at school help families to understand what their children are studying.

In conclusion, I agree that worksheets and long exercises should be banned in primary schools, because they bring few benefits and can cause stress. Instead, schools should encourage children to read at home every day, which is more enjoyable and more useful.
