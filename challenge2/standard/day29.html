Reading Passage 1 — How Children Learn to Read

Question types: TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN, sentence completion and multiple choice. Suggested time: 15 minutes. Passage on the left, questions on the right. Answers are checked when you press Next.

How Children Learn to Read

Reading Passage 1 · Education · 339 words

ASpeaking is natural for human beings. Almost every child in the world learns to speak without formal lessons, simply by listening to the people around them. Reading is different. Writing was invented only around five thousand years ago — a very short time in human history — and so the brain has no special area that is designed for reading. Instead, every child who learns to read must train parts of the brain that were originally used for other jobs, such as recognising faces and objects.

BThe first step is understanding that written marks stand for sounds. In languages that use an alphabet, children must learn that each letter, or group of letters, represents a sound, and that these sounds can be joined together to make words. Many teachers call this ‘phonics’. Research suggests that children who are taught phonics clearly and regularly usually learn to read more quickly than those who are expected to recognise whole words by sight.

CHowever, reading is not only about turning letters into sounds. Children also need a large vocabulary, because it is easier to read a word you already know when you hear it. This is one reason why reading aloud to young children is so valuable. Children who hear stories every day at home often start school knowing many more words than children who do not, and this difference can grow larger over time.

DThe age at which children start formal reading lessons varies from country to country. In England, most children begin at about four or five years old, while in Finland formal reading lessons have traditionally started at seven. Interestingly, Finnish teenagers have often done very well in international reading tests, which suggests that an early start is not the only thing that matters.

EFinally, motivation plays a large part. Children who enjoy reading read more, and children who read more become better readers. Experts therefore advise parents to let children choose their own books, including comics and magazines, and to make reading a pleasure rather than a test.

Reading Passage 2 — Is Listening the Same as Reading?

Question types: matching headings, summary completion with a word bank and multiple choice. Suggested time: 12 minutes.

Is Listening the Same as Reading?

Reading Passage 2 · Literacy · 265 words

AAudiobooks are more popular than ever. People listen to them while driving, cooking or exercising, and many say that audiobooks have allowed them to ‘read’ far more books than before. But is listening to a book really the same as reading it? The question causes surprisingly strong arguments.

BSome research suggests that, for many simple texts, people understand and remember about the same amount whether they read or listen. This is not very surprising. Long before writing existed, people passed on stories, history and knowledge by speaking, and the brain processes the meaning of language in similar ways whatever its source.

CHowever, there are important differences. When you read, you control the speed: you can slow down at a difficult sentence, stop to think, or go back to check something. With an audiobook, the speaker sets the pace, and going back is less convenient. For this reason, many teachers believe that printed text is better for difficult material, such as a textbook you will be tested on.

DListening also has advantages of its own. A good narrator can bring a story to life, showing emotion and humour through the voice. For people with sight problems or reading difficulties, audiobooks make literature available in a way that printed books cannot. And for language learners, listening to a book while following the printed text can improve both pronunciation and reading speed.

EPerhaps the best answer is that reading and listening are different tools for different purposes. A thriller on a long journey may be perfect as an audiobook; a chapter of a science textbook probably is not.

Speaking Part 2 — A Book or Article You Enjoyed

The Part 2 task from Complete IELTS Unit 7. Make brief notes for each part of the card and note down useful vocabulary, then speak for two minutes.

Part 2 task card

Describe a book or article that you enjoyed reading for your studies.

You should say:
  what the book or article was about
  why you read it
  how long it took you to read
and explain why you enjoyed reading it.

Model answer

I’d like to talk about an article I read last year for my biology class. It was about how bees communicate with each other — they actually do a kind of dance to show other bees where to find flowers.

I read it because our teacher asked us to choose an article and give a short presentation about it. At first I chose it just because it was short, to be honest! But it only took me about twenty minutes to read the first time, and then I read it again more carefully, making notes, which took another hour or so.

The main reason I enjoyed it was that it was full of surprising facts. I had no idea that insects could share such detailed information. The writer also explained everything very clearly, with diagrams, so even the scientific parts were easy to follow. And when I gave my presentation, my classmates asked lots of questions, which made me feel like an expert. Since then, I’ve started reading more science articles in my free time.

2. What is the MAIN reason she enjoyed it?
