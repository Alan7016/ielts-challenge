Doctors are increasingly worried about the emissions produced by heavy traffic in big cities. Each car adds its own exhaust fumes to the air, and over time this builds up into dangerous levels of particulate matter and other pollutant gases. During gridlock, when hundreds of vehicles sit idling at once, the problem gets even worse, since engines keep running without the car actually moving anywhere. People who inhale this polluted air every day are more likely to develop respiratory problems, including asthma. Some cities have tried building a bypass around the centre to keep heavy traffic out, but as urban sprawl continues to push the suburbs further out, more people end up commuting long distances by car anyway.

Task 2 of 6

Article — How Traffic Is Polluting Our Cities

Read the article below, then answer the questions that follow.

Every day, millions of cars, buses, and trucks fill the streets of cities around the world. Along with the noise and the traffic jams, these vehicles produce something we cannot always see: air pollution. Traffic is now one of the biggest causes of poor air quality in cities, and doctors are becoming increasingly concerned about the effect this has on people's health.

Most vehicles run on petrol or diesel, and burning these fuels releases harmful gases and particulate matter into the air. These emissions are especially high in gridlock, when cars sit idling in traffic for long periods with their engines still running. As a result, busy roads and city centres often have the worst air quality, since this is where traffic is heaviest.

Because people breathe in this polluted air every day, doctors have linked traffic pollution to a range of respiratory problems. Children, in particular, are at risk: their lungs are still developing, so long-term exposure to dirty air can lead to conditions like asthma. Studies have also shown that people who live or work close to busy roads are more likely to inhale higher levels of harmful pollutants than those who live further away.

Some cities have started to take action. In several parts of Europe and Asia, low-emission zones have been introduced, meaning older, more polluting vehicles are charged extra — or banned altogether — from entering the city centre. Other cities have built a bypass around the centre so that through-traffic no longer needs to drive past homes, schools, and hospitals. Due to growing urban sprawl, however, more people are moving to the suburbs and commuting further by car each day, which makes the problem harder to solve.

Experts agree that reducing traffic pollution will not be easy, since it means persuading people to leave their cars at home. This could mean investing more in public transport, cycling lanes, and electric vehicles. Until then, for many city residents, breathing clean air on their daily commute remains only a distant hope.

5. Urban sprawl is making the traffic pollution problem easier to solve.

Traffic jams — no end in sight

There are no easy answers to the problems of traffic congestion.

A  Traffic congestion affects people throughout the world. Traffic jams cause smog in dozens of cities across both the developed and developing world. In the US, commuters spend an average of a full working week each year sitting in traffic jams, according to the Texas Transportation Institute. While alternative ways of getting around are available, most people still choose their cars because they are looking for convenience, comfort and privacy.

B  The most promising technique for reducing city traffic congestion is called congestion pricing, whereby cities charge a toll to enter certain parts of town at certain times of day. In theory, if the toll is high enough, some drivers will cancel their trips or go by bus or train. And in practice it seems to work: Singapore, London and Stockholm have reduced traffic and pollution in city centres thanks to congestion pricing.

C  Another way to reduce rush-hour traffic is for employers to implement flextime, which lets employees travel to and from work at off-peak times to avoid the rush hour. Those who have to travel during busy times can do their part by ride sharing. Employers can also allow more staff to telecommute (work from home) so as to keep more cars off the road altogether.

D  Some urban planners still believe that the best way to ease traffic congestion is to build more roads, especially roads that can take drivers over or around crowded city streets. But such techniques do not really keep cars off the road; they only accommodate more of them.

E  Other, more forward-thinking planners know that more and more drivers and cars are taking to the roads every day, and they are unwilling to encourage more private automobiles, both for people and the environment. For this reason, the American government has decided to spend $7 billion on helping to increase capacity on public-transport systems and upgrade them with more efficient technologies. But environmentalists complain that such funding is tiny compared to the $50 billion being spent on roads and bridges.

Adapted from The Environmental Magazine, EarthTalk.

Vocabulary — Make and Cause

IELTS candidates often confuse make and cause.

Remember:

cause — to make something happen: The hurricane caused widespread damage.

make — to cause someone/something to become happy, sad, difficult, etc.: You've made me very happy.

cause is followed by a noun/adjective + noun. make is followed by a noun/pronoun + adjective.

One More Reading — Should City Centres Ban Cars?

Read the passage, then answer the questions below.

In a growing number of cities, from Oslo to Madrid, local governments have started banning private cars from parts of the city centre altogether. Streets that were once packed with traffic are being turned over to pedestrians, cyclists, and outdoor cafés instead.

Supporters argue that the change makes cities more pleasant to live in: less noise, cleaner air, and more space for people rather than vehicles. Local businesses have sometimes worried that fewer cars would mean fewer customers, but several studies have found that pedestrian-only streets can actually increase the amount shoppers spend, since people linger longer when they aren't dodging traffic.

Not everyone is convinced, however. Critics point out that banning cars can be especially hard on older residents, people with disabilities, and delivery drivers, all of whom may depend on vehicle access. Success also seems to depend on whether a city has good public transport to offer as an alternative — without it, a car ban simply pushes traffic, and pollution, onto the surrounding streets instead of solving the problem.

1. Some cities have banned private cars from parts of the city centre.

2. Studies have found that pedestrian-only streets always reduce how much shoppers spend.

3. Older residents and people with disabilities are never affected by car bans.

4. Good public transport can help a car ban succeed.

5. Every city that has tried a car ban has been equally successful.
