The Story of Chocolate
From a bitter drink to a treat for everyone.

Reading Passage 1 · Food and history · 358 words

AChocolate is made from the seeds of the cacao tree, which grows in hot, wet regions near the equator. The seeds, often called beans, grow inside large pods that hang directly from the trunk and branches of the tree. Each pod contains between 30 and 50 beans.

BPeople in Central America were using cacao more than 3,000 years ago. The Maya and later the Aztecs made a bitter drink from roasted beans mixed with water, chilli and spices. The drink was not sweet at all, and it was usually served cold. Cacao beans were so valuable that the Aztecs used them as money: a rabbit, for example, could be bought for about ten beans.

CSpanish explorers brought cacao to Europe in the sixteenth century. At first, Europeans did not like the bitter taste, but when sugar and cinnamon were added, the hot drink became fashionable among rich families. For more than two hundred years, chocolate was a luxury that only wealthy people could afford.

DEverything changed in the nineteenth century. In 1828, a Dutch chemist, Coenraad van Houten, invented a machine that removed some of the fat from the beans, making a powder that was easier to mix with water. In 1847, a British company made the first chocolate bar, and in 1875 Swiss producers added milk to create milk chocolate. Factories could now produce chocolate cheaply, and it became a treat for everyone.

EToday, most of the world’s cacao is grown in West Africa, especially in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, which together produce more than half of the global supply. Most of it is grown on small family farms. However, many farmers earn very little, and some experts are worried that young people no longer want to work on cacao farms.

FThere are also environmental concerns. Cacao trees need shade, but in some areas forests have been cut down to make room for new farms. Climate change may also make some growing regions too hot and dry in the future. Some chocolate companies are now paying farmers more and helping them to plant trees, but many people believe that the price of chocolate will have to rise.

Living in a Tiny House
Why are thousands of people choosing to live in very small homes?

Reading Passage 2 · Homes and lifestyles · 289 words

AImagine a home that is smaller than a parking space for two cars. That is the size of a typical ‘tiny house’ — usually between 10 and 40 square metres. In the last fifteen years, thousands of people in the United States, Europe and Australia have chosen to live in homes like these.

BMany tiny houses are built on wheels, so their owners can move them from place to place. Others are built on small pieces of land, often in the countryside. Inside, every centimetre is used carefully: beds are placed above the kitchen, stairs contain drawers, and tables fold away into the wall.

CThe main reason people choose tiny houses is money. In many cities, a normal house costs ten or twenty times a person’s yearly salary. A tiny house, by contrast, can be built for between 30,000 and 80,000 dollars. Many owners say they no longer need a large loan from a bank, and so they can work fewer hours.

DOthers are attracted by the idea of a simpler life. Because there is little space, owners have to give away most of their possessions. Many say that this makes them happier, as they spend less time cleaning and shopping and more time outdoors or with friends.

ETiny houses are also better for the environment. They need much less energy for heating and lighting, and some owners use solar panels and collect rainwater, so they do not need to connect to public services at all.

FHowever, tiny homes are not for everyone. Families with children often find them too small, and in some countries it is difficult to get permission to live in one all year round. Some owners also discover that they miss having guests to stay.

Robots That Deliver
Small robots are carrying food along city pavements. Are they here to stay?

Reading Passage 3 · Technology · 283 words

AOn the streets of some cities, small six-wheeled robots now carry food and parcels to people’s homes. About the size of a supermarket trolley, they travel along pavements at walking speed, stopping at traffic lights and crossing roads carefully. Customers order using an app and receive a message when the robot arrives; then they open the lid with a code.

BThe robots were first tested in Estonia in 2015 by a company called Starship Technologies. Since then, they have made millions of deliveries in the United States, Britain and other countries, many of them on university campuses, where students order coffee and snacks between classes.

CCompanies say the robots have several advantages. They are cheap to run, they do not need breaks, and because they are electric they produce no exhaust fumes. Each robot is watched by a human operator, who can take control if it gets stuck, but one operator can look after several robots at the same time.

DThere are also problems. Robots can only carry small loads, and they find snow, steps and very busy pavements difficult. Some pedestrians complain that the robots get in their way, and there have been cases of people kicking them or trying to steal the food inside. In a few cities, the authorities have limited the number of robots allowed on the streets.

EIn my opinion, delivery robots will not replace human delivery drivers, at least not soon. They are useful for short trips in quiet areas, such as campuses or new housing estates. But for long distances or heavy goods, people and vans will still be needed for many years. Cities should make clear rules now, before the pavements become crowded with machines.

Learning to Swim as an Adult

Extra reading 1 · Health · 214 words

AAround the world, millions of adults cannot swim. In some countries, more than half of the adult population has never learnt. Many of them feel embarrassed, and some avoid holidays near the sea or trips with friends because of it.

BSwimming teachers say that adults are often more afraid of the water than children are. Children usually learn through play, but adults tend to think about the dangers, and fear makes their bodies tense. A tense body sinks more easily, which makes the fear even stronger.

CFor this reason, most adult courses begin with breathing. Learners practise breathing slowly and calmly, and they learn to float in shallow water where they can always stand up. Only when they feel relaxed do they start learning strokes such as front crawl or breaststroke.

DProgress can be surprisingly fast. Many adults can swim a short distance after about ten lessons, although becoming a confident swimmer takes longer. Teachers recommend practising at least twice a week, because skills are quickly forgotten after long breaks.

ELearning to swim is not only about safety. Swimming is excellent exercise for people of all ages, and it puts very little pressure on the joints. Many adult learners also say that overcoming their fear gave them more confidence in other areas of life.

Nowadays, many young people leave home without knowing how to prepare a simple meal. For this reason, some people believe that cooking should be taught to all children at school. I strongly agree with this view.

The main reason is that cooking helps children to eat more healthily. When children prepare food themselves, they learn which ingredients are fresh and nutritious and which are full of sugar and fat. They are also more likely to try new things: for example, a child who has cooked vegetable soup is much more likely to eat it. In this way, cooking lessons could help to reduce problems such as childhood obesity.

Another benefit is that cooking makes young people more independent. Students who go to university or start work in another city need to feed themselves every day. If they can cook, they will spend less money on fast food and take better care of their health. Cooking also teaches useful skills such as planning, measuring and working safely.

Of course, some people argue that schools already have too little time for important subjects such as mathematics and languages, and that kitchens are expensive to build. However, cooking lessons do not need to take many hours. A short course once a week, perhaps for one term, would be enough to teach the basics.

In conclusion, I believe that every child should learn to cook at school, because it is a skill that improves their health and independence for the rest of their lives.
