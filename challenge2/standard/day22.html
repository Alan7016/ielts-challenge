The Real Price of a Cheap T-shirt

Society · Environment · 566 words

ATwenty years ago, most families bought new clothes a few times a year: a coat for winter, shoes for the new school year, perhaps something special for a wedding. Today, many shoppers buy something new almost every week. According to a 2016 report by the consultancy McKinsey, the amount of clothing produced in the world roughly doubled between 2000 and 2014, while the number of times each garment was worn fell sharply. Clothes, in other words, have become almost disposable.

BThe main reason is price. Large fashion chains have learned to design, produce and deliver new styles in a few weeks instead of several months, and they sell them at prices that would have seemed impossible a generation ago. A T-shirt can cost less than a sandwich. Because the items are so cheap, buying them feels like a small decision, and shops encourage impulse buying by changing their collections constantly. A customer who sees something she likes knows that it may not be there next week.

CSocial media has made the cycle faster. Many young people say they do not want to be photographed in the same outfit twice, and online influencers are often paid to show a different look every day. Shopping apps add to the pressure: they send messages about sales and "last chance" offers, and a new top is only a few taps away. For some consumers, buying clothes has become a hobby rather than a response to a real need.

DThe low prices, however, hide real costs. Most cheap clothes are made in countries such as Bangladesh, Vietnam and Cambodia, where many garment workers earn very low wages and work long hours. In 2013, the Rana Plaza building near Dhaka, which contained several clothing factories, collapsed and killed more than 1,100 people. Cracks had appeared in the walls the day before, but workers were told to return to their sewing machines.

EThe environment pays a price too. Growing cotton needs large amounts of water, and the chemicals used to dye cloth often end up in rivers. Many cheap garments are made from polyester and other synthetic materials, which are produced from oil and can take hundreds of years to break down. Every time these clothes are washed, tiny plastic fibres are released into the water and eventually reach the sea.

FThen there is the question of what happens to clothes when we no longer want them. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation, a British charity, has calculated that the equivalent of one rubbish truck full of textiles is buried in landfill or burned every second, and that less than one per cent of the material used to make clothing is recycled into new clothing. Some unwanted clothes are sent abroad: in the Atacama Desert in Chile, huge piles of second-hand garments from Europe, the USA and Asia have been dumped in the sand.

GSolutions exist, but none of them is simple. Some governments are discussing laws that would make manufacturers responsible for collecting and recycling the clothes they sell. Several brands now offer repair services or take back old items. Second-hand shopping, both in charity shops and on apps, has become fashionable among young people, and campaigns encourage consumers to ask one simple question before they buy: "Will I wear this at least thirty times?" Experts agree, however, that the most effective solution is also the least popular one: buying fewer, more durable clothes.

How to organise this essay

  • Introduction: paraphrase the question + say what the essay will do.

  • Conclusion: 1–2 sentences; no new ideas.

These days, a large number of people buy low-priced clothing, wear it only a handful of times and then throw it away. This essay will explain the main reasons for this trend and suggest some ways to deal with it.

There are two main causes of this problem. The first is price. Fashion chains now produce new styles so quickly and so cheaply that a T-shirt can cost less than a sandwich. As a result, buying clothes has become a small decision, and many consumers buy on impulse rather than because they need something. The second cause is social media. Many young people do not want to be photographed in the same outfit twice, and influencers show a different look every day. This creates pressure to buy constantly, so clothes become almost disposable.

However, there are practical ways to tackle this issue. Firstly, governments should take action by making manufacturers responsible for collecting and recycling the clothes they sell. If companies had to pay for this, they would have a reason to make more durable garments. Secondly, schools and public campaigns could raise awareness of the hidden costs of fast fashion, such as water pollution and the low wages of garment workers. Encouraging people to buy second-hand or to repair their clothes would also reduce the amount of textiles that end up in landfill.

In conclusion, low prices and social media are the main reasons why people treat clothes as disposable. Stricter rules for manufacturers and better education for consumers could help to make fashion less wasteful.
