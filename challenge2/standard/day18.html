Article — How Much Screen Time Is Too Much?

Read this short article. It will give you ideas for your essay later today.

How Much Screen Time Is Too Much?

Family & health · 271 words

Ten years ago, most children got their first mobile phone at around the age of 13. Today, the average age in many countries is just 10, and some children have a tablet before they can read. So how much time on screens is safe?

Doctors in many countries give similar advice. Children under two should have almost no screen time at all. Children between two and five should have no more than one hour a day. For older children, there is no exact number, but experts say that screens should not take the place of sleep, homework, physical activity or time with family.

Why are doctors worried? First, screens can affect sleep. The blue light from phones tells the brain that it is still daytime, so children who use phones in bed often fall asleep later. Second, time on screens is time that children are not moving. Third, many apps are designed to be addictive. They give small rewards, like "likes" and points, so that users keep coming back.

However, screens are not all bad. Many children use them to learn. A 12-year-old in Tashkent can watch a science lesson from a teacher in London, or practise English with a free app. Video calls also help families stay close when parents work far away. For some shy children, online games are a safe way to make friends.

So what can parents do? Experts give three simple tips. First, keep phones out of bedrooms at night. Second, have "screen-free" times, for example during meals. Third, parents should be a good example: if adults are always on their phones, children will copy them.

This is an IELTS-style reading passage. Read it carefully — it should take you about 20 minutes — then answer questions 1–14.

Phones Off: One School's Experiment

Education · 373 words

AIn September 2023, Greenhill Secondary School, a large school in the north of England, introduced a new rule: students were no longer allowed to use their phones during the school day. Phones had to be switched off and kept in a locked bag from 8.30 a.m. until 3.30 p.m. Many students were angry, and some parents worried that they would not be able to contact their children in an emergency.

BThe head teacher, Mrs Sarah Collins, had several reasons for the decision. Teachers had complained that students were checking their phones in lessons, and that videos of fights were being filmed and shared online. "We also noticed that the playground had become very quiet," she said. "Students were sitting next to each other, but they were all looking at screens instead of talking."

CTo answer parents' worries, the school made a simple promise: in an emergency, parents could call the school office, and a message would reach their child within five minutes. In the first year, the office received an average of just two such calls a week.

DAfter one school year, the school looked at the results. The number of students sent out of lessons for bad behaviour fell by 30 per cent. Reports of cyberbullying dropped by almost half. Teachers also said that students concentrated better, especially in the first lesson after lunch. Exam results improved slightly, although Mrs Collins admits that other changes at the school may also have helped.

EPerhaps the biggest change was in the playground. Lunchtime clubs for chess, football and music became much more popular, and the school library had twice as many visitors. "At first I hated the rule," said Daniel, aged 14. "But now I actually talk to people at lunch. I've made friends I never knew before."

FNot everyone agrees with the ban. Some students say they need their phones to check the timetable or to use educational apps. Others argue that schools should teach young people how to use phones sensibly, not simply take them away. "When they leave school, nobody will lock their phones in a bag," said one parent. "They need to learn self-control." Greenhill is now considering allowing phones in some lessons, where teachers can use them for learning.

Writing Task 2 — What Is It?

Now the main focus of today: Writing Task 2. Read the information, then practise recognising the different types of question.

📘 Writing Task 2 — the basics

  • You write an essay of at least 250 words in about 40 minutes.
  • Task 2 is worth twice as much as Task 1 — so it is very important.
  • You must answer the exact question, give your ideas, explain them and give examples.
  • Write in a formal style: no don't, can't, kids, stuff, a lot of.
  • A clear structure: introduction → 2 body paragraphs → conclusion.

The five common question types

typethe question ends with…
A OpinionTo what extent do you agree or disagree?
B DiscussionDiscuss both views and give your own opinion.
C Advantages / disadvantagesWhat are the advantages and disadvantages of …?
D Problem / solutionWhat problems does this cause? How can they be solved?
E Two-part questionTwo different direct questions, e.g. Why …? Is this a positive development?

Grammar — Giving Opinions and Advice

In Task 2 you often say what should happen and what will happen. You need modal verbs and the first conditional.

Key grammar

useexample
should / shouldn't — advice, opinionParents should limit screen time.
must — something very importantSchools must protect children from cyberbullying.
can — possibility, abilityPhones can help children learn.
might / may — maybe, not sureChildren might become addicted.
If + present simple, will + verbIf children use phones in bed, they will sleep badly.

After a modal, use the base verb: should to limit → should limit · can helps → can help.

Writing Task 2

Some people think that children should not be allowed to have their own smartphone until they are 16 years old. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Write at least 250 words.

In recent years, children have been getting their first smartphones at a younger and younger age. Some people believe that young people should not be permitted to own a mobile phone before the age of sixteen. Although smartphones have some benefits, I largely agree with this idea.

Firstly, smartphones can stop children from concentrating at school and sleeping well. Many apps and games are designed to be addictive, so children often check their phones in lessons instead of listening to the teacher. Moreover, the blue light from screens makes it harder to fall asleep, and children who use their phones in bed are often tired the next day. For example, when one school in England banned phones, teachers said that students concentrated much better.

Secondly, young children are not ready to deal with the dangers of the internet. They may see harmful websites or become victims of cyberbullying, and they often do not tell their parents. At the same school, reports of cyberbullying fell by almost half after the ban. A sixteen-year-old is usually more responsible and better able to protect himself or herself online.

Of course, some people argue that phones help parents contact their children in an emergency. However, a simple phone without internet access can do this job just as well.

In conclusion, I believe that children should wait until they are sixteen to have their own smartphone, because phones can harm their studies and their safety. Until then, parents should give them a basic phone for calls. (about 270 words)
