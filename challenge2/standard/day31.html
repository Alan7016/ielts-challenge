Back on Two Wheels
Many cities once gave their streets to cars. Now the bicycle is returning.

Reading Passage 1 · Cities and transport · 434 words

AIn the 1950s, the bicycle was one of the most common ways of getting to work in many European cities. In Amsterdam, for example, around 80% of journeys to work were made by bike. But as cars became cheaper, cities changed. New roads were built, car parks replaced parks and squares, and cycling began to look old-fashioned and dangerous. By the 1970s, the number of cyclists in many cities had fallen sharply.

BAmsterdam was one of the first cities to change direction. In the early 1970s, many children were killed in road accidents, and parents organised protests with the slogan ‘Stop the child murder’. At the same time, an oil crisis made petrol very expensive. The city government listened, and slowly began to build separate cycle lanes and to make some streets free of cars.

CToday, cities all over the world are trying to follow this example. Paris has built hundreds of kilometres of new cycle lanes since 2015, and while the city was building them, the number of cyclists on some main roads more than doubled. In Bogotá, Colombia, more than 100 kilometres of roads are closed to cars every Sunday morning so that people can walk, run and cycle safely. This event, called the Ciclovía, now attracts around 1.5 million people each week.

DBike-sharing schemes have also helped. Users can pick up a bike from a docking station, ride it across the city and leave it at another station. Many schemes now offer electric bikes, which make hills and long distances much easier, especially for older riders. In some cities, people can unlock a bike using an app on their phone, and the first thirty minutes are free.

ESupporters of cycling point to many benefits. Cycling produces no pollution, takes up far less space than driving, and is good for both physical and mental health. A study in the United Kingdom found that people who cycled to work had a lower risk of heart disease than people who drove or took public transport. Cycling is also cheap: after buying a bike, the costs of using it are very low.

FHowever, there are still problems. Many people say that they would like to cycle but feel unsafe on busy roads. Bikes are often stolen, and in some places there is nowhere safe to leave them. Weather is another difficulty, as few people enjoy cycling in heavy rain or snow. Experts agree that the most important step is to build cycle lanes which are physically separated from traffic. ‘If you build safe lanes,’ one city planner says, ‘people of all ages will use them.’

Why Do We Yawn?
It is one of the most common things the human body does — and one of the least understood.

Reading Passage 2 · The human body · 412 words

AEverybody yawns. We yawn when we are tired, when we are bored, and sometimes for no clear reason at all. Babies yawn even before they are born: scans have shown that they begin to yawn in the womb at around eleven weeks. Yet although yawning is one of the most common things the human body does, scientists still do not fully agree about why it happens.

BFor many years, the most popular explanation was that we yawn to get more oxygen. According to this idea, when we are tired our breathing becomes slow, oxygen levels in the blood fall, and a big yawn brings in extra air. However, in the 1980s, a psychologist called Robert Provine tested this idea. He asked volunteers to breathe air containing extra oxygen. They yawned just as often as before. The oxygen theory, it seemed, was wrong.

CA newer theory suggests that yawning helps to cool the brain. Like a computer, the brain works best at a certain temperature. When we yawn, we take in a large amount of air and stretch the jaw, which increases the flow of blood to the head. Researchers have found that people yawn less often when they hold a cold pack against their forehead, and that yawning is more common in mild weather than in very hot or very cold weather.

DPerhaps the strangest thing about yawning is that it is contagious. When we see, hear or even read about someone yawning, we often start to yawn ourselves. You may be yawning right now! Studies show that around half of adults yawn after watching a video of other people yawning. Interestingly, we are more likely to ‘catch’ a yawn from a family member or close friend than from a stranger.

ESome scientists believe this is connected to empathy, the ability to understand the feelings of others. Young children under the age of about four rarely catch yawns, and this is roughly the age at which children begin to understand that other people have their own thoughts and feelings. However, other researchers point out that the link is weak, and that age may be more important than empathy.

FHumans are not the only ones who yawn. Almost all animals with a backbone do it, including fish, birds and snakes. Chimpanzees and dogs can also catch yawns, and some studies suggest that dogs even catch them from their owners. Studying animals may help scientists finally to solve the mystery of why we yawn.

Farming in the Desert
Hot desert coasts have plenty of sunshine and seawater. Can they also grow food?

Reading Passage 3 · Food and technology · 459 words

AThe world needs more food, but good farmland is limited, and in many countries fresh water is becoming scarce. Agriculture already uses around 70% of the world’s fresh water. In dry regions such as the Middle East and North Africa, farmers often pump water from deep underground, but these supplies are running out. For this reason, some engineers have started to ask a surprising question: could we grow food using seawater and sunshine, two things that hot desert coasts have in large amounts?

BOne answer is the seawater greenhouse. The idea was developed by a British inventor, Charlie Paton, in the 1990s, after a holiday in Morocco. The first full-sized version was built on the island of Tenerife, and later projects followed in Oman, Abu Dhabi, Australia and Somaliland.

CThe greenhouse works in a simple way. First, seawater is pumped from the sea to the front wall of the greenhouse. Here, it runs down a wall made of a material similar to cardboard. Wind from the sea blows through this wet wall, and as the water evaporates, the air becomes cool and humid. This cool air then passes over the plants, which need less water because they lose less through their leaves. Finally, at the back of the greenhouse, the warm, wet air meets pipes that contain cold seawater. Drops of fresh water form on the pipes, and this water is collected and used for the crops.

DA different project in South Australia, called Sundrop Farms, uses the sun’s energy in a more high-tech way. Thousands of mirrors reflect sunlight onto a tall tower, producing heat and electricity. This energy is used to remove the salt from seawater, giving the farm enough fresh water for around 180,000 tomato plants. The farm produces about 15,000 tonnes of tomatoes a year, which are sold in Australian supermarkets.

EThese projects have attracted a lot of attention, but they are not without problems. Building them is expensive, and in their early years several projects closed because they could not make a profit. Sundrop Farms also had to be repaired after a storm damaged it in 2016. Critics also point out that removing salt from seawater produces very salty waste, which can harm sea life if it is put back into the sea carelessly.

FIn my view, these difficulties are real, but they should not stop us. The cost of solar technology has fallen sharply in the past twenty years, and it is likely to fall further. I believe that desert farming will never replace traditional agriculture, but it could become an important source of fresh vegetables for countries that have little water and few other options. Governments in dry countries should support research into these methods now, before their underground water runs out.

Four Days at School, Three Days Off

Extra reading · Education · 255 words

AIn some parts of the United States, more than 2,000 schools now open only four days a week. Students have longer lessons from Monday to Thursday, and Friday is a day off. The idea started in small country towns, where school districts wanted to save money on buses, heating and electricity. Now it is spreading to larger towns too.

BSupporters say the shorter week has several advantages. Teachers can use Fridays to plan lessons, and many school districts say it is easier to find and keep good teachers, because the four-day week is attractive to them. Some students say they feel less tired and enjoy having more time for hobbies, part-time jobs or family.

CHowever, research suggests the savings are smaller than many people expected — usually only around 1 to 2 per cent of a school’s budget. More worryingly, a number of studies have found that students in four-day schools make slightly less progress in reading and maths than similar students in five-day schools, especially when the shorter week continues for several years.

DParents are divided. Some love the long weekend, but others, especially those who work full-time, must find and pay for childcare on Fridays. For families with little money, the extra day off can also mean one less free school lunch each week.

EMost experts agree that the four-day week is not simply good or bad. It seems to work best when schools keep the total number of teaching hours the same and offer activities or extra lessons on the free day.

A Short History of Tea

Speed reading · 300 words · 215 words

ATea was first drunk in China, probably more than 4,000 years ago. According to an old story, the emperor Shen Nong was boiling water outside when some leaves from a tea bush fell into his pot. He liked the taste, and a new drink was born. Whether the story is true or not, tea had become a popular drink across China by the time of the Tang dynasty, which began in 618.

BTea reached Japan in the ninth century, brought by Buddhist monks who had studied in China. In Japan, it later became part of a special ceremony which is still practised today. European traders did not discover tea until much later. Dutch ships brought the first tea to Europe in 1610, and it arrived in England in the 1650s, where it was sold in coffee houses as a medicine.

CAt first, tea in England was very expensive, and only rich people could afford it. But in the eighteenth century, the price fell, and tea became the national drink. In recent years, many city governments have chosen to build cycle lanes rather than new roads for cars. Although this policy has some drawbacks, I believe that its benefits are far greater.

The main advantage of cycle lanes is that they make cities healthier. Cycling produces no pollution, so when more people cycle, the air becomes cleaner. It is also excellent exercise, and studies have shown that people who cycle to work have a lower risk of heart disease. In addition, bicycles take up much less space than cars, which means that more people can travel on the same street. Paris is a good example: when the city built new lanes, the number of cyclists on some main roads more than doubled.

On the other hand, there are some disadvantages. Drivers often complain that cycle lanes make the roads narrower and cause traffic jams. Cycling is also less attractive in cold or rainy weather, so lanes may be empty for part of the year. Finally, building safe lanes that are separated from traffic costs a lot of money.

However, these problems can be reduced. Traffic usually becomes lighter when people have a real choice of transport, and the cost of a cycle lane is much lower than the cost of a new road. In my view, the long-term benefits for health, the environment and city life clearly outweigh the disadvantages.
