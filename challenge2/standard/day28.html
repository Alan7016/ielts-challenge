Reading Faster Without Understanding Less
Can we train ourselves to read more quickly — and still remember what we have read?

Reading Passage 1 · Literacy skills · 589 words

AMost people think that speed reading simply means moving your eyes across a page very quickly. In fact, reading fast is only half of it. The other half is how much you understand and remember once you have finished. A reader who races through a chapter but cannot say what it was about has not really read it at all. For this reason, the best speed readers are judged not only on how many words they read in a minute but also on how many questions about the text they can answer correctly afterwards.

BWhy is this skill becoming so important? Modern life produces enormous amounts of written information. Office workers may have dozens of emails, reports and documents to deal with every day, and university students often have long reading lists for every assignment. People in these situations often feel that they need to read more, and read faster, just to keep up.

CBefore trying to read faster, however, it is worth stopping to think about why you are reading. If you only need a general idea of a topic, you can skim the text quickly, looking mainly at headings and the first sentence of each paragraph. If you need to find one particular fact, such as a date or a name, you can scan for it and ignore everything else. But if you have to understand every detail, for example in instructions or an exam question, you should slow down. Choosing the right speed for the purpose is the first skill of an efficient reader.

DOne simple change can make a big difference: reading silently. Many people, especially children, move their lips or say the words in their head as they read. This habit slows them down, because the brain has to wait for each word to be 'spoken' before moving on. Readers who learn to take in the meaning without saying every word can often read considerably faster.

EA second technique is known as chunking. Beginners tend to read one letter or one word at a time. As readers become more experienced, their eyes start to take in small groups of words — 'chunks' — in a single look, such as 'at the end of' or 'in the morning'. Instead of stopping on every word, the eyes jump from one chunk to the next.

FSkilled readers take in large chunks, and their eyes rest on each one for only a fraction of a second. They hardly ever look back at words they have already read. As a result, their eyes do less work, and they can process far more information in the same amount of time.

GSlow readers, on the other hand, look at very small chunks, sometimes single words, and they often go back to re-read earlier parts of a sentence. Each time they do this, they lose the thread of the text, and their understanding becomes confused. This stop-start movement is also tiring for the eyes, which is one reason why many slow readers say that they find reading boring and hard to concentrate on.

HSo what is the best way to improve? Experts agree that there is no substitute for regular practice. However, practice only works if you want to keep reading. If you keep finding yourself reading the same paragraph again and again, it may be a sign that the material is not right for you. Choose texts that you find genuinely interesting — magazines, stories or articles about your hobbies — and you will be surprised how quickly your speed improves.

Libraries That Come to You
Where there is no library building, some communities have found other ways to bring books to readers.

Reading Passage 2 · Literacy around the world · 388 words

AIn most towns, a library is a building that readers visit. But in some parts of the world, distance, poor roads or a lack of money mean that many people cannot reach a library at all. For these communities, the solution has been to take the library to the readers instead.

BOne of the best-known examples is in north-eastern Kenya, where many families are nomadic herders who move with their animals in search of water and grass. Because roads are rare and vehicles often cannot cross the sandy ground, the national library service began using camels in the 1990s. Each camel can carry boxes of books, together with a tent that serves as a reading room, to settlements far from the nearest town. Librarians stay for a while, lend books, and return some time later to collect them and bring new ones.

COther countries have used different animals. In some rural areas of Zimbabwe, donkeys have pulled carts loaded with books to schools and villages. The carts are slow, but they are cheap to run and can travel along narrow paths where a van would get stuck. Some of the carts have even carried simple equipment, such as solar panels, so that villagers can use other learning materials too.

DWater can also be a route to reading. In Norway, where many people live on small islands or in villages along the long coastline, a book boat has visited coastal communities for decades. It stops at small harbours for a short time, and families come on board to choose books. For many children, the boat's arrival is one of the highlights of the year.

EThese projects face real difficulties. Books can be damaged by dust, rain or sea air, and lending is difficult to organise when the borrowers move from place to place. Funding is also a constant worry, because mobile libraries depend on government support or donations. Some critics argue that the money would be better spent on building permanent schools.

FSupporters, however, point out that mobile libraries do more than lend books. Librarians often run reading activities for children, help adults who are learning to read, and bring newspapers and information about health. In places where literacy rates are low, they believe, even a few boxes of books can make a real difference to a child's future.

Writing Task 1 — Tenses, Overview and Spelling

Choose the right tense for the time period on the graph, always include an overview, and spell your adverbs correctly — examiners notice all three.

Past simple for a finished period in the past: Numbers rose between 2010 and 2015.
Present perfect for a trend that continues to now: Since 2020, numbers have risen steadily.
Overview: one or two sentences giving the main trend, with no small details: Overall, there was an upward trend…

The line graph compares the number of library books borrowed by boys and girls at Northfield School over a seven-year period from 2018 to 2024.

Overall, borrowing by boys increased dramatically, while the figure for girls remained fairly stable. By the end of the period, boys were borrowing slightly more books than girls.

In 2018, girls borrowed about 12,000 books, three times as many as boys, who borrowed only 4,000. Both figures fell slightly in 2020, to 11,000 and 3,500 respectively. After that, the number of books borrowed by girls stayed between 11,000 and 12,000, and then decreased gradually to about 10,800 in 2024.

In contrast, borrowing by boys rose sharply from 2020 onwards. It reached 8,000 in 2022 and continued to climb, reaching a peak of 11,000 in 2024. As a result, the two lines crossed in the final year of the period.
