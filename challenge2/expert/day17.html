Before you write about today's graph, read the story behind it. This article describes the same three bakeries you will write about later — notice how it describes changes over time. The bakeries come from a textbook exercise; the article and the reasons it gives are invented for practice.

Crust and Consequence: A Decade of Upheaval on One London High Street

Business · 737 words

Few commercial sectors appear as reassuringly unchanging as the neighbourhood bakery. Yet a decade of figures from three independent bakeries in one corner of north London tells a rather different story — one of complacency punished, caution belatedly rewarded, and a newcomer that quietly outgrew its rivals. At the turn of the millennium, the hierarchy seemed fixed. Lovely Loaves, a long-established family firm, was taking in roughly £82,000 a year, comfortably eclipsing Robbie's Bakery, on around £54,000, while Bernie's Buns, then little more than a counter in a converted newsagent's, earned a modest £20,000.

For the first half of the decade, Lovely Loaves did little to suggest that its dominance was under threat. Revenue climbed to a high of approximately £93,000 in 2001, and while the following three years brought some volatility — a dip to £80,000 in 2003 was followed by a recovery to £90,000 the next year — the business never looked in danger. Its owners, by their own later admission, saw little reason to change a formula that had served them for thirty years.

That confidence proved misplaced. The arrival in 2005 of a supermarket with an in-store bakery, barely five minutes' walk away, coincided with the steepest single fall recorded by any of the three firms: income slumped by almost a third, to £62,000. Rather than stabilising, the decline gathered pace after a brief plateau in 2006–2007, and by 2008 annual earnings had sunk to around £41,000. They then flatlined at roughly £40,000 for the remaining two years — less than half their 2001 peak.

The owners' response was, by their own account, too little and too late. Extended opening hours and a leaflet campaign in 2006 appear to have done little more than slow the rate of decline for a year, and a planned refurbishment was abandoned when the bank declined to lend. By the time the family considered broadening the menu, many of its regulars had, as one of them put it, "simply got out of the habit of coming in".

Robbie's Bakery followed an almost opposite trajectory. For six consecutive years its takings were strikingly static at about £54,000, a figure the founder attributes to "a loyal but ageing customer base and absolutely no advertising". It was only when management passed to his daughter in 2006 that the business began to move. A modest rise to £60,000 that year was enough to edge it past its struggling rival, and a combination of a new café area and wholesale contracts with local restaurants then propelled income sharply upwards. Earnings leapt from £68,000 in 2007 to £97,000 in 2008 — the largest year-on-year increase in the data — before growing more gradually to a peak of approximately £104,000 in 2010, nearly double the figure at which the bakery had stagnated for so long.

The most striking transformation, though, was at Bernie's Buns. Its early progress was halting: income crept up to £25,000 in 2001 before slipping back to £22,000 in 2002. Relocation to larger premises in 2003 lifted earnings to £38,000, and there they remained, give or take a little, until 2006, when they eased slightly to £36,000. From 2007, however, the firm's growth accelerated markedly, with revenue reaching £61,000 in 2008 and approximately £66,000 by 2010 — a more than threefold increase over the decade. Notably, Bernie's overtook Lovely Loaves in 2008, the year in which the two firms' fortunes crossed most dramatically.

Its owner, Bernie Adeyemi, is disarmingly modest about the turnaround. He credits two decisions above all: taking on catering orders for local offices, which provided a dependable weekday income, and extending his range well beyond the sweet buns that gave the shop its name. "We listened," he says. "If three customers in a week asked for something, we started baking it." Whether by accident or design, it was precisely the kind of responsiveness his larger neighbour lacked.

Economists are wary of drawing sweeping conclusions from three small businesses. Nonetheless, Dr Hannah Okafor, who researches independent retail at a London university, argues that the pattern is instructive. "What these figures illustrate is not that small is beautiful or that supermarkets are unbeatable," she says. "It is that inertia is expensive. The two bakeries that diversified — into cafés, wholesale, specialist products — expanded, whereas the one that relied on reputation alone saw its income halve." Whether that lesson has been absorbed more widely remains, she concedes, an open question.

Tip: many topics are really about money or people. Words like spending, investment, expenditure are about money; phrases like home ownership, unemployment are about people — use the word money or people in your introduction. And watch out for rates, proportions and percentages: these are neither “how many” nor “how much”.
