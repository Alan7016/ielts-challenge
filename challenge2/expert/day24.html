The Two Sigma Problem

Education research · 382 words

In 1984 the American educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom published a short paper with a striking claim. Comparing students taught in conventional classes of around thirty with students taught by a tutor one-to-one, he reported that the tutored students performed about two standard deviations — two 'sigma' — better. Put simply, the average tutored student outperformed roughly 98 per cent of the students taught in an ordinary class. The tutoring in these studies was combined with what Bloom called mastery learning: students were tested regularly, given corrective help, and only moved on once they had genuinely understood each unit.

Bloom was not arguing that every child should have a private tutor. On the contrary, he acknowledged that one-to-one teaching was far too expensive for any society to provide on a large scale. His real point was a challenge: if tutoring could produce such dramatic gains, researchers should look for methods of group instruction that came as close as possible to the same effect. He called this 'the two sigma problem'.

For four decades, the problem has served as a kind of North Star for educational reformers. Each wave of technology has been presented, at least by its enthusiasts, as the long-awaited solution: first computer-assisted instruction, then adaptive software that adjusts the difficulty of exercises to each learner, then online video platforms, and most recently artificial-intelligence tutors that can hold a conversation with a student. Alongside these, cheaper human approaches such as peer tutoring and small-group teaching have also been tested.

There is, however, an awkward complication. When later researchers examined large numbers of tutoring studies, they generally found positive effects — but effects considerably smaller than the two sigma Bloom had reported. Some have pointed out that the original studies were small and short, and that students who are taught to the test of a particular unit may naturally score very highly on it. Two sigma, in other words, may describe an exceptional result rather than a typical one.

None of this makes Bloom's challenge irrelevant. Even a fraction of his reported effect would represent an enormous improvement if it could be delivered to millions of students at low cost. But it does suggest that any new technology claiming to have 'solved' the two sigma problem deserves to be examined with considerable care.

The Classroom Turned Inside Out
Video lessons and self-paced software promise to free every learner to move at their own speed. Can schools built around age and timetables keep up?

Reading Passage 3 · Education and technology · 808 words

AIn one corner of the room, a twelve-year-old is working through problems on equations that most of her classmates will not meet for another year. Beside her, a boy is watching a short video on fractions for the third time, pausing it every few seconds to copy the diagrams into his notebook. Their teacher moves between them with a tablet that shows, in real time, which pupils have mastered which skills and which are stuck. Nobody is listening to a lecture. To anyone educated in a traditional classroom, the scene looks almost chaotic; to its supporters, it represents the future of schooling.

BThe best-known source of such videos began almost by accident. In 2004, Salman Khan, then working as a financial analyst, started tutoring a young cousin in mathematics over the internet. When other relatives asked for help, he began recording short lessons and posting them online so that they could watch at their own convenience. To his surprise, the videos attracted thousands of viewers who were not related to him at all, and in 2008 he set up a non-profit organisation, Khan Academy, leaving his job the following year to work on it full-time. It now offers lessons in many subjects and languages.

CPart of the appeal lies in the format. A recorded explanation can be paused, rewound and replayed as often as a learner needs, without the embarrassment of asking a teacher to repeat something in front of thirty classmates. Combined with exercises that adapt to the learner's answers, this arrangement allows each student, at least in principle, to move on only once they have genuinely mastered a topic — an approach educationalists call mastery learning.

DThe attraction for schools is easy to understand. Teachers have long complained that a single lesson pitched at the middle of a class inevitably leaves some pupils bored and others lost. The obvious remedy, individual instruction, has always been prohibitively expensive, and successive waves of classroom technology, from educational television to interactive whiteboards, have promised far more than they delivered. Self-paced software appears to offer something closer to a personal tutor at a fraction of the cost.

EVideo lessons have also made possible a wider change known as the 'flipped classroom'. The idea is usually credited to two chemistry teachers in Colorado, Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams, who began recording their lessons around 2007 so that pupils who missed classes for sports or illness could catch up. They soon noticed that the recordings could be used by everyone. Students watched the explanation at home and spent lesson time doing the exercises that would traditionally have been homework, with the teacher on hand at the moment they were most likely to need help.

FYet the model has its critics, and their objections deserve to be taken seriously. Some argue that a recorded lecture followed by automated practice is not a revolution at all but the oldest form of teaching — transmission and drill — in a new format: old wine in new bottles. Others point out that software of this kind works best in subjects such as mathematics, where answers are clearly right or wrong, and is far less useful in history or literature, where the most valuable answers are open to interpretation. A flipped classroom also assumes that every pupil has a quiet place to study and a reliable internet connection at home, an assumption that does not hold for many families.

GA subtler danger is what might be called the illusion of progress. A student can collect badges and complete exercises by recognising familiar patterns, while understanding very little of why a method works. Self-pacing can also widen gaps: highly motivated pupils race ahead, while those who most need encouragement may quietly fall further behind, especially when nobody is setting the pace for them. Courses delivered entirely online have often been reported to have very low completion rates for precisely this reason.

HPerhaps the most awkward challenge, however, is organisational. Most school systems still group children by age and expect each year group to cover the same material at the same time. A model in which a twelve-year-old may be studying advanced algebra while a classmate is still revising fractions sits uneasily with timetables, textbooks and national examinations designed around the opposite assumption. Some teachers who welcome the approach have admitted that they are not sure what to do with a pupil who finishes the year's course in March.

INone of this suggests that video platforms will replace teachers. If anything, the evidence so far points the other way: the technology appears to be most effective when a skilled teacher decides how and when to use it, notices who is struggling, and provides the discussion, encouragement and challenge that no video can. What it may change is not whether teachers are needed but what they spend their time doing.
