The Placebo Paradox
Inactive treatments cannot cure disease. So why do they so often make patients feel better?

Reading Passage 1 · Medicine · 555 words

AIn 1955, the American anaesthetist Henry Beecher published a paper titled ‘The Powerful Placebo’, in which he claimed that roughly a third of patients across a range of conditions improved after receiving an inactive treatment. The figure became one of the most widely quoted statistics in medicine. Later analyses, however, showed that Beecher had not separated the effect of the placebo from the natural course of illness: many of his patients would simply have got better anyway. The idea that a sugar pill can cure disease turned out to be, at best, an exaggeration.

BYet the placebo response is not imaginary. Brain-imaging studies have shown that when patients expect pain relief, their brains release endorphins, the body’s natural painkillers, and that this release can be blocked by a drug called naloxone. In patients with Parkinson’s disease, a placebo injection has been found to trigger the release of dopamine, the chemical whose loss causes the disease’s symptoms. In other words, expectation produces measurable changes in the body — but mainly in symptoms that the brain itself regulates, such as pain, nausea and fatigue, rather than in the underlying disease.

CContext appears to matter enormously. Experiments have found that two placebo pills work better than one, that injections outperform pills, and that capsules described as expensive produce stronger effects than identical ones presented as cheap. Even colour plays a part: in several studies, blue pills were more effective as sedatives and red ones as stimulants — except, curiously, among Italian men, for whom blue is the colour of the national football team and is associated with excitement rather than calm.

DThe relationship between doctor and patient may be the most powerful ingredient of all. In a study of patients with irritable bowel syndrome led by Ted Kaptchuk at Harvard, one group received placebo acupuncture from a practitioner who was brisk and businesslike, while another received the same fake treatment from one who was warm, attentive and spent 45 minutes listening to them. The second group reported significantly greater improvement, suggesting that the ritual of care can itself be therapeutic.

ESuch findings create an ethical problem. Prescribing a placebo has traditionally required deception: the treatment works, it was assumed, only if the patient believes it is real. Most medical codes regard deceiving patients as unacceptable, and doctors who secretly prescribe inactive pills risk damaging the trust on which medicine depends. Surveys nevertheless suggest that a significant proportion of doctors in several countries admit to having given ‘impure’ placebos, such as vitamins, or antibiotics for viral infections, partly to satisfy patients.

FA surprising possible solution has emerged in the past decade. In ‘open-label’ placebo trials, patients are told plainly that they are receiving pills that contain no active ingredient, but that such pills have produced improvements in other studies. Remarkably, several trials — for conditions including chronic back pain and irritable bowel syndrome — have found that patients still benefit. Researchers do not yet fully understand why, though one explanation is that the act of taking a pill as part of a routine is itself associated with getting better. Sceptics caution that most of these trials have been small and that patients could not be prevented from guessing which group they were in. If the results hold, however, they may allow doctors to harness the placebo effect without lying.

Mining the Deep Ocean
The seabed holds metals the energy transition needs. Whether it should be mined is one of the hardest environmental questions of the decade.

Reading Passage 2 · Environment and resources · 600 words

AThree to six kilometres below the surface of the Pacific Ocean, scattered across the abyssal plains between Hawaii and Mexico, lie trillions of potato-sized lumps of rock known as polymetallic nodules. Formed over millions of years as metals in seawater slowly precipitate around a fragment of shell or a shark’s tooth, they are rich in manganese, nickel, cobalt and copper — metals essential to the batteries of electric vehicles. The area in which they are most concentrated, the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, covers some 4.5 million square kilometres.

BFor mining companies, the attraction is obvious. Demand for battery metals is expected to rise sharply as countries phase out petrol cars, and land-based mines are frequently associated with deforestation, polluted rivers and poor working conditions. Gerard Barron, chief executive of The Metals Company, has described the nodules as ‘a battery in a rock’, arguing that collecting them from the seabed would cause less harm than digging new mines in rainforests.

CScientists who study the deep sea are far less convinced. Diva Amon, a marine biologist who has taken part in expeditions to the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, has pointed out that the majority of species collected there are new to science. Because so little is known about these ecosystems, she argues, it is impossible to predict how mining would affect them. The nodules themselves are habitats: many animals, including sponges and corals, can live only by attaching themselves to a hard surface, and on the soft mud of the abyssal plains the nodules are often the only such surface available. Once removed, they would take millions of years to form again.

DThere are concerns, too, about the clouds of sediment that collecting machines would stir up. Deep-sea animals have evolved in exceptionally clear and still water, and many filter food from it. Plumes of sediment could spread for tens or even hundreds of kilometres, potentially smothering animals far beyond the mined area. A 1989 experiment off the coast of Peru, in which researchers ploughed a section of seabed to simulate mining, offers a sobering indication: when scientists returned 26 years later, the tracks were still clearly visible and many animal populations had not recovered.

ERegulating the industry is unusually complicated. The seabed beyond national boundaries is governed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA), a body established under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which describes these resources as the ‘common heritage of mankind’. The ISA has issued exploration contracts but has not yet agreed the rules that would allow commercial mining to begin. In 2021, the small Pacific nation of Nauru, which sponsors a mining company, triggered a little-known clause requiring the ISA to complete its regulations within two years, increasing the pressure for a decision.

FOpposition has grown in response. More than twenty countries, including France, Germany and New Zealand, have called for a pause or moratorium until the environmental risks are better understood, and several major car and technology manufacturers have pledged not to use metals from the deep sea until those risks are clearer. Some analysts also question whether the metals will be needed at all, since battery chemistries are changing rapidly and some newer designs use little or no cobalt or nickel.

GSupporters counter that waiting indefinitely is not a neutral choice, since every year of delay means more mining on land, with its well-documented damage to people and ecosystems. The underlying difficulty is that the comparison is between a familiar harm and an unknown one. Choosing between them requires not only better science, but also a decision about how much uncertainty societies are prepared to accept.

How Clever Are Animals?
The study of animal minds has moved from suspicion to enthusiasm. The challenge now is to get the balance right.

Reading Passage 3 · Animal cognition · 556 words

AFor much of the twentieth century, scientists who studied animal behaviour were deeply suspicious of any suggestion that animals might think. The dominant approach, behaviourism, held that behaviour should be explained in terms of learned associations between stimuli and responses, and that talk of animal minds was unscientific speculation. Researchers were warned against ‘anthropomorphism’ — attributing human thoughts and feelings to other species — and with good reason: the history of the field contained cautionary tales such as that of Clever Hans, a horse in early-twentieth-century Berlin who appeared to solve arithmetic problems but was in fact responding to unconscious cues from his questioners.

BSince the 1970s, however, the pendulum has swung decisively. A series of striking discoveries has revealed cognitive abilities in species once considered mere bundles of reflexes. New Caledonian crows manufacture hooked tools from twigs and can solve problems requiring several steps in sequence. Chimpanzees appear to understand what other individuals can and cannot see. Honeybees can learn the concept of ‘zero’, and octopuses, whose last common ancestor with humans was a worm-like creature living more than 500 million years ago, can open jars, escape from tanks and recognise individual people.

CThe octopus is especially instructive, because its intelligence evolved entirely independently of our own. Two-thirds of its neurons are located not in its brain but in its arms, each of which can taste, touch and to some degree act on its own. If such a radically different nervous system can produce flexible, problem-solving behaviour, then intelligence may be less a single ladder, with humans at the top, than a collection of solutions that evolution has arrived at repeatedly in response to similar challenges.

DI find this view persuasive, but it carries its own risks. In their enthusiasm, some researchers and many journalists have described animal achievements in language that implies far more than the evidence shows. A crow that bends a wire into a hook has demonstrated an impressive capacity for problem-solving; whether it ‘understands’ physics in anything like the way a human does is a separate question that the experiment cannot answer. Overstating such findings may ultimately damage the credibility of a field that has fought hard for scientific respectability.

EThere is also a methodological problem. Many experiments in animal cognition rely on small numbers of animals, sometimes a single individual, and on tasks designed with human abilities in mind. An animal that fails such a test may lack the ability being measured — or may simply lack interest in the reward, be distracted by an unfamiliar laboratory, or perceive the task through senses we barely share. The classic ‘mirror test’ of self-recognition, which most species fail, may tell us more about the importance of vision to primates than about self-awareness in, say, dogs, which rely far more on smell.

FNone of this means the study of animal minds should return to the cautious silence of the behaviourist era. The evidence that many species possess rich cognitive lives is now overwhelming, and it has practical consequences: several countries have extended animal-welfare protections to octopuses and crabs on the basis of research into their capacity to feel pain. What it does mean is that researchers should design tests that suit the animal rather than the investigator, and should describe their results with the same precision they would expect of any other science.

The Myth of the 10,000-Hour Rule

Extra reading 1 · Learning and expertise · 330 words

AFew ideas about success have spread as widely as the claim that it takes 10,000 hours of practice to become an expert. Popularised by the journalist Malcolm Gladwell in his 2008 book Outliers, the ‘rule’ was based on research by the psychologist Anders Ericsson, who had studied violin students at a music academy in Berlin. Ericsson found that the best performers had, by the age of twenty, accumulated an average of around 10,000 hours of practice — considerably more than their less accomplished classmates.

BEricsson himself, however, repeatedly objected to the way his findings were summarised. The figure of 10,000 was an average, not a threshold: some of the top violinists had practised far less. More importantly, his research emphasised not the quantity of practice but its quality. What he called ‘deliberate practice’ involves working at the edge of one’s ability on specific weaknesses, usually with feedback from a teacher, and is often mentally exhausting rather than enjoyable. Simply repeating an activity one can already do — playing familiar pieces, or hitting the same golf shots — produces little improvement, however many hours it fills.

CLater research has also questioned how much practice explains at all. A large analysis published in 2014 by Brooke Macnamara and colleagues, which combined the results of 88 studies, concluded that deliberate practice accounted for about a quarter of the differences in performance in games such as chess, around a fifth in music and sport, and only about 4 per cent in education. Other factors — the age at which people start, working-memory capacity, personality and simple opportunity — appear to matter considerably.

DNone of this means that practice is unimportant; in almost every field, experts practise a great deal. But the popular version of the rule may mislead in two directions. It can encourage people to believe that hours alone guarantee success, and it can discourage those who make slower progress from recognising that the way they practise may matter more than how long.

As demand grows for the metals needed in batteries and other clean technologies, companies are proposing activities, such as mining the deep seabed, whose environmental consequences remain largely unknown. I agree that caution should be the starting point, but I believe that requiring effects to be “fully understood” sets a standard that could never be met and could itself cause harm.

The case for restraint is strong, particularly where damage may be irreversible. Deep-sea ecosystems are among the least studied on Earth, and most species collected there have never been described. Evidence from a disturbance experiment off Peru, where tracks remained visible and animal populations had not recovered after twenty-six years, suggests that mistakes made there could last for generations. When the potential loss is permanent, the burden of proof should lie with those who wish to proceed, not with those who urge caution.

However, complete understanding of any complex natural system is unattainable, and waiting for it can become a disguised decision to do nothing. Inaction is not neutral: if metals are not obtained from the sea, they will be dug from land, where mining is already linked to deforestation and pollution. A blanket ban until certainty is achieved may therefore simply move environmental damage elsewhere.

A more defensible policy would allow new industries to proceed only in carefully staged steps — small, monitored trials, independent scientific review and the power to halt operations if serious harm appears. In conclusion, while I strongly support a precautionary approach, I disagree that full understanding should be a precondition, since it is both impossible to achieve and not without its own risks.
