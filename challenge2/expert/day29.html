Through the Lens: The Ethics of Wildlife Photography

Reading Passage 1 · Ethics and the environment · 425 words

AWildlife photography has never been more popular. Affordable cameras, powerful zoom lenses and smartphones have turned millions of people into nature photographers, and social media allows a striking image of a fox or an owl to reach an audience of millions within hours. Many conservationists welcome this: photographs can awaken public concern for species and habitats far more effectively than statistics. Yet the boom has also raised uncomfortable questions about what photographers are prepared to do for the perfect shot.

BThe most widely criticised practice is baiting — luring animals with food so that they appear in a convenient place. Supporters argue that it does little harm and allows close-up images that would otherwise be impossible. Critics point out that regularly fed animals may lose their fear of people, become dependent on handouts or gather in unnatural numbers, increasing the spread of disease. In my view, occasional baiting of common garden birds is hardly a scandal, but baiting predators such as owls with live mice, as some photographers have done, is indefensible.

CDisturbance is a subtler problem. A photographer who approaches a nest too closely may cause parent birds to abandon their eggs; a crowd of photographers waiting for a rare visitor can prevent it from feeding. Drones, increasingly used to capture aerial images, can alarm birds and mammals that perceive them as predators. Some countries have responded by restricting drone use in protected areas, and many photography organisations now publish codes of conduct.

DSocial media has added a further complication. When a photograph is posted with its exact location, other photographers may follow, and a quiet site can be overwhelmed within days. For this reason, some conservation groups now ask photographers not to reveal the locations of sensitive species. I believe this request is entirely reasonable, even if it means sacrificing some of the attention a photograph might otherwise attract.

EFinally, there is the question of honesty. Major photography competitions have disqualified entries after judges concluded that animals had been captive or, in at least one widely reported case, stuffed. Such incidents damage public trust, because the power of a wildlife photograph depends on the viewer’s belief that the moment really happened.

FNone of this means that wildlife photography should be discouraged. At its best, it is a form of witness that has helped to protect species and places. But the welfare of the subject must always come before the picture. A photographer who disturbs a nest in order to show the world how beautiful birds are has misunderstood the purpose of the work.

Nature Out of Place

Reading Passage 2 · Ecology · 384 words

AWhen species are moved, deliberately or accidentally, beyond their natural range, most fail to survive in their new homes. A small proportion, however, not only survive but spread rapidly, with damaging consequences for native wildlife, agriculture and even human health. These so-called invasive species are now regarded by many ecologists as one of the leading causes of biodiversity loss worldwide.

BSome of the most notorious invasions began with good intentions. In 1935, cane toads were brought to north-eastern Australia in the hope that they would control beetles damaging sugar cane crops. They proved largely ineffective against the beetles but thrived in their new environment, spreading across thousands of kilometres. Because their skin contains a powerful poison, native predators such as snakes and lizards that attempt to eat them are frequently killed.

COther introductions were entirely accidental. Ships take on water for stability, known as ballast water, and release it in distant ports, often carrying living organisms with it. The zebra mussel, native to the region around the Black and Caspian Seas, is thought to have reached the Great Lakes of North America in this way in the 1980s. It multiplied so quickly that it blocked water intake pipes and altered entire freshwater ecosystems.

DIslands are particularly vulnerable. Species that evolved without ground-dwelling predators often have no defence against them. On the Pacific island of Guam, the brown tree snake, which probably arrived in cargo after the Second World War, caused the disappearance of most of the island’s native forest birds. Rats, introduced to islands across the world by ships, have had similar effects on seabird colonies.

EThe economic costs are enormous. A recent international assessment estimated that invasive species cost the global economy hundreds of billions of dollars a year, through damage to crops, fisheries, infrastructure and health, and that these costs have risen sharply in recent decades.

FPrevention is generally far cheaper than control. Stricter checks on imported goods, rules requiring ships to treat ballast water and rapid action when a new arrival is detected can all reduce the risk. On islands, removing invasive rats and other predators has allowed seabird populations to recover. Nevertheless, some scientists caution against treating every non-native species as an enemy, pointing out that many introduced plants and animals cause little harm and some even provide useful habitats.

Do Animals Grieve?

Reading Passage 3 · Animal behaviour · 322 words

AFor much of the twentieth century, scientists were wary of attributing emotions to animals. To describe an animal as ‘grieving’ was considered unscientific — an example of anthropomorphism, the tendency to project human feelings onto other species. In recent decades, however, a growing number of observations have led some researchers to reconsider.

BElephants provide some of the most frequently cited examples. Field researchers have repeatedly recorded elephants approaching the remains of dead members of their species, touching the bones with their trunks and feet, and lingering beside them, behaviour they seldom show towards the remains of other animals. Some observers have interpreted this as a form of mourning; others suggest it may reflect curiosity or an attempt to gather information.

CAmong primates, mother chimpanzees have occasionally been seen carrying the bodies of their dead infants for days or even weeks. Similar behaviour has been reported in some monkeys. Researchers remain divided over its meaning: it may express a strong bond that does not end immediately with death, or it may simply be that the mother does not yet recognise that the infant has died.

DOne case attracted worldwide attention in 2018, when a female orca in the Pacific Northwest of North America carried her dead calf for around seventeen days, covering a distance of more than a thousand miles. For many members of the public, it was difficult to see this as anything other than grief. Scientists were more cautious, noting that pushing a dead calf for so long would have been physically demanding and must have had some significance, but that its emotional content could not be measured directly.

ESceptics argue that, without access to an animal’s inner experience, claims about grief remain speculative. Supporters reply that refusing to recognise emotions in animals with complex social lives may be as unscientific as exaggerating them. What both sides increasingly agree on is the need for careful, systematic observation rather than isolated anecdotes.
