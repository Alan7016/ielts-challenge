Tim Urban uses humour and simple drawings to explain why some people put things off. Drawing on his own experience of writing a 90-page university thesis in the final three days, he describes the procrastinator’s brain as containing a Rational Decision-Maker that is regularly overruled by an Instant Gratification Monkey, which prefers easy, enjoyable activities. The only thing the Monkey fears is the Panic Monster, which wakes up when a deadline approaches and forces the person to work. Urban’s key point is that this system breaks down when there is no deadline — for example, in pursuing a career change, improving one’s health or maintaining relationships — because the Panic Monster never appears, and the delay can continue for years. He ends with a “Life Calendar” showing a 90-year life as a grid of weeks, arguing that everyone is procrastinating about something. I found the talk entertaining, and its point about deadline-free goals is genuinely thought-provoking.

Space programmes cost billions, and at a time of poverty, disease and climate change, it is reasonable to ask whether that money should be spent closer to home. While I understand this view, I believe that well-chosen investment in space is one of the ways in which we solve problems on Earth.

Those who oppose space spending argue that governments have a moral duty to address urgent human needs first. Millions of people lack clean water, adequate healthcare or education, and the sums spent on a single mission could fund hospitals or schools for years. From this perspective, sending probes to distant planets is a luxury that wealthy nations can only justify once basic problems have been solved.

Supporters respond that this presents a false choice. Space research has produced technologies that most people use daily, from satellite navigation and weather forecasting to advances in medical imaging and water purification. Satellites are now essential for monitoring deforestation, crop yields and rising sea levels, which makes them central to tackling climate change rather than a distraction from it. Moreover, space exploration inspires young people to study science and engineering, which benefits every area of a country’s economy.

In my view, the strongest argument is that space and Earth are not competing priorities. Governments should continue to fund space research, but with a clear emphasis on missions that bring tangible benefits on Earth, such as climate observation and communications. Prestige projects with little practical value are harder to justify while urgent human needs remain unmet.
