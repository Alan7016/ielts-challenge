The Science of Crowds
Crowd disasters are rarely caused by panic. Understanding why could save lives.

Reading Passage 1 · Physics and psychology · 336 words

AOn 24 July 2010, twenty-one people died in a crush at the Love Parade music festival in Duisburg, Germany. As with many similar disasters, early reports spoke of ‘panic’ — of a frightened crowd stampeding and trampling those who fell. Researchers who study crowds now regard this explanation as largely mistaken.

BThe physicist Dirk Helbing and colleagues analysed video footage of the disaster and of a 2006 crush during the Hajj pilgrimage at Mina, near Mecca. They found that the deaths were caused not by people pushing in fear but by density. When more than about five people occupy each square metre, individuals can no longer control their own movement; forces travel through the crowd like waves, and pressure can build to levels that make breathing impossible.

CAt lower densities, crowds organise themselves with remarkable efficiency. In busy pedestrian streets, people spontaneously form lanes moving in opposite directions, and at narrow doorways, flows alternate in bursts. These patterns emerge without any central coordination, which is why researchers often compare crowds to flowing liquids or flocks of birds.

DPsychologists have challenged the panic theory from another direction. Studies of emergencies such as the London bombings of 2005 found that survivors more often helped strangers than abandoned them. John Drury of the University of Sussex argues that shared danger tends to create a sense of common identity, which encourages cooperation rather than selfishness.

EThese findings have practical consequences. Organisers of large events now use computer models to predict where dangerous densities may develop, and design routes that avoid bottlenecks. Paradoxically, placing a pillar slightly in front of an exit can, in some circumstances, increase the rate at which people leave, by breaking up the build-up of pressure.

FNonetheless, models cannot capture everything. Real crowds contain children, older people and groups who refuse to separate, and information — or rumour — can spread unpredictably. Most experts agree that the most important safety measure remains the simplest: limiting the number of people allowed into a space in the first place.

Rethinking Easter Island
For decades, Rapa Nui was a warning about environmental self-destruction. The evidence now tells a different story.

Reading Passage 2 · Archaeology · 348 words

AEaster Island, or Rapa Nui, lies more than 2,000 kilometres from the nearest inhabited land in the south-eastern Pacific. It is famous for nearly 1,000 giant stone statues, known as moai, carved by its Polynesian inhabitants. For decades, the island was also famous as a warning: a society that had destroyed its own environment and collapsed as a result.

BThis ‘ecocide’ story was popularised by the geographer Jared Diamond in his 2005 book Collapse. According to Diamond, the islanders cut down the palm forests that once covered Rapa Nui, partly to move the statues, leading to soil erosion, food shortages, warfare and a dramatic population decline before Europeans arrived in 1722.

CIn recent years, however, many archaeologists have challenged this account. Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo argued that the island was settled later than previously thought — around 1200 AD rather than 800 AD — leaving less time for a slow decline. They also pointed to evidence that Polynesian rats, which arrived with the first settlers, ate the seeds of the palm trees, preventing the forest from regrowing.

DStudies of the island’s soils and food remains suggest that, rather than starving, islanders adapted to deforestation. They developed ‘rock gardens’, covering fields with stones to retain moisture and protect crops from wind, and continued to grow sweet potatoes in large quantities. A 2020 study led by Robert DiNapoli, examining the dating of statue platforms, found that construction continued after European contact, contradicting the idea of an earlier collapse.

EIf there was a catastrophe, the evidence now suggests it came from outside. European visitors brought diseases such as smallpox, to which islanders had no immunity, and in the 1860s Peruvian slave raiders kidnapped around 1,500 islanders, perhaps half the population. By 1877 only about 110 people remained on the island.

FThe debate matters beyond Rapa Nui. The ecocide story has often been used as a lesson about humanity’s relationship with the environment. Its critics argue that it unfairly blames the victims of colonialism for their own suffering, and that the island’s history is better understood as an example of resilience.

Should We Bring Back Extinct Species?
Genetic technology promises to reverse extinction. The writer is not convinced it should be a priority.

Reading Passage 3 · Biotechnology · 383 words

AIn 2003, a team of Spanish and French scientists briefly brought an extinct animal back to life. Using frozen skin cells taken from the last Pyrenean ibex, a wild goat that had died three years earlier, they produced a cloned kid. It lived for only a few minutes before dying from a lung defect. Nevertheless, the experiment raised a question that had previously belonged to science fiction: could extinction be reversed?

BSince then, so-called ‘de-extinction’ has attracted considerable investment. The US company Colossal Biosciences, founded in 2021, has raised hundreds of millions of dollars with the aim of recreating animals resembling the woolly mammoth and the thylacine, or Tasmanian tiger. Because no complete cells of these animals survive, the company plans to edit the genes of their closest living relatives — the Asian elephant and a small marsupial called the dunnart — to give them key characteristics of the extinct species.

CSupporters argue that the technology could benefit conservation more broadly. The genetic tools being developed could help endangered species by increasing their genetic diversity, or by making them resistant to diseases. Some also claim that reintroducing large herbivores such as mammoth-like elephants to the Arctic could help to preserve permafrost by trampling snow and encouraging grassland, though this idea remains highly speculative.

DI find many of these arguments unconvincing. An animal created by editing an elephant’s genes would not be a mammoth; it would be a cold-adapted elephant, without the behaviour that mammoths learnt from their herds over thousands of years. Moreover, the habitats in which many extinct species lived have themselves disappeared, so it is unclear where the recreated animals would survive.

EThere is also the question of cost. Research suggests that resources spent on de-extinction could save several times as many species if invested in protecting existing ones. With around a million species estimated to be at risk of extinction, according to a 2019 United Nations report, this is not a trivial concern.

FThat said, it would be wrong to dismiss the research altogether. The techniques may yet prove valuable for species on the brink, such as the northern white rhino, of which only two females survive. De-extinction should perhaps be seen not as a way of undoing past mistakes, but as one tool among many for preventing future ones.

Why Handwriting Still Matters

Extra reading 2 · Learning · 214 words

AAs keyboards and touchscreens replace pens, many schools have reduced the time spent teaching handwriting; several American states stopped requiring joined-up writing in the 2010s. Yet a growing body of research suggests that writing by hand may offer cognitive benefits that typing does not.

BIn a frequently cited 2014 study, the psychologists Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took lecture notes on laptops tended to transcribe the lecturer’s words verbatim, while those writing by hand, being slower, had to summarise. Although both groups remembered facts similarly, the handwriting group performed better on conceptual questions. Later attempts to replicate the study, however, produced mixed results, and some researchers argue that the difference disappears when laptop users are told not to copy verbatim.

CNeuroscience offers further evidence. A 2024 Norwegian study using EEG found that handwriting produced more widespread connectivity across brain regions associated with memory and learning than typing did. For young children, forming letters by hand appears to help them recognise letters more quickly.

DNone of this means that typing is harmful; for long texts, and for students with certain disabilities, it is often essential. The emerging consensus is not that one method should replace the other, but that handwriting deserves a continued place in education, particularly in the early years.

Advances in genetic engineering have raised the possibility of recreating animals that disappeared long ago, and some companies have already invested heavily in the idea. While I accept that this research may produce useful tools, I believe that the vast majority of conservation funding should be devoted to protecting species that still exist.

Those who support de-extinction argue that its benefits extend beyond spectacular projects such as recreating the mammoth. The techniques required — editing genes, cloning cells and managing genetic diversity — could be applied to endangered animals, for instance by making them resistant to diseases. Moreover, scientific progress often comes from ambitious goals, and research of this kind may lead to discoveries that no one can yet predict.

However, the case for prioritising existing species is far stronger. Estimates suggest that money spent on de-extinction could save several times as many species if invested in habitat protection and anti-poaching measures, and with around a million species thought to be at risk, the opportunity cost is enormous. In addition, a recreated animal would rarely be a true copy: an elephant with edited genes would lack the learnt behaviour of mammoth herds, and the ecosystems in which many extinct species lived no longer exist.

In conclusion, de-extinction research should not be abandoned entirely, particularly where it can help species on the brink, such as the northern white rhino. Nevertheless, it should be judged by its contribution to preventing future extinctions rather than reversing past ones, and protecting living species must remain the priority.
