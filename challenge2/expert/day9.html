Task 2 of 6

Article — The Global Challenge of an Ageing Population

Read the article below, then answer the questions that follow.

By the middle of this century, for the first time in recorded history, people aged over 65 are projected to outnumber children under five almost everywhere on Earth. This demographic shift, driven by falling birth rates and rising life expectancy, is unfolding at a pace that has caught many governments unprepared, and its consequences will reach far beyond healthcare policy into the very structure of national economies.

The pattern is most visible in East Asia and parts of Europe, where decades of low fertility have already produced dependency ratios once considered unimaginable. In Japan, often cited as a preview of what lies ahead for other nations, more than a quarter of the population is already over 65, and projections suggest this figure will climb further before the population itself begins to shrink at an accelerating rate. South Korea, Italy, and several Eastern European countries are following a strikingly similar trajectory, albeit a few decades behind.

What makes this trend particularly difficult to manage is its self-reinforcing nature. As the workforce contracts, tax revenue available to fund pensions and healthcare for a growing elderly population inevitably falls, just as demand for that spending rises sharply. Some governments have responded by gradually raising the retirement age; others have tried, with limited success, to encourage higher birth rates through financial incentives for young families. A third strategy, increasing immigration to offset workforce decline, has proven politically contentious in several countries, even where the economic case for it is strong.

Technology may offer a partial solution. Automation and robotics are already being deployed in Japanese elder-care facilities to reduce reliance on an increasingly scarce pool of care workers, and some economists argue that productivity gains from artificial intelligence could offset a shrinking workforce more effectively than any policy aimed directly at demographics. Whether this proves sufficient remains an open question, and one that will likely define economic policy debates for decades to come.

What is beyond dispute is the scale of the shift. Sustained increases in life expectancy, once viewed almost universally as an unambiguous triumph of modern medicine, are now forcing a far more complicated reckoning: societies must find ways to remain sustainable even as the balance between generations tilts in a direction with no historical precedent.

Useful language for describing trends (Writing Task 1)

You'll use phrases like these throughout today's Writing Task 1 work.

  • rose steadily — went up gradually
  • increased dramatically — went up suddenly, by a lot
  • remained stable — stayed roughly the same
  • fluctuated — went up and down irregularly
  • reached a peak — hit its highest point
  • fell sharply — went down suddenly, by a lot
  • declined gradually — went down slowly over time
  • a parallel increase — a similar rise happening at the same time
  • a considerable rise — a large, noticeable increase
  • at a faster/slower rate — describes how quickly a trend is changing

The table and chart provide information regarding population growth and the proportion of the population over 65 over a 100-year period in Japan.

According to the information, Japan's general population figures in 1950 were very different from those in 2005, and future predictions show even greater differences. In 1950, the number of people was just over 84 million, and only 4.9 percent (4.1 million) of these people were above the age of 65. By 2005, the percentage of older people had risen considerably to 20 percent, while the overall population had shown a parallel increase to nearly 128 million.

However, total population figures peaked in 2005, and it is expected that the number of people living in Japan will fall substantially over the next 50 years to a little below 90 million. In spite of this fall, the rise in the ageing population will continue, and at a faster rate, so that by 2055, 41 percent (36.5 million) of Japanese people will be over 65.

These statistics show two contrasting trends in Japan's demographics that will result in fewer citizens, but greater numbers of elderly people.

Key Grammar — Expressing Large and Small Differences

Answer these questions relating to comparisons in the two sample answers from today (Japan's population, and the diet and health charts).

1. Which adverb is used to emphasise "different" in the first sample answer?

2. What are the two opposites of "more"? Which is used with countable nouns?

3. Which adverbs are used to emphasise "higher" in the second sample answer?

4. Which two adverbs are used with "fewer" and "smaller" in the second sample answer? Which expresses a big difference, and which a small difference?

5. What phrase does the writer use to compare the incidence of illness in the two groups?

1. very ("were very different from those in 2005")

2. less (uncountable) and fewer (countable) — "fewer" is used with countable nouns, as in "far fewer vegetables".

3. much and considerably ("a much higher incidence", "considerably higher than in Group A")

4. far (fewer vegetables — a big difference) and slightly (smaller quantity of fruit — a small difference).

5. "a much higher incidence of illness... than" (with "over double the number" adding a second, more precise comparison).
