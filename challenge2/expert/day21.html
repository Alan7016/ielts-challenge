Dixit Algorizmi: How a Scholar from Khwarazm Became a Word

History of ideas · Khwarazm to the computer · 706 words

AEvery time a phone suggests the next word in a message, a map recalculates a route or a bank decides whether to approve a loan, an algorithm is at work. Few of the billions of people who depend on such procedures every day know that the word itself is a person's name — or rather, a distortion of one. It comes from Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a scholar whose name links him to Khwarazm, the ancient region around Khiva in present-day Uzbekistan.

BRemarkably little is known about his life. He was probably born around 780, and by the early ninth century he was working in Baghdad at the House of Wisdom, the great centre of learning supported by the caliph al-Ma'mun. There, scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic and built upon them. Al-Khwarizmi wrote on astronomy and geography as well as mathematics, but two of his books would prove decisive for the history of numbers.

CThe first, written around 820, was a guide to calculation whose title included the phrase al-jabr — "restoring" or "completing" — a reference to one of the operations used to solve equations. The book was far from abstract: much of it dealt with problems of inheritance, trade and the measurement of land. Yet its systematic method of reducing problems to standard forms and solving them step by step eventually gave its name, "algebra", to an entire branch of mathematics.

DThe second book explained the Indian system of numerals, with its nine digits and a symbol for zero, and showed how to add, subtract, multiply and divide with them. The Arabic original has been lost; the work survives only in Latin versions made in the twelfth century, one of which opens with the words "Dixit Algorizmi" — "Al-Khwarizmi said". Medieval readers soon treated the Latinised name as if it described the method itself, and calculating with the new numerals became known as "algorism".

EThe new system did not conquer Europe quickly. In 1202 Leonardo of Pisa, later known as Fibonacci, published his Liber Abaci, demonstrating the numerals' value for merchants, yet for centuries many accountants preferred Roman numerals and the counting board. In 1299 the bankers' guild of Florence forbade its members to use the new numerals in their account books, apparently because they were easier to falsify: a 0 could become a 6 or a 9 with a single stroke of the pen. The rivalry between "algorists", who calculated on paper, and "abacists", who used counters, continued into the sixteenth century.

FMeanwhile, the word itself kept changing. In Middle English, "algorism" was sometimes written "augrim", and writers who assumed that the word must be connected to the Greek arithmos, "number", gradually reshaped it, producing "algorithm". For several hundred years it meant little more than the art of calculating with Hindu–Arabic numerals.

GIts modern sense — a finite sequence of precise instructions that can be followed mechanically to solve a problem — emerged only slowly. In 1843 Ada Lovelace published a step-by-step method for calculating Bernoulli numbers on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine; it is often described as the first computer program, although she did not call it an algorithm. In 1936 Alan Turing described an imaginary machine in order to define precisely what it means for a problem to be computable, and by the middle of the twentieth century "algorithm" had become a central term in the new science of computing.

HThere is an irony in this history. Al-Khwarizmi's own procedures were written in words, not symbols: his algebra contains no equations of the kind a modern student would recognise, only carefully described steps. In that sense, he was writing algorithms in the modern meaning of the word centuries before the word acquired that meaning — and long before anyone imagined that such instructions might be carried out by machines rather than by people.

IIn Uzbekistan, his memory is far from obscure. A statue of him stands outside the old city walls of Khiva, and the country's leading university of information technology, in Tashkent, bears his name. It is a fitting tribute. Most people who use his name have never heard of him; but few names have travelled further from their owner, or ended up doing more work.

The Myth of the Lone Genius

Essay · History and sociology of science · 735 words

Popular history loves a lone genius. Newton under the apple tree, Edison in his laboratory, al-Khwarizmi in the House of Wisdom: each story reduces a long, collective and often messy process to a single mind and a single moment. The appeal is obvious. Stories need heroes, and a name is easier to remember than a network. But the heroic version is not merely simplified; in important respects it is wrong, and the error has consequences for how we fund, reward and teach science.

Scientists themselves have often known this. In 1675 Isaac Newton wrote to his rival Robert Hooke that if he had seen further, it was "by standing on the shoulders of giants". The image was not Newton's own: it is attributed to the twelfth-century scholar Bernard of Chartres, who described his generation as dwarfs perched on the shoulders of giants, able to see more not because of their own sharp sight but because they had been lifted up by others. Whether Newton meant the remark humbly or, as some historians suspect, as a veiled insult to Hooke, who was said to be short, the image has outlived the quarrel.

A more systematic challenge came from sociology. In 1922 William Ogburn and Dorothy Thomas published a list of 148 discoveries and inventions that had been made independently by two or more people at roughly the same time — from calculus, developed separately by Newton and Leibniz, to the theory of evolution by natural selection, reached by both Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace. Their conclusion was that once the necessary knowledge and tools exist, certain discoveries become, in a sense, inevitable: if one person does not make them, someone else soon will.

That argument can be pushed too far. "Inevitable" discoveries still require someone to make them, and the individuals involved often differed enormously in how completely they understood what they had found. Darwin had spent some twenty years gathering evidence; Wallace's essay, brilliant as it was, was a sketch. To say that an idea was "in the air" explains why it appeared when it did, but not why particular people grasped it first, nor why some versions proved far more fruitful than others.

Nor is credit distributed fairly, even when many people contribute. In 1968 the sociologist Robert Merton described what he called the Matthew effect, after a line in the Gospel of Matthew: "unto every one that hath shall be given". Established scientists, Merton observed, tend to receive disproportionate credit for work done jointly with — or even independently by — less well-known colleagues. Fame attracts further fame, while equally important contributors fade from the record. The statistician Stephen Stigler later proposed, half seriously, a "law" that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer — and, with deliberate irony, attributed the law to Merton.

Al-Khwarizmi's case fits this pattern in interesting ways. The numerals he explained were Indian, not his own invention, and he drew on earlier Greek, Persian and Indian mathematics. His name became attached to a method largely because a translator placed it at the head of a text; a different manuscript tradition might have given us an entirely different word. Yet it would be equally misleading to treat him as a mere channel through which other people's ideas passed. The systematic way in which he organised and explained those ideas was itself an achievement — and arguably the reason they spread at all.

Why does any of this matter beyond the history books? Because the myth of the lone genius shapes institutions. The most famous prizes are awarded to individuals — sometimes to no more than three people for work that involved hundreds. Funding bodies prefer projects with a single well-known name attached. Students are taught a history of great men and occasional great women, and may conclude that if they are not geniuses, science is not for them. A more accurate picture — of science as a conversation that runs across centuries, languages and continents — would be not only fairer but more encouraging.

None of this requires us to abandon admiration. It requires us to aim it more accurately: at the translator as well as the author, the assistant as well as the professor, and at the long chain of nameless teachers through whom knowledge travelled from Khwarazm to the device in your pocket. Genius, where it exists, is real. It is simply never alone.

📄 The passage — Dixit Algorizmi: How a Scholar from Khwarazm Became a Word

AEvery time a phone suggests the next word in a message, a map recalculates a route or a bank decides whether to approve a loan, an algorithm is at work. Few of the billions of people who depend on such procedures every day know that the word itself is a person's name — or rather, a distortion of one. It comes from Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a scholar whose name links him to Khwarazm, the ancient region around Khiva in present-day Uzbekistan.

BRemarkably little is known about his life. He was probably born around 780, and by the early ninth century he was working in Baghdad at the House of Wisdom, the great centre of learning supported by the caliph al-Ma'mun. There, scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic and built upon them. Al-Khwarizmi wrote on astronomy and geography as well as mathematics, but two of his books would prove decisive for the history of numbers.

CThe first, written around 820, was a guide to calculation whose title included the phrase al-jabr — "restoring" or "completing" — a reference to one of the operations used to solve equations. The book was far from abstract: much of it dealt with problems of inheritance, trade and the measurement of land. Yet its systematic method of reducing problems to standard forms and solving them step by step eventually gave its name, "algebra", to an entire branch of mathematics.

DThe second book explained the Indian system of numerals, with its nine digits and a symbol for zero, and showed how to add, subtract, multiply and divide with them. The Arabic original has been lost; the work survives only in Latin versions made in the twelfth century, one of which opens with the words "Dixit Algorizmi" — "Al-Khwarizmi said". Medieval readers soon treated the Latinised name as if it described the method itself, and calculating with the new numerals became known as "algorism".

EThe new system did not conquer Europe quickly. In 1202 Leonardo of Pisa, later known as Fibonacci, published his Liber Abaci, demonstrating the numerals' value for merchants, yet for centuries many accountants preferred Roman numerals and the counting board. In 1299 the bankers' guild of Florence forbade its members to use the new numerals in their account books, apparently because they were easier to falsify: a 0 could become a 6 or a 9 with a single stroke of the pen. The rivalry between "algorists", who calculated on paper, and "abacists", who used counters, continued into the sixteenth century.

FMeanwhile, the word itself kept changing. In Middle English, "algorism" was sometimes written "augrim", and writers who assumed that the word must be connected to the Greek arithmos, "number", gradually reshaped it, producing "algorithm". For several hundred years it meant little more than the art of calculating with Hindu–Arabic numerals.

GIts modern sense — a finite sequence of precise instructions that can be followed mechanically to solve a problem — emerged only slowly. In 1843 Ada Lovelace published a step-by-step method for calculating Bernoulli numbers on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine; it is often described as the first computer program, although she did not call it an algorithm. In 1936 Alan Turing described an imaginary machine in order to define precisely what it means for a problem to be computable, and by the middle of the twentieth century "algorithm" had become a central term in the new science of computing.

HThere is an irony in this history. Al-Khwarizmi's own procedures were written in words, not symbols: his algebra contains no equations of the kind a modern student would recognise, only carefully described steps. In that sense, he was writing algorithms in the modern meaning of the word centuries before the word acquired that meaning — and long before anyone imagined that such instructions might be carried out by machines rather than by people.

IIn Uzbekistan, his memory is far from obscure. A statue of him stands outside the old city walls of Khiva, and the country's leading university of information technology, in Tashkent, bears his name. It is a fitting tribute. Most people who use his name have never heard of him; but few names have travelled further from their owner, or ended up doing more work.
