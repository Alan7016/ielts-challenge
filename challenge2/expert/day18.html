A full-length IELTS Academic passage. Allow 25 minutes, then answer questions 1–23. The writer expresses views, so read for opinion, not just facts.

The Automation Paradox

Economics · 671 words

AIn 1930, the economist John Maynard Keynes predicted that, within a century, technological progress would make his grandchildren's generation so productive that they would need to work only fifteen hours a week. The productivity half of his prediction proved remarkably accurate: output per worker in the developed world has risen roughly fivefold since then. The leisure half did not. Most adults in wealthy countries still work around forty hours a week, and many work considerably more. This gap between what technology makes possible and what actually happens lies at the heart of today's debate about automation.

BFears that machines will destroy jobs are hardly new. In the early nineteenth century, English textile workers known as the Luddites smashed mechanised looms that threatened their livelihoods. Yet over the following two centuries, employment did not collapse; it grew, as new industries absorbed the workers that old ones no longer needed. Economists sometimes call the belief that there is a fixed amount of work to be done the "lump of labour fallacy", and many argue that today's anxiety about artificial intelligence is simply its latest form.

CThere are, however, reasons to suspect that this time may be different. Previous waves of automation largely replaced physical labour, pushing workers towards jobs that required thinking, communication and judgement. Artificial intelligence is the first technology that competes directly with these cognitive abilities. Systems that can draft legal contracts, analyse medical scans and write computer code threaten precisely the kind of work that was once considered safe. Moreover, the speed of change is unprecedented: whereas the transition from farm to factory unfolded over several generations, AI tools have moved from research laboratories into everyday use in less than a decade.

DThe economic historian Carl Benedikt Frey draws an important distinction between technologies that replace workers and those that enable them. During the first decades of the Industrial Revolution, he notes, wages for ordinary workers stagnated for almost fifty years, even as the economy grew; the benefits flowed overwhelmingly to the owners of factories. Only later, when technology began to create new, better-paid roles, did living standards rise broadly. If AI follows the first pattern rather than the second, Frey warns, a long and painful transitional period is likely.

EOther researchers are more optimistic. Studies by the economist David Autor suggest that most occupations consist of many separate tasks, only some of which can be automated. A radiologist, for example, does far more than interpret images: she consults colleagues, advises patients and makes judgements about uncertain cases. In Autor's view, AI is more likely to change jobs than to eliminate them, and could even help middle-skilled workers to perform tasks that once required years of specialised training.

FEven if the total number of jobs remains stable, the question of who benefits remains. Productivity gains do not automatically become leisure or higher wages; they are distributed according to economic and political choices. The reason Keynes's grandchildren still work forty-hour weeks, many economists argue, is not that technology failed, but that the rewards of growth were largely channelled into higher consumption and into profits rather than shorter working hours.

GFor this reason, a growing number of proposals focus less on technology itself than on the institutions that surround it. Some advocate universal basic income, a regular payment to every citizen regardless of employment, which was trialled in Finland between 2017 and 2018. Others favour lifelong learning accounts, shorter standard working weeks or taxes on automated systems. Critics of basic income point out that it would be extremely expensive, and that the Finnish trial, while improving participants' wellbeing, did not significantly increase employment.

HIt seems to me that the most important lesson of history is not that technology always creates enough jobs in the end, but that the end can be a very long time coming. Whether automation produces Keynes's age of leisure or an era of insecurity and inequality will depend far more on the decisions societies make in the next twenty years than on the capabilities of the machines themselves.

Reading — An Opinion Piece: "In Defence of Boring Work"

Columns and opinion pieces test inference: understanding what the writer implies, their tone, and how they build an argument. This skill transfers directly to your own essays.

In Defence of Boring Work

Opinion · 368 words

Every new wave of automation arrives with the same promise: machines will take over the dull, repetitive tasks, leaving humans free for the creative and meaningful work they were always meant to do. It is an appealing vision, and in many respects a sincere one. But it rests on an assumption that deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives — namely, that repetitive work has no value to the people who do it.

Consider the warehouse worker, the data-entry clerk or the hospital porter. Their tasks may appear monotonous from the outside, yet surveys repeatedly find that a large proportion of people in such roles describe their jobs as satisfying. The satisfaction rarely comes from the task itself. It comes from routine, from colleagues, from the modest but real pride of doing something well and being paid for it. Remove the job, and you remove all of these at once.

Advocates of automation tend to reply that displaced workers can simply retrain for better positions. The evidence for this is, to put it politely, mixed. Studies of factory closures in the United States have found that many workers who lost their jobs in their forties and fifties never regained their previous income; a significant number left the workforce altogether. "Retraining" is a reassuring word in a policy document. In a town where the main employer has disappeared, it can be a cruel one.

None of this is an argument against technology. Automation has eliminated genuinely dangerous work, from mining to the handling of toxic chemicals, and few would wish to reverse that. The point is rather that we should be honest about what is lost as well as what is gained. A job is not merely a bundle of tasks to be optimised. For many people, it is a structure for the day, a source of identity and a reason to leave the house.

If we are serious about a future in which machines do more of the work, we will need to find other ways of providing what work currently provides — not only income, but purpose and community. That is a far harder problem than building a better robot, and one that engineers alone cannot solve.

Writing Task 2 — Answering the Exact Question

At band 8+, Task Response depends on a fully developed position that answers every part of the question. Many candidates write a good essay on a slightly different question.

📘 "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"

  • This is not a simple advantages/disadvantages essay — you must weigh them and give a verdict.
  • Your thesis must say which side is stronger and why (the criterion you use to weigh them: scale, permanence, who is affected…).
  • A typical structure: introduction + verdict · the (weaker) side · the (stronger) side, explicitly compared · conclusion re-stating the verdict.
  • Avoid sitting on the fence ("there are both advantages and disadvantages"). A qualified verdict is fine: "the benefits outweigh the drawbacks, provided that …".

Artificial intelligence and automation are now capable of performing not only manual labour but also many cognitive tasks once thought to be uniquely human. While optimists envisage a future of abundance and leisure, I would argue that, at least in the coming decades, the disadvantages of this development outweigh its advantages — not because the technology itself is harmful, but because its benefits are unlikely to be shared fairly without significant changes in policy.

It would be wrong to dismiss the advantages. Automation has already eliminated much dangerous and exhausting work, from mining to the handling of toxic substances, and its capacity to raise productivity is undeniable: output per worker in developed economies has increased roughly fivefold since 1930. In fields such as medicine, AI is more likely to augment than replace professionals, enabling faster diagnosis and freeing doctors to spend more time with patients.

What concerns me most, however, is the distribution of these gains and the speed at which the transition is occurring. History offers little reassurance here. During the early Industrial Revolution, ordinary wages stagnated for almost fifty years while factory owners prospered, and only later did living standards rise more broadly. Today's transition is far faster: AI tools have moved from laboratories into everyday use within a decade, leaving displaced workers little time to adapt. It is true that new industries have always eventually absorbed redundant workers; yet studies of factory closures show that many people who lose their jobs in middle age never regain their previous income. Such insecurity carries social costs well beyond lost earnings, since work provides many people with structure, identity and a sense of purpose.

In conclusion, although automation promises greater productivity and safer working conditions, I believe that its drawbacks currently outweigh these benefits. Only if governments ensure that the gains are widely distributed — through retraining, a stronger safety net or shorter working weeks — is the prosperous, leisured society some predict likely to materialise.
