Three Days from Hunger? How Cities Are Fed

Economics · Food systems · 387 words

It is sometimes said that every large city is only a few days away from running out of food. The claim sounds alarmist, but it contains an uncomfortable truth. A modern supermarket keeps remarkably little stock on its premises. Rather than filling warehouses, retailers rely on what is known as just-in-time delivery: computer systems track what is sold, and replacement stock arrives, often overnight, in precisely the quantities required. Storage is expensive, and fresh food is perishable, so holding large reserves makes little commercial sense.

The result is a system of extraordinary efficiency. The food in an ordinary city kitchen may have passed through farms on several continents, processing plants, packaging centres, ports, distribution hubs and a fleet of refrigerated lorries before reaching the shelf. Thanks to this chain, urban consumers enjoy a variety of food, at a range of prices, that would have astonished their grandparents. Tomatoes in winter and tropical fruit in northern cities are no longer luxuries.

Efficiency, however, comes at a cost. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and a system designed to carry almost no spare capacity has little ability to absorb a shock. A fuel strike, a storm that closes a major road, a blocked shipping route or a wave of panic buying can empty shelves within days. Such events are usually temporary — the chains have, on the whole, proved remarkably reliable — but they reveal how dependent cities are on systems that most of their inhabitants never see.

Some analysts argue that cities have traded resilience for convenience, and that they should rebuild a degree of local food production: market gardens on the urban fringe, rooftop farms, community growing schemes and, more ambitiously, high-tech indoor farms. Others reply that local production can never feed a large city, which may require the output of an area of farmland many times its own size, and that a diverse global supply is itself a form of resilience, since a poor harvest in one region can be offset by imports from another.

Both sides agree on one point: the question is no longer simply how to produce more food, but how to produce and move it in ways that can withstand disruption. As the next passage shows, some of the proposed answers involve moving farms not outwards, but upwards.

Farming Upwards: The Promise and Limits of Vertical Farms
Indoor farms stacked floor upon floor were hailed as the future of agriculture. A decade of commercial experience has produced a more complicated picture.

Reading Passage 2 · Agriculture and technology · 685 words

AThe idea is disarmingly simple. Instead of spreading crops across fields exposed to drought, frost and pests, why not grow them indoors, in stacked layers, under artificial light, in a building that could stand in the middle of a city? The concept was popularised by an American professor in the late 1990s, who asked his students to design a skyscraper that could feed a city’s population. For years it remained largely theoretical. Then, in the 2010s, falling prices for light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and a surge of investment turned it into an industry, with companies building ever larger facilities on several continents.

BMost vertical farms dispense with soil altogether. In hydroponic systems, the roots of the plants sit in water enriched with precisely measured nutrients; in aeroponic systems, they hang in the air and are sprayed with a nutrient mist. Temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide and the colour and intensity of light are all controlled by computer, and sensors monitor the plants continuously. Because the environment is sealed, pests are rare and pesticides are generally unnecessary, and because water is recirculated rather than lost to evaporation or drainage, a vertical farm can use only a small fraction of the water that the same crop would need in a field.

CThe advantages extend beyond resource use. Crops can be grown all year round, regardless of the season or the weather outside, and harvests are highly predictable. Since the farms can be located close to — or inside — the cities they supply, produce can reach shops within hours of being picked, which reduces spoilage and allows growers to select varieties for flavour rather than for their ability to survive long journeys. In regions with little fertile land or water, such as parts of the Middle East, the appeal is obvious.

DYet the list of crops actually grown in vertical farms is strikingly short. The great majority produce leafy greens such as lettuce, spinach and kale, together with herbs such as basil. The reason is economic. These plants grow quickly, are mostly edible, and command a relatively high price per kilogram, so the high cost of each square metre of growing space can be recovered. Staple crops present a very different calculation. Wheat, rice and maize require large amounts of light to produce grain that sells for very little; a field of wheat receives its sunlight free of charge, whereas an indoor farm must pay for every photon. Analysts who have attempted the calculation conclude that growing staple grains indoors would be prohibitively expensive at current energy prices.

EEnergy, in fact, is the industry’s central weakness. Replacing the sun is costly, and a large vertical farm can consume as much electricity as a small town. When energy prices rose sharply in the early 2020s, the consequences were severe: several well-funded companies scaled back their operations or went out of business altogether. Critics also point out that unless the electricity comes from low-carbon sources, the emissions associated with lighting a vertical farm may outweigh the savings from shorter transport, particularly since transport accounts for a relatively modest share of the emissions associated with most foods.

FSupporters respond that the industry is still young and that costs will fall as it matures. LEDs continue to become more efficient, automation is reducing the need for labour, and plant scientists are developing varieties specifically suited to indoor conditions — compact plants that need less light and can be harvested sooner. Some farms are being built next to sources of cheap renewable or waste energy, and others are experimenting with crops of higher value, such as strawberries, or with plants grown to produce medicines.

GA realistic assessment probably lies somewhere between the early enthusiasm and the recent pessimism. Vertical farms are unlikely ever to feed the world’s cities with bread or rice. They can, however, supply fresh, perishable produce reliably in places where conventional farming is difficult, and they may prove valuable as one component of a more diverse and resilient food system. The mistake, perhaps, was ever to imagine that a single technology could transform something as vast and varied as global agriculture.

The Protein Problem

Environment · Nutrition · 376 words

Of all the choices that shape the environmental impact of the food system, few matter as much as how we obtain protein. Meat, milk and eggs are rich, convenient sources of it, and as incomes rise around the world, demand for them tends to rise too. Yet livestock farming makes extraordinary demands on the planet’s resources. Taken together, pasture for grazing animals and cropland used to grow their feed account for a very large share of all agricultural land, while cattle in particular release methane, a greenhouse gas far more potent in the short term than carbon dioxide.

The root of the problem is efficiency. An animal uses most of the energy in its feed simply to stay alive — to move, to keep warm and to grow bones, skin and organs that people do not eat. Only a fraction of what it consumes ends up as edible meat. The ratio varies widely: chickens and farmed fish convert feed relatively efficiently, pigs less so, and beef cattle least efficiently of all. In effect, much of the grain grown on the world’s farms is fed to animals rather than to people.

None of this means that livestock has no place in a sustainable food system. Grazing animals can turn grass growing on land unsuitable for crops into food, and in many poorer communities animals provide not only protein but income, insurance and essential micronutrients. The debate is less about whether we should keep animals than about how many, of which kinds, and how they are raised.

Several responses are being pursued at once. Some campaigns encourage consumers in wealthy countries to eat less meat. Researchers are working on feed additives that reduce methane emissions and on more productive breeds. A growing industry produces plant-based imitations of meat, while scientists are attempting to grow meat from animal cells in laboratories, though producing it cheaply at scale has proved difficult. And there is a further, older option that many people find much harder to accept — eating insects.

Each of these approaches has its champions and its critics, and none is likely to solve the problem alone. What they share is a recognition that the world cannot simply multiply its current way of producing protein to meet future demand.

The Case for Eating Insects — and Its Limits
Insects are hailed as the protein of the future. A closer look suggests they may matter most where few people expect.

Reading Passage 3 · Food and environment · 698 words

AFor a large part of the world’s population, the suggestion that insects should be eaten is not a provocative idea but a description of an ordinary meal. A widely cited report by the United Nations’ Food and Agriculture Organization estimated that insects form part of the traditional diet of around two billion people, and that well over a thousand species are eaten in one place or another — from grasshoppers in Mexico and Thailand to caterpillars in parts of southern Africa. It is chiefly in Europe and North America, and in many other places where the habit has died out, that the idea provokes disgust. That disgust, I would suggest, tells us more about culture than about food: few Europeans hesitate to eat prawns, which are, biologically speaking, not so very different.

BThe environmental argument for insects rests above all on efficiency. Because insects are cold-blooded, they do not spend energy keeping their bodies warm, and so they convert feed into body mass far more efficiently than cattle — by most estimates several times more efficiently. A much greater proportion of an insect is edible, too: almost the whole of a cricket can be eaten, compared with well under half of a cow. Insect farming also appears to require far less land and water, and to produce far fewer greenhouse gases, per kilogram of protein than conventional livestock. Nutritionally, many species are rich not only in protein but in fats, fibre and minerals such as iron and zinc.

CThese figures have inspired an enthusiastic literature predicting that insects will soon take their place alongside beef and chicken in Western supermarkets. I am sceptical. Regulators in the European Union have now approved several insect species for human consumption, and products ranging from cricket-flour pasta to mealworm burgers have appeared. Yet sales have remained modest, and a number of start-ups that set out to persuade Western consumers have struggled. Disgust is not easily overcome by statistics, and the most successful products tend to be those in which the insect is ground into an invisible powder — which rather undermines the claim that consumers have embraced insects as food.

DThere are also legitimate concerns that enthusiasts sometimes pass over too quickly. Some people who are allergic to shellfish may also react to insects, which share certain proteins, so clear labelling is essential. The efficiency figures, moreover, depend heavily on what the insects are fed: insects raised on the same grain that could feed chickens offer a much smaller advantage than those raised on waste. And the question of whether insects can suffer, though uncertain, deserves to be taken seriously by an industry that may one day farm them in the trillions.

EWhere insects may matter most, in my view, is not on the human plate at all, but in the feed trough. A large share of the soya and fishmeal fed to farmed fish and poultry is produced at considerable environmental cost — soya plantations are associated with deforestation, and fishmeal depends on catching wild fish. Certain insects, notably the larvae of the black soldier fly, can be raised on food waste and agricultural by-products and then processed into a high-protein feed. Fish and chickens, unlike Western consumers, need no persuading: insects are part of their natural diet. Converting waste into feed in this way addresses two problems at once.

FNone of this makes the case for human consumption worthless. In regions where insects are already eaten, supporting traditional harvesting and developing safe, small-scale farming could improve nutrition and incomes considerably, and that, rather than winning over sceptical Europeans, may be the more important goal. Nor should we assume that Western tastes will never change: sushi was once regarded with suspicion in many countries where it is now commonplace.

GThe danger lies in expecting too much. Insects will not, on their own, solve the problems created by the world’s growing appetite for meat; no single food can. But as a feed that turns waste into protein, as a traditional food worth protecting, and perhaps eventually as an ingredient in Western diets, they deserve a far more serious place in the conversation than the jokes and dares that have usually accompanied them.

Model answer — Jasur (Band 8.5)

The tradition I’d like to describe is the preparation of osh — or plov, as it’s known elsewhere — which is far more than a dish in Uzbekistan; it’s practically a social institution. It’s eaten at weddings, at funerals, on Thursdays and Fridays in many families, and at virtually any gathering where a large number of people need to be fed.

What makes it distinctive is the way it’s cooked. Traditionally it’s men who prepare it for big events, often starting before dawn, in an enormous cast-iron pot called a kazan set over an open fire. Having heated the oil until it’s almost smoking, the oshpaz — the cook — fries the meat and onions, adds carrots cut into long thin strips, and then layers the rice on top, leaving it to steam without stirring. Get the timing wrong and the whole thing is ruined, which is why a good oshpaz is genuinely respected.

At weddings there’s a custom called the morning osh, held at dawn for the men of the neighbourhood, and what always strikes me is that hundreds of people can be fed within an hour, with remarkably little fuss.

As for why it matters, I’d say it’s because osh embodies hospitality. Not only does it feed people, but it signals that a family is ready to share whatever it has. Sitting around a shared plate also flattens social differences to some extent — the neighbour and the visiting official eat from the same dish. So in a sense, it’s less about the food itself than about what sharing it represents.

Pronunciation — Weak Forms and Contrastive Stress

Natural, fluent English (Band 8 for Pronunciation) uses weak forms for grammar words and contrastive stress to highlight the key idea. Overusing strong forms makes speech sound robotic; misplacing stress can change the meaning.

Weak forms: unstressed of, to, for, and, can, was, than, a, the are reduced to /ə/: a cup of tea → /ə kʌp əv tiː/. They become strong when stressed for contrast or at the end of a sentence: “What’s it made of?” /ɒv/.
