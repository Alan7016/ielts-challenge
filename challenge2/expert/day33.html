The Case for Free Public Transport
Fare-free buses and trains promise cleaner, fairer cities. The evidence is more complicated.

Catch-up reading · Cities and policy · 476 words

AIn March 2020, Luxembourg became the first country in the world to make all public transport free — buses, trams and trains — for residents and visitors alike. Tallinn, the capital of Estonia, had already abolished fares for registered residents in 2013, and dozens of smaller towns in France, Poland and the United States have followed similar paths. Supporters present fare-free transport as a simple way to reduce traffic, cut emissions and make cities fairer. The evidence, however, suggests that the picture is considerably more complicated.

BThe most consistent finding is that removing fares does increase passenger numbers. In Tallinn, public transport use rose by around fourteen per cent in the first year. The difficulty lies in where these new passengers come from. Several studies have found that a large share were not former drivers but people who had previously walked or cycled. Shifting pedestrians onto buses brings little environmental benefit and may even reduce the amount of physical exercise people take.

CCar use, by contrast, has often proved stubborn. In Tallinn, the number of car journeys fell only slightly, and in some cities it barely changed at all. Researchers explain this by pointing out that price is rarely the main reason why drivers choose their cars. Journey time, comfort, reliability and the ability to travel directly from door to door matter far more to most motorists than the cost of a ticket.

DThere is also the question of who pays. Fares typically cover between a third and a half of the operating costs of public transport in Europe. When they are abolished, the money must come from elsewhere — usually local or national taxes. In Luxembourg, a wealthy country where fares had in any case been low, the lost income amounted to roughly forty million euros a year, a manageable sum. For larger and poorer cities, the cost could be far harder to absorb, and could lead to reduced investment in services.

EIn my view, the strongest case for fare-free transport is not environmental but social. For low-income households, transport costs can consume a significant share of the budget and limit access to jobs, education and healthcare. Removing fares, or offering free travel to specific groups such as students and the unemployed, can make a real difference to people’s lives. Dunkirk in France, which abolished fares in 2018, reported that many passengers used buses to reach places they had previously been unable to visit.

FNevertheless, I would argue that cities seeking to reduce car use should treat free fares as, at most, one element of a broader strategy. Frequent, reliable services, dedicated bus lanes and restrictions on parking and car access in city centres appear to have a much greater effect on how people travel. A free bus that arrives late and is stuck in traffic is still not a serious alternative to the car.

Why Do We Forget Our Dreams?

Extra reading 1 · Science · 263 words

AMost people dream several times every night, yet by the time they have finished breakfast, they usually remember little or nothing of what they experienced. Researchers estimate that we forget around ninety-five per cent of our dreams. Why should something that feels so vivid while it is happening disappear so quickly?

BOne explanation involves a chemical called noradrenaline, which plays an important role in forming memories. During the stage of sleep in which most vivid dreams occur, known as REM sleep, levels of noradrenaline in the brain are extremely low. Without it, the brain may simply fail to store the experience in long-term memory.

CAnother factor is timing. Studies have found that people who wake up during or immediately after a dream are far more likely to remember it than those who sleep on. People who often recall their dreams also tend to wake up more frequently during the night, although these periods are usually too short for them to notice.

DThe content of the dream matters as well. Dreams that are emotional, strange or frightening are remembered more often than ordinary ones, perhaps because emotional experiences are more easily stored in memory in general.

EFor those who would like to remember more of their dreams, sleep researchers offer simple advice. Keeping a notebook beside the bed and writing down anything you remember as soon as you wake — before checking your phone or getting up — can significantly increase recall within a couple of weeks. Even a single word or image can be enough to bring back a surprising amount of detail.

Extra Reading 2 — The Rise of Urban Farming

The second extra article. Decide TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN, then summarise it in writing and aloud.

The Rise of Urban Farming

Extra reading 2 · Food and cities · 260 words

AGrowing food in cities is not new — during the Second World War, ‘victory gardens’ in Britain and the United States produced a significant proportion of the vegetables eaten at home. But in recent decades, urban agriculture has taken new and more technological forms, from rooftop greenhouses to ‘vertical farms’ in which crops are grown in stacked layers under artificial light.

BVertical farms make bold claims. Because plants are grown indoors in carefully controlled conditions, they need no pesticides, can be harvested all year round and use up to ninety-five per cent less water than conventional fields. Crops can also be grown close to the people who eat them, reducing the distance food has to travel.

CThe main problem is energy. Replacing sunlight with LED lighting requires enormous amounts of electricity, and when energy prices rose sharply in 2022, several high-profile vertical farming companies in Europe and the United States went out of business. At present, the technology is economically viable mainly for high-value, fast-growing crops such as salad leaves and herbs; growing staple foods such as wheat or rice indoors remains far too expensive.

DCommunity gardens offer a lower-tech alternative. Their contribution to a city’s total food supply is small, but researchers have found that they bring other benefits, including stronger social connections between neighbours, better diets among participants and cooler temperatures in surrounding streets.

EUrban farming, then, is unlikely to feed entire cities. Its value may lie less in the quantity of food it produces than in the way it reconnects city dwellers with how food is grown.

Introduction: Several cities, and even one country, have made public transport free, arguing that it reduces traffic and helps people on low incomes. I agree with this policy only in part: free travel for those who need it most is clearly justified, but abolishing fares for everyone is an expensive and surprisingly ineffective way of changing how people travel.

Body: The central weakness of universal free fares is that they do little to persuade drivers to leave their cars. This is because motorists choose cars mainly for speed, comfort and convenience rather than cost. At the same time, fares usually cover a third to a half of operating costs, so abolishing them diverts money that could otherwise fund more frequent services or dedicated bus lanes — measures that appear to have a far greater effect on travel behaviour.
