Multitasking: Wonders or Perils?

A. In the modern workplace, the ability to multitask is often worn as a badge of honour. Job advertisements routinely list it as a desirable skill, and many professionals pride themselves on juggling emails, phone calls, and reports simultaneously. This cultural celebration of multitasking rests on an appealing but largely mistaken assumption: that the human brain can process several demanding tasks at once without any loss of quality or speed. Decades of cognitive research suggest otherwise, and the gap between popular belief and scientific evidence on this point is wider than almost any other area of everyday psychology.

B. What we call multitasking is, in almost every case, not simultaneous processing at all but rapid switching between tasks. The brain does not run two demanding cognitive processes in parallel; instead, it toggles its attention back and forth, disengaging from one task and re-engaging with another. Each switch carries what psychologists term a "switching cost": a brief lag during which performance dips as the brain reconfigures itself for the new task. Individually, these costs seem trivial, often lasting only a fraction of a second. Accumulated across a working day punctuated by constant interruptions, however, they can amount to a substantial and measurable loss of productive time — by some estimates, as much as two hours out of an eight-hour day.

C. Compounding this problem is a phenomenon researchers call attention residue. When a person shifts from one task to another without fully completing the first, a portion of their attention remains tethered to it. Someone who leaves an unfinished email to answer a colleague's question, for instance, does not bring their full cognitive resources to that conversation; some fraction of their mind is still composing the email. This residue degrades performance on the second task and, counterintuitively, on the return to the first one as well, since the brain must now re-establish context that has partially decayed in the meantime.

D. The practical consequences extend well beyond office productivity. Studies of drivers using mobile phones, even hands-free ones, have repeatedly found reaction times comparable to those of drivers who are legally intoxicated. In hospital settings, interruptions during medication administration have been linked to measurable increases in dosing errors. Even in less safety-critical contexts, workers who are frequently interrupted report higher stress and take significantly longer to complete tasks than those permitted uninterrupted blocks of time, despite often perceiving themselves, quite wrongly, as more productive for having stayed constantly busy.

E. None of this means multitasking is universally harmful. Highly automatic tasks — walking while talking, for example — genuinely can be performed alongside a demanding cognitive task, precisely because they require minimal conscious attention. The danger lies in the conflation of automatic and effortful tasks: the belief that because we can walk and talk simultaneously, we can equally well write a report and participate in a meeting. The research is fairly consistent on this point: for tasks that require genuine thought, the fastest and most accurate route to completion is very rarely to do several of them at once.

Making natural dyes

Natural dyes made from plant material produce much softer colours than commercial dyes and contain no chemicals. If you want to make blue dye, for example, you need to cut up red cabbage and boil it in water for 30–40 minutes. Let mixture stand overnight, then boil it again and remove plant material.

Before you dye item of clothing, it is important to check fixing process, which ensures that the colour will not run. most common fixers are lemon juice and vinegar. They also need to be boiled with your garment as part of dyeing process.

Once you have done this, you should put garment into stainless steel pot with dye and simmer two together for 30 to 40 minutes until you get right colour. more you stir during this time, the better your garment will fix.
