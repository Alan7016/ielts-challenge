Reading — Reading the Rocks

A full IELTS-length passage linking this week's themes: fossils, ocean chemistry and deep time. Allow 20 minutes for questions 1–17.

Reading the Rocks: What Past Extinctions Reveal About Today's Oceans

Earth science · 442 words

AAround 252 million years ago, at the end of the Permian period, life on Earth came closer to being wiped out than at any other time. More than 80 per cent of marine species disappeared, along with a large proportion of life on land. For decades, the cause of this "Great Dying" was fiercely debated. Today, a broad consensus has emerged — and it carries an uncomfortable message for the present.

BThe evidence comes largely from rocks laid down on the ancient sea floor. As layers of sediment accumulate, they preserve not only fossils but also chemical traces of the conditions in which they formed. By analysing the ratios of particular elements in these layers, geochemists can reconstruct changes in ocean temperature, oxygen levels and acidity with remarkable precision. Such techniques have shown that the extinction coincided with massive volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia, which released enormous quantities of carbon dioxide over tens of thousands of years.

CThe consequences were not limited to warming. As the oceans absorbed carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, they became more acidic, making it harder for organisms such as corals and shellfish to build their calcium carbonate skeletons. Strikingly, the fossil record shows that these creatures suffered disproportionately: species with heavy carbonate shells were far more likely to vanish than those with other kinds of skeleton. Warmer water also holds less oxygen, and large areas of the ocean appear to have become almost lifeless.

DIt would be misleading to draw a simple parallel with today. The Permian eruptions released carbon over a much longer period than the two centuries of industrial emissions so far, and the planet's geography and ecosystems were entirely different. Yet the comparison is instructive in one crucial respect. The rate at which humans are currently adding carbon dioxide to the atmosphere appears to be considerably faster than during the Permian event. It is the speed of change, many scientists argue, rather than the absolute amount, that determines whether organisms can adapt.

ERecovery, when it eventually came, was extraordinarily slow. Reefs disappeared from the fossil record for several million years, and it took perhaps ten million years for marine ecosystems to regain their former diversity. In the blink of an eye in geological terms — but an almost unimaginable span from a human perspective.

FNone of this means that a modern mass extinction is inevitable. Unlike the volcanoes of Siberia, human emissions can be reduced, and protected areas can give vulnerable species a refuge. What the rocks suggest is not a forecast but a warning: that the Earth's systems, once pushed far enough, may take far longer to repair than to damage.

There are a lot of good things about protecting the ocean. Firstly, the ocean is very important for people because lots of people eat fish. Also, if the ocean gets more acidic, a lot of animals will die and this is very bad. Moreover, protecting the ocean is good for tourism. So protecting the ocean is a good idea.

Protecting marine ecosystems brings considerable economic as well as environmental benefits. Billions of people depend on fish as a primary source of protein, and this dependence makes the health of the oceans a matter of food security rather than merely of conservation. Ocean acidification poses a particular threat in this respect, since it weakens the shells and skeletons of species at the base of marine food chains; the Permian extinction, in which organisms with heavy carbonate shells were disproportionately wiped out, shows how severe such effects can become. Healthy reefs also sustain lucrative tourism industries, giving coastal communities a direct financial incentive to safeguard them.
