Whose Heritage? The Debate Over Returning Museum Objects

Culture · Ethics · History · 998 words

AIn 1869, a manuscript that had been kept for centuries in a mosque in Samarkand was acquired by the Russian imperial authorities and sent to the Imperial Public Library in St Petersburg. The manuscript, a very early copy of the Qur'an traditionally associated with the Caliph Uthman, remained there for almost half a century. After the 1917 revolution it was handed over to Muslim representatives and taken first to Ufa, and in 1924 it was returned to Central Asia. Today it is kept in Tashkent, where it is one of the most important objects in the country's religious and cultural life. Its journey, from Samarkand to St Petersburg and back, anticipated a debate that has since become one of the most contentious in the museum world.

BThat debate concerns the vast numbers of objects held in museums in Europe and North America that were taken from other parts of the world, frequently during periods of conquest or colonial rule. The scale is considerable. A report commissioned by the French president in 2018 and written by the scholars Felwine Sarr and Bénédicte Savoy estimated that the overwhelming majority of the material cultural heritage of sub-Saharan Africa — perhaps as much as 90 per cent — is held outside the continent. Not all of it was stolen; some objects were bought, exchanged or given as gifts. Yet the line between a purchase and a seizure is not always easy to draw when one party held most of the power.

CThe most widely cited example is that of the Benin Bronzes, thousands of brass plaques and sculptures taken by British forces from the royal palace of the Kingdom of Benin, in what is now Nigeria, during a punitive expedition in 1897. Many were sold to cover the costs of the expedition and are now spread across dozens of institutions. For decades requests for their return were largely ignored. In the past few years, however, the position has shifted with remarkable speed: in 2022 Germany agreed to transfer ownership of the Benin Bronzes in its public collections to Nigeria, and several British institutions, including the Horniman Museum in London, have returned objects of their own.

DOpponents of large-scale return advance several arguments. The first is legal. Many objects were acquired in ways that were lawful at the time, and in some countries museums are forbidden to give them up; the British Museum, for example, is governed by an Act of Parliament that prevents its trustees from deaccessioning objects except in narrow circumstances. The second is practical: some institutions in countries of origin, it is argued, lack the resources to conserve fragile objects. The third, and most ambitious, is philosophical. In 2002, a group of major Western museums signed a declaration defending the idea of the 'universal museum', in which objects from many cultures can be seen side by side by visitors from all over the world.

EEach of these arguments, however, is weaker than it first appears. That an acquisition was legal under the laws of an occupying power does not make it legitimate; laws written by the stronger party are hardly a neutral standard. The conservation argument has an uncomfortable history of its own, having often been used to imply that only Western institutions can be trusted with valuable objects — and it is increasingly contradicted by the opening of well-equipped museums in Africa and Asia. As for the universal museum, critics point out that its universality has always been somewhat one-sided: visitors from London or Paris can see the heritage of Benin with ease, while many people in Benin City would need a visa and an expensive flight to see their own.

FNone of this means that the issue is simple. Some of the most difficult questions arise after a return has been agreed. To whom, exactly, should an object be returned — to a national government, a museum, a community, or the descendants of a ruler from whom it was taken? In 2023, a Nigerian presidential decree recognised the Oba, or traditional king, of Benin as the owner of the returned bronzes, prompting debate about whether they would remain on public display. Returning an object, in other words, does not end the argument about it; it simply relocates it.

GThe case of the Parthenon Marbles illustrates how entrenched positions can become. The sculptures were removed from the Acropolis in Athens in the early nineteenth century by agents of Lord Elgin, a British diplomat, and have been in the British Museum since 1816. Greece has requested their return for decades and, when it opened the new Acropolis Museum in 2009, deliberately left space for them. The British Museum has argued that the sculptures were acquired legally and are best understood within a world collection. Proposals for some kind of long-term loan or sharing arrangement have been discussed, but at the time of writing no agreement has been reached.

HSupporters of the status quo frequently warn that returning even a few objects would set a precedent that could empty the great museums. The evidence so far does not support this fear. Most claims concern a relatively small number of objects of exceptional cultural or religious significance, and many communities have made it clear that they are interested less in removing everything than in being recognised as owners, with the right to decide whether objects are lent, shared or brought home. Meanwhile, careful research into provenance — establishing where an object came from and how it was acquired — has become a routine part of museum work.

IThe most constructive developments have come from institutions willing to treat the question as a matter of relationships rather than possessions. Joint exhibitions, long-term loans, shared research and digital records can allow objects to be seen widely while their ownership is settled fairly. Whether a museum's role is to own the world's heritage or to act as one of its custodians may ultimately be the question that decides how museums are judged in this century.

The conservation argument has an uncomfortable history of its own, having often been used to imply that only Western institutions can be trusted with valuable objects — and it is increasingly contradicted by the opening of well-equipped museums in Africa and Asia. As for the universal museum, critics point out that its universality has always been somewhat one-sided: visitors from London or Paris can see the heritage of Benin with ease, while many people in Benin City would need a visa and an expensive flight to see their own.

FNone of this means that the issue is simple. Some of the most difficult questions arise after a return has been agreed. To whom, exactly, should an object be returned — to a national government, a museum, a community, or the descendants of a ruler from whom it was taken? In 2023, a Nigerian presidential decree recognised the Oba, or traditional king, of Benin as the owner of the returned bronzes, prompting debate about whether they would remain on public display. Returning an object, in other words, does not end the argument about it; it simply relocates it.

GThe case of the Parthenon Marbles illustrates how entrenched positions can become. The sculptures were removed from the Acropolis in Athens in the early nineteenth century by agents of Lord Elgin, a British diplomat, and have been in the British Museum since 1816. Greece has requested their return for decades and, when it opened the new Acropolis Museum in 2009, deliberately left space for them. The British Museum has argued that the sculptures were acquired legally and are best understood within a world collection. Proposals for some kind of long-term loan or sharing arrangement have been discussed, but at the time of writing no agreement has been reached.

HSupporters of the status quo frequently warn that returning even a few objects would set a precedent that could empty the great museums. The evidence so far does not support this fear. Most claims concern a relatively small number of objects of exceptional cultural or religious significance, and many communities have made it clear that they are interested less in removing everything than in being recognised as owners, with the right to decide whether objects are lent, shared or brought home. Meanwhile, careful research into provenance — establishing where an object came from and how it was acquired — has become a routine part of museum work.

IThe most constructive developments have come from institutions willing to treat the question as a matter of relationships rather than possessions. Joint exhibitions, long-term loans, shared research and digital records can allow objects to be seen widely while their ownership is settled fairly. Whether a museum's role is to own the world's heritage or to act as one of its custodians may ultimately be the question that decides how museums are judged in this century.

Museums in Europe and North America hold countless artefacts that were removed from other parts of the world, often during wars or under colonial administrations, and there are growing calls for their repatriation. I largely agree that such objects should be returned, particularly where they were taken by force; however, where items were genuinely purchased, more flexible arrangements such as shared ownership may be more appropriate.

The most compelling argument for restitution concerns the circumstances of acquisition. Many of the most contested objects were seized during military campaigns, when their owners had no meaningful ability to refuse. The Benin Bronzes, for instance, were taken during a punitive expedition in 1897 and sold to recover its costs. Museums sometimes respond that such acquisitions were lawful at the time, yet this argument confuses legality with legitimacy, since the relevant laws were drawn up by the very powers that benefited from them.

Return can also restore access and cultural identity. Advocates of the "universal museum" claim that displaying the heritage of many civilisations together benefits a global audience. While such collections undoubtedly have educational value, their accessibility has been distinctly one-sided: a visitor from London can view the bronzes with ease, whereas many Nigerians would need a visa and an expensive flight to see their own history. The Samarkand manuscript, now one of the most significant objects in Uzbekistan's cultural life, shows what an object can mean when it is back among the people for whom it was made.

Critics warn that returning even a few items would set a precedent capable of emptying the great museums. This fear, however, appears exaggerated. Most claims concern a relatively small number of objects of exceptional significance, and many communities seek recognition of ownership rather than the wholesale removal of collections, which leaves room for loans and joint exhibitions.

In conclusion, objects acquired under duress should, in my view, be returned, while cases of legitimate purchase can be resolved through partnership.
