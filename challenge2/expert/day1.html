Task 2 of 6

Article — Oxford: Nine Centuries of Learning

Read the article. The highlighted phrases are defined below it.

Nestled among spires and cobbled lanes, the University of Oxford has earned a reputation as one of the most prestigious seats of learning in the world. Its origins are famously difficult to pin down; there is no single founding date, no ceremonial ribbon-cutting to mark its birth. Instead, historians agree that some form of teaching existed in Oxford as early as 1096, making it the oldest university in the English-speaking world, though its exact beginnings remain shrouded in mystery.

The university's growth accelerated dramatically in 1167, when King Henry II banned English students from attending the University of Paris, effectively forcing scholars back home. Oxford absorbed this sudden influx of academic talent, and within decades it had become firmly established as a centre of intellectual life. By the 13th century, the first residential colleges — University College, Balliol, and Merton — had been founded, laying the groundwork for the distinctive collegiate system that still defines Oxford today.

Rather than being a single, unified institution in the way many universities are, Oxford is best understood as a federation: a central university that awards degrees and sets academic standards, surrounded by dozens of semi-autonomous colleges, each with its own history, traditions, and quirks. Every student belongs to a college as well as to the university as a whole, and it is within these smaller communities that much of Oxford's celebrated tutorial system takes place — a method of teaching in which students meet with an academic, often one-on-one or in very small groups, to defend and refine their ideas through rigorous discussion.

Oxford's history has not been without turbulence. Relations between the university's scholars — collectively known as "gown" — and the townspeople of Oxford, known as "town," were frequently strained in the medieval period, occasionally boiling over into violence. The most notorious episode, the St Scholastica Day riot of 1355, began as a dispute in a tavern and escalated into two days of street fighting that left dozens dead. The event was so significant that the mayor of Oxford was required to publicly apologise to the university every year for centuries afterwards.

Change, when it came to Oxford, often arrived slowly. Women were permitted to attend lectures from the late 19th century, but it was not until 1920 that they were formally allowed to receive degrees on equal terms with men — a reform that lagged notably behind several other British universities. Today, women make up roughly half of the student population, a transformation that would have seemed unthinkable to the university's medieval founders.

Central to Oxford's identity is the Bodleian Library, one of the oldest libraries in Europe, whose collection has grown so vast that it now occupies numerous buildings across the city and beyond. Under a centuries-old agreement, the Bodleian is entitled to request a free copy of every book published in the United Kingdom, a privilege that has helped it amass millions of volumes over the years.

Perhaps nothing illustrates Oxford's global reach better than the list of its former students. Alumni of the university include several dozen Nobel laureates, numerous heads of state, and a striking number of British prime ministers — a legacy that has led critics and admirers alike to debate the university's outsized influence on public life. The Rhodes Scholarship, established in 1902, further cemented Oxford's international pull, bringing outstanding students from around the world to study there each year, a tradition that continues into the present day.

For all its ancient rituals — the subfusc academic dress still worn for examinations, the formal Latin graduation ceremonies, the candlelit dinners in wood-panelled halls — Oxford has continually reinvented itself to remain at the forefront of research and scholarship. It is this rare combination of deep tradition and constant renewal that continues to draw students, scholars, and visitors from every corner of the globe.

Language from the passage

Here are ten sophisticated phrases taken directly from "The MIT Factor." Study each one, then use as many as you naturally can in the writing task below.

1. precious little — very little; almost none.
"...there's precious little going on that you would normally see on a university campus."
New example: There's precious little evidence to support the claim.

2. push at the boundaries of — to challenge or extend the limits of something.
"Machover's passion for pushing at the boundaries of the existing world..."
New example: The artist is known for pushing at the boundaries of what's considered acceptable in modern art.

3. unleash (someone's) potential — to release or allow the full development of an ability.
"...to extend and unleash human potential..."
New example: The right teacher can unleash a student's potential in ways a textbook never could.

4. take (something) for granted — to fail to appreciate something because it is so familiar.
"...the common everyday objects that we now all take for granted."
New example: It's easy to take clean drinking water for granted until it isn't available.

5. at a premium — highly valued, especially because it is scarce.
"Knowledge was at a premium, but it had to be useful."
New example: In a fast-moving industry, up-to-date skills are at a premium.

6. enshrined in — formally and permanently established in something.
"This down-to-earth quality is enshrined in the school motto..."
New example: The right to a fair trial is enshrined in the country's constitution.

7. work in an iterative way — to make progress through repeated, small, incremental steps.
"I like to take on things... rather than to work in an iterative way just making small steps forward."
New example: Good software design usually happens in an iterative way, with constant small improvements.

8. take (something) to market — to launch or introduce a product for sale.
"...he might be too late in taking his concept to market."
New example: The company rushed the product to market before it was fully tested.

9. go on to (achieve/become) — to describe what someone does next or subsequently achieves.
"...he might become one of those who go on to succeed in spectacular fashion."
New example: Many graduates from the programme go on to become leaders in their field.

10. forge ahead with — to continue determinedly with a plan or project.
"...it is also forging ahead with alternative energies from solar to wind and geothermal..."
New example: Despite the setbacks, the team decided to forge ahead with the original plan.
