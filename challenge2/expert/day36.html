Why Do Languages Change?
Every living language is constantly changing. Linguists have long debated why.

Reading Passage 1 · Linguistics · 396 words

AA speaker of modern English who travelled back a thousand years would struggle to understand a single sentence. Old English, spoken before the Norman Conquest of 1066, had a grammar closer to modern German, with nouns that changed their endings according to their role in the sentence. Even Shakespeare’s English, only four centuries old, contains words and constructions that today’s readers find puzzling. Change, it seems, is an inevitable feature of every living language.

BFor centuries, such change was regarded as decay. Writers in the eighteenth century, such as Jonathan Swift, called for an academy to ‘fix’ the English language and prevent it from being corrupted. Similar institutions already existed in other countries: the Académie française had been established in 1635 to protect the purity of French. Yet despite its efforts, French has continued to change, and English, which never had such an academy, is not noticeably more ‘disordered’ than French.

CLinguists today regard change as neither good nor bad, simply natural, and they have identified several mechanisms that drive it. One is the principle of least effort: speakers tend to simplify pronunciation, so that, for example, the ‘k’ in ‘knight’ and ‘knee’, once pronounced, has disappeared. Another is analogy, in which irregular forms are replaced by regular ones — the past tense of ‘help’ was once ‘holp’, but it has since become ‘helped’, following the regular pattern.

DContact between languages is another powerful force. After 1066, French became the language of the English ruling class, and thousands of French words entered English, particularly in law, government and food. This explains why English has pairs such as ‘cow’ (from Old English) and ‘beef’ (from French): the Anglo-Saxon farmers raised the animals, while the French-speaking nobility ate the meat.

ESocial factors also matter. The sociolinguist William Labov, studying speech on the island of Martha’s Vineyard in the 1960s, found that some young residents were exaggerating a traditional local pronunciation, apparently to distinguish themselves from the summer tourists. His research showed that change is often led by particular social groups — frequently young women — and spreads as others adopt it to signal identity.

FMany people today worry that technology, especially texting and social media, is damaging language. Yet research suggests that young people switch between informal digital writing and formal writing with considerable skill. If anything, the internet has made language change more visible, not necessarily faster.

The Great Stink
How a hot summer in London changed the way cities deal with waste.

Reading Passage 2 · History and engineering · 410 words

AIn the summer of 1858, an unusually hot spell turned the River Thames in London into an open sewer. For decades, the city’s growing population had flushed its waste directly into the river, from which much of its drinking water was also taken. As temperatures rose, the smell became so overpowering that the newspapers named it ‘the Great Stink’. Curtains in the Houses of Parliament were soaked in chemicals in an attempt to block out the odour.

BThe crisis was not only unpleasant but deadly. London had suffered repeated outbreaks of cholera, including one in 1854 that killed more than 600 people in the Soho district alone. At the time, most doctors believed the disease was spread by ‘miasma’, or bad air. The physician John Snow had argued otherwise: by mapping cases in Soho, he showed that they clustered around a single water pump in Broad Street, and he persuaded local officials to remove its handle. Yet his theory that cholera was carried by contaminated water was not widely accepted until years later.

CIronically, it was the belief in miasma that finally prompted action. Fearing that the smell itself was dangerous, Parliament passed legislation within weeks and gave the engineer Joseph Bazalgette the funds to build a new sewer system. His plan was to intercept waste before it reached the river and carry it to outfalls east of the city.

DThe scale of the project was immense. Over the next sixteen years, workers built around 2,100 kilometres of street sewers and 132 kilometres of main intercepting sewers, using more than 300 million bricks. Crucially, Bazalgette made the pipes far wider than his calculations required, reasoning that the city would continue to grow. The new embankments built along the Thames to house the sewers also created new roads and public gardens.

EThe results were dramatic. Cholera largely disappeared from the parts of London served by the system; an outbreak in 1866 struck mainly an eastern district that had not yet been connected. Bazalgette’s generous design meant that the network continued to serve London well into the twenty-first century.

FToday, however, the system is under strain. Because rainwater and sewage share the same pipes, heavy rain causes overflows that release untreated sewage into the Thames dozens of times a year. A new ‘super sewer’, the Thames Tideway Tunnel, 25 kilometres long, was completed in 2025 to capture these overflows — a reminder that infrastructure must keep pace with the cities it serves.

Universal Basic Income
Should every citizen receive a regular payment from the state, with no conditions?

Reading Passage 3 · Economics and society · 432 words

AThe idea of a universal basic income (UBI) — a regular cash payment made by the state to every adult, regardless of income or employment — is not new. Thomas Paine proposed a version of it in 1797, and in the twentieth century it attracted supporters as different as the civil rights leader Martin Luther King and the free-market economist Milton Friedman. In recent years, the prospect of automation replacing jobs has brought the idea back into public debate.

BAdvocates make several arguments. A basic income would provide security in an economy where work is increasingly insecure. Because it is paid to everyone, it would avoid the stigma and complexity of conditional welfare systems, which require claimants to prove that they are looking for work and can trap them in poverty: a person who takes a low-paid job may lose more in benefits than they gain in wages. A UBI, by contrast, is never withdrawn, so work always pays.

CCritics raise equally serious objections. The most obvious is cost. A payment large enough to live on, given to every adult, would require a substantial increase in taxation. Some economists fear that it could also be inflationary, or that it might reduce the incentive to work. Others object on principle, arguing that it is unfair to give money to the wealthy, who do not need it, and that targeted support for those in need is a more efficient use of public funds.

DExperiments have produced interesting but limited evidence. In Finland, a two-year trial from 2017 gave 2,000 unemployed people a monthly payment of €560 with no conditions. Participants reported better wellbeing and less stress, but their employment was only slightly higher than that of a control group. In Kenya, the charity GiveDirectly began a twelve-year programme in 2017 providing payments to thousands of villagers; early results suggest that recipients invested in businesses and livestock rather than spending the money on alcohol, as some had predicted.

EHowever, these experiments have important limitations. Most were temporary, so participants knew the payments would end, and most involved only a small group rather than an entire population. A true UBI would need to be permanent and universal, and its effects on prices, taxes and the wider economy cannot be tested in a small trial.

FIn my view, the strongest case for a basic income is not that it would end poverty on its own, but that it would replace a welfare system which too often punishes people for working. Whether societies are willing to pay for it is, ultimately, a political rather than an economic question.

How Long Does It Take to Form a Habit?

Extra reading 1 · Psychology · 216 words

AIt is often claimed that it takes twenty-one days to form a new habit. The figure appears to originate in the 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics by Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon, who observed that patients took ‘a minimum of about 21 days’ to adjust to changes in their appearance. Over time, ‘a minimum of’ disappeared from popular retellings.

BResearch suggests the reality is more varied. In a 2009 study at University College London, Phillippa Lally and colleagues asked 96 volunteers to adopt a new daily behaviour, such as drinking water after breakfast or running before dinner, and to report how automatic it felt. On average, it took 66 days for the behaviour to become automatic, but the range was enormous — from 18 to 254 days. Simpler behaviours became habits faster than more demanding ones.

CEncouragingly, missing a single day did not significantly affect the process. What mattered most was consistency in the same context: performing the behaviour at the same time and place, after the same cue. Psychologists call this ‘context-dependent repetition’.

DSome researchers recommend ‘habit stacking’ — attaching a new behaviour to an existing routine, such as doing stretching exercises while the kettle boils. Others emphasise reducing friction: making the desired behaviour as easy as possible, for example by leaving running shoes by the door.

3. Some participants took longer than eight months to form a habit.

4. Morning behaviours became habits faster than evening ones.

5. Missing one day meant participants had to start again.

6. Leaving running shoes by the door is an example of reducing friction.

The Story of the Pencil

Speed reading · 280 words · 186 words

AFor most of history, people wrote with brushes, quills or pieces of charcoal. BThe Borrowdale mine was so valuable that it was guarded, and graphite was sometimes smuggled. CIn the nineteenth century, pencil factories grew in Germany and the United States. Today, more than fourteen billion pencils are produced every year.

The idea of paying every adult a regular income, whether or not they work, has moved from the margins of political debate towards the mainstream. I agree with the principle to a considerable extent, although I believe that only a modest, carefully funded version would be workable.

The strongest argument for a basic income is that it would remove the perverse incentives built into conditional welfare systems. At present, an unemployed person who accepts a low-paid or part-time job may lose more in benefits than they gain in wages, which effectively penalises them for working. A universal payment, by contrast, is never withdrawn, so any additional work always increases income. It would also spare claimants the stigma and bureaucracy of proving that they deserve support. Moreover, as automation makes employment less secure, a guaranteed income could provide a stable foundation from which people retrain or start businesses — a pattern suggested by early results in Kenya.

Nevertheless, the objections cannot be dismissed. A payment large enough to live on would be extremely expensive, and it seems wasteful to give public money to those who do not need it. These concerns, however, are less decisive than they first appear. Much of the cost could be recovered from wealthier citizens through progressive taxation, so that in practice the payment would benefit mainly those on lower incomes. A modest initial payment, replacing rather than adding to existing benefits, would also limit the burden on public finances.

In conclusion, while a full income that allowed people to live without working is probably unaffordable, I strongly support a basic payment that replaces conditional benefits, because it would reward work rather than punish it.
