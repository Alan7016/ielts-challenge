Reading Passage 1 — The Antikythera Mechanism

The test starts now. About 18 minutes for Questions 1–13.

The Antikythera Mechanism
An ancient Greek device that rewrote the history of technology.

Reading Passage 1 · History of science · 388 words

AIn 1901, sponge divers working off the Greek island of Antikythera recovered a collection of treasures from a Roman-era shipwreck: bronze and marble statues, glassware and coins. Among them was an unremarkable lump of corroded bronze and wood, roughly the size of a shoebox. Only months later did an archaeologist notice that it contained a gearwheel, and it would take more than a century to understand what it was.

BThe first serious study was carried out by the British historian of science Derek de Solla Price, who from the 1950s used X-ray images to examine the fragments. In his 1974 book Gears from the Greeks, he argued that the device was a mechanical calculator for astronomical positions. Many scholars were sceptical: nothing so complex was thought to have existed before the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe, more than a thousand years later.

CDecisive evidence came in 2005, when the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project used high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scanning to see inside the fragments. The scans revealed around thirty surviving bronze gears and thousands of characters of previously hidden inscriptions, which functioned as a kind of user’s manual.

DTogether, the gears and inscriptions show that the mechanism was operated by turning a handle. Dials on the front displayed the positions of the Sun and Moon in the zodiac and the phase of the Moon, while dials on the back predicted solar and lunar eclipses. One small dial even tracked the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games, including the Olympic Games.

EThe mechanism is generally dated to between the second and first centuries BC, and the ship is thought to have sunk around 60 BC. Who made it remains unknown. Inscriptions and the design of its calendar have led some researchers to link it with Corinth or its colonies, possibly including Syracuse, the home of Archimedes, though there is no direct evidence that he was involved.

FNot all of the mechanism survives, and the display of the five planets known in antiquity was lost. In 2021, a team at University College London led by Tony Freeth published a proposed reconstruction of this planetary display, consistent with the inscriptions and the surviving gears. Whether or not every detail is correct, the device demonstrates a level of mechanical sophistication that forces us to reconsider what the ancient world was capable of.

Reading Passage 2 — The Hidden Organ

About 20 minutes for Questions 14–26.

The Hidden Organ
The microbes living in our gut may influence our health in ways we are only beginning to understand.

Reading Passage 2 · Medicine · 398 words

AThe human gut is home to tens of trillions of microorganisms — bacteria, fungi and viruses — collectively known as the gut microbiome. For decades it was claimed that bacterial cells in the body outnumber human cells by ten to one. In 2016, however, the researchers Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo recalculated the figures and concluded that the ratio is closer to one to one, with around 38 trillion bacteria in a typical adult. Even so, the microbiome contains far more genes than the human genome.

BThese microbes are not passive passengers. They break down dietary fibre that human enzymes cannot digest, producing compounds that nourish the cells of the gut wall; they synthesise certain vitamins; and they help to train the immune system. Some scientists now describe the microbiome as a ‘hidden organ’.

CSome of the most striking evidence has come from experiments on mice raised in completely sterile conditions. In 2006, the team of Jeffrey Gordon at Washington University in St Louis showed that when gut microbes from obese mice were transferred to such ‘germ-free’ mice, the recipients gained more fat than mice given microbes from lean animals, even when they ate the same amount of food.

DThe microbiome’s medical potential is clearest in the treatment of infection with Clostridioides difficile, a bacterium that can cause severe diarrhoea, often after antibiotics have wiped out competing microbes. In a 2013 trial in the Netherlands, transplanting microbes from the faeces of healthy donors cured 81% of patients after a single treatment, compared with 31% of those given antibiotics alone. The trial was stopped early because the difference was so large.

EStudies of traditional societies suggest that modern lifestyles may have reduced the diversity of our microbes. The Hadza, hunter-gatherers in Tanzania, have a considerably more diverse microbiome than people in industrialised countries, and their microbes change with the seasons as their diet changes. Researchers believe that processed food, low fibre intake and frequent antibiotic use may all contribute to this loss.

FEnthusiasm should, however, be tempered by caution. Many findings come from mice or from studies that show associations rather than causes, and the evidence for most commercial probiotic products remains weak. Links between the gut and the brain — the so-called ‘gut–brain axis’ — are an exciting area of research, but claims that changing one’s microbes can treat depression or anxiety are, for now, premature.

Reading Passage 3 — Whose Heritage?

About 22 minutes for Questions 27–40.

Whose Heritage?
The debate over returning museum objects to their countries of origin.

Reading Passage 3 · Culture and ethics · 486 words

AThe great museums of Europe and North America hold millions of objects from other parts of the world. Many were acquired legitimately, through purchase, exchange or archaeological excavation conducted with permission. Others, however, were taken during colonial wars or in circumstances that would today be regarded as theft. Over the past decade, the question of whether such objects should be returned — a process known as restitution — has moved from the margins to the centre of museum policy.

BThe most widely discussed case is that of the Benin Bronzes, thousands of sculptures and plaques taken by British forces from the Kingdom of Benin, in what is now Nigeria, during a military expedition in 1897. The objects were later sold and dispersed to museums around the world. In 2022, Germany transferred ownership of more than 1,000 Benin Bronzes in its public collections to Nigeria, and several museums in Britain and the United States have also returned items.

CA turning point came in 2018, when a report commissioned by the French president Emmanuel Macron and written by the scholars Felwine Sarr and Bénédicte Savoy recommended that objects taken from sub-Saharan Africa without consent be returned. France subsequently returned 26 artefacts to the Republic of Benin in 2021, although critics noted that this was a small fraction of the tens of thousands of African objects held in French museums.

DOpponents of large-scale restitution make several arguments. Some point out that ‘universal’ museums allow visitors to see the cultures of the world side by side, encouraging understanding between peoples. Others raise practical concerns about whether returned objects will be adequately conserved. In some countries, legal obstacles also stand in the way: the British Museum, for example, is prevented by the British Museum Act of 1963 from permanently removing objects from its collection except in very limited circumstances, which is why the Parthenon Marbles, taken from Athens in the early nineteenth century, remain in London despite repeated requests from Greece.

ESupporters reply that such arguments are often applied selectively. The concern about conservation, in particular, assumes that institutions in the countries of origin are less capable than those in Europe — an assumption that many find patronising, and one that is contradicted by the construction of major new museums in West Africa. Moreover, they argue, the ‘universal museum’ ideal is difficult to sustain when most of the world’s population cannot obtain visas to visit it.

FIn my view, the strongest case for restitution concerns objects whose provenance shows that they were looted. For these, there is no convincing argument for retention, and the burden should lie with museums to demonstrate legitimate acquisition rather than with claimants to prove theft. This does not mean that every foreign object must be sent home: many were acquired fairly, and long-term loans and shared ownership offer workable alternatives. But it does mean that museums can no longer treat their collections as a closed question.

The Four-Day Working Week

Extra reading 1 · Work · 216 words

AFor most of the past century, the standard working week in many countries has been five days. In recent years, however, a growing number of employers have experimented with a four-day week, in which employees work fewer hours for the same pay.

Their working week was reduced from 40 hours to 35 or 36 hours without any loss of pay. Researchers reported that productivity stayed the same or improved in most workplaces, while workers’ wellbeing increased considerably.

AIn warehouses in cities from Singapore to New Jersey, lettuces, herbs and strawberries grow in stacked trays under pink and blue LED lights. A Short History of the Umbrella

AUmbrellas were first used not against rain but against the sun. CEarly umbrellas were heavy, with frames of wood or whalebone. In 1852 the English manufacturer Samuel Fox introduced a frame with steel ribs, which was lighter and stronger, and the basic design has changed little since. The folding pocket umbrella followed in the twentieth century. Today, around one billion umbrellas are sold every year, and most are made in China.

Many of the world’s leading museums hold objects that originated in other countries, and there are growing calls for these to be returned. I agree with this principle to a considerable extent where objects were taken by force or deception, but I do not believe that every foreign artefact must be repatriated.

The strongest argument for restitution is that many objects were acquired unjustly. The Benin Bronzes, for instance, were seized by British troops in 1897 and later sold to collections around the world. To continue displaying such objects as if their ownership were unproblematic is, in effect, to profit from an injustice. Moreover, for the communities from which they were taken, these objects are not merely works of art but part of their history and identity, which their descendants have a stronger claim to than foreign institutions.

Opponents argue that “universal” museums allow visitors to compare the cultures of the world side by side, and that some countries lack the facilities to conserve fragile objects. Neither argument, however, is decisive. The universal ideal rings hollow when most of the world’s population cannot obtain visas to visit these museums, and the conservation concern is increasingly undermined by the construction of modern museums in countries such as Nigeria.

That said, restitution need not be all-or-nothing. Many objects were bought, exchanged or excavated with permission, and there is no reason why these should be returned. Even for disputed items, arrangements such as long-term loans or shared ownership can allow objects to be seen in both places.

In conclusion, I believe museums have a clear obligation to return objects that were looted, while those acquired legitimately may justifiably remain, ideally as part of a more equal partnership between institutions.
