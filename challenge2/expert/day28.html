The Unseen Workforce
Most of the world’s flowering plants depend on animals to reproduce. What happens when those animals start to disappear?

Reading Passage 1 · Ecology and agriculture · 673 words

AIn a modern greenhouse, rows of tomato plants grow under carefully controlled conditions, yet they still face an ancient problem. A tomato flower holds its pollen tightly inside tube-like structures and releases it only when it is shaken vigorously. For decades, commercial growers attempted to do this themselves, walking along the rows with electric vibrating wands and touching each flower in turn. The work was slow, costly and never entirely effective. The solution, when it came, was to introduce bumblebees, which grip the flower and vibrate their flight muscles at high frequency — a technique known as buzz pollination — shaking the pollen free in a fraction of a second.

BThe partnership between flowers and the animals that carry their pollen is extraordinarily old. Fossil evidence suggests that beetles were among the earliest pollinators, visiting plants long before bees evolved. Over many millions of years, plants and animals have shaped one another: some flowers produce scents that attract night-flying moths, others have long tubes that only hummingbirds can reach, and a few rely on bats that feed on nectar after dark. Estimates of the number of animal species involved in pollination run well into the hundreds of thousands, most of them insects.

CAmong all these species, modern agriculture has come to rely heavily on one: the western honeybee. Its appeal to farmers is easy to understand. Honeybees live in large colonies that can be managed, fed and moved, and they will visit an enormous range of crops. Every spring, huge numbers of hives are transported by lorry across the United States to the almond orchards of California, which flower at the same time and could not produce a commercial crop without them. In effect, pollination has become a service that farmers rent.

DThis dependence suddenly looked dangerous in the mid-2000s, when beekeepers in North America and Europe began reporting unusually severe losses. In the most puzzling cases, a hive that had seemed healthy weeks earlier was found almost empty. Inspectors typically found a living queen, stored honey and developing young, but very few adult worker bees — and, strangely, few dead bodies nearby. The phenomenon was given the name colony collapse disorder, and it quickly attracted international attention.

EThe search for an explanation proved complicated. Researchers examined parasitic mites that feed on bees and spread viruses, new classes of pesticides that can impair bees’ ability to navigate, and the poor diet that results when bees forage on a single crop for weeks at a time. Most scientists now believe that no single culprit is responsible; rather, several stresses acting together weaken colonies until they can no longer cope. Since then, the dramatic form of collapse has been reported less often, but high annual losses of honeybee colonies remain a serious concern for beekeepers.

FThe crisis also drew attention to pollinators that had largely been taken for granted. There are many thousands of species of wild bee, along with flies, beetles, moths and other insects, and for many crops these wild visitors turn out to be highly effective. A large international study of crop fields found that fruit production increased with visits from wild insects even where honeybees were plentiful, suggesting that honeybees cannot simply replace the services that wild species provide. Yet many wild pollinators are themselves in decline, largely because of the loss of the flowering habitats on which they depend.

GWhat can be done? The measures recommended by scientists are often modest. Strips of wild flowers along field margins, hedgerows left uncut, a reduction in the use of insecticides and a greater variety of crops can all provide food and shelter for wild pollinators. In a few places where pollinators have become scarce, farmers have resorted to pollinating fruit trees by hand, carrying pollen to each blossom with a brush — a vivid reminder of how much labour these animals normally provide without charge. Relying on a single managed species, scientists warn, is a risky strategy; the more varied a farm’s pollinators, the more secure its harvest is likely to be.

Letting Nature Take Over
Rewilding promises to restore damaged landscapes by stepping back. Its critics say it asks too much of the people who live there.

Reading Passage 2 · Conservation · 447 words

AConservation has traditionally been an exercise in careful management: protecting particular species, maintaining habitats in a fixed state and intervening whenever things appear to be going wrong. Rewilding takes a different approach. Its central idea is that, once certain missing elements are restored — a large herbivore here, a predator there, a river allowed to flood — natural processes can be left to shape the landscape with minimal human direction. The goal is not a predetermined picture of nature but a self-sustaining system.

BThe most frequently cited example is the return of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in the mid-1990s. Early accounts described a dramatic chain of effects: wolves reduced the number of elk and changed their behaviour, allowing riverside trees to recover, which in turn benefited beavers and songbirds. More recent research suggests this story was somewhat too neat. Other factors, including the return of bears and changes in climate, also played a part, and in some areas tree recovery has been patchy. In my view, this does not undermine the case for rewilding, but it does warn against promising precise outcomes.

CIn Europe, where landscapes have been shaped by people for thousands of years, rewilding has often taken the form of smaller experiments. On one former farm in southern England, owners who had struggled to make intensive agriculture pay allowed the land to revert, introducing free-roaming cattle, pigs, ponies and deer. Within two decades, scrub and wetland had replaced fields, and several species that had become rare nationally, including the turtle dove and the nightingale, had returned in significant numbers.

DNot everyone welcomes such changes. Farmers worry that predators will attack livestock, and that land taken out of production threatens both their livelihoods and national food supplies. Rural communities sometimes perceive rewilding as a project imposed by urban conservationists who will not bear its costs. These concerns are, I believe, entirely legitimate, and schemes that ignore them deserve to fail.

ESupporters reply that rewilding can create new kinds of income, through nature tourism, carbon storage and payments for services such as flood prevention, and that it is most appropriate on land that produces little food in the first place. Whether these benefits will reach the people who bear the costs, however, depends heavily on how schemes are designed and who controls them.

FRewilding, then, is neither a cure for the biodiversity crisis nor a fashionable threat to rural life. At its best, it offers a way of restoring ecological processes that conventional conservation has struggled to maintain. But it will succeed only if those who live alongside the returning wildlife are partners in the process rather than obstacles to be overcome.

Evolution in the City

Reading Passage 3 · Urban ecology · 388 words

ACities are among the most rapidly changing environments on Earth, and for a long time biologists regarded them as places where wildlife simply struggled to survive. Increasingly, however, researchers are discovering that some species are not merely surviving in urban areas but adapting to them — and, in some cases, evolving.

BSeveral bird species provide striking examples. In a number of European cities, great tits have been recorded singing at a higher pitch than their relatives in nearby forests. The most widely accepted explanation is that low-frequency traffic noise drowns out deeper notes, so birds whose songs carry above the noise are more likely to attract mates and defend territories. Whether these differences are inherited or learned by each generation is still being investigated.

CClearer evidence of genetic change comes from studies of lizards in the Caribbean. Researchers comparing lizards from urban and forest sites found that city lizards had longer limbs and more of the specialised scales on their toes that allow them to cling to surfaces. These features appear to help them run on the smooth walls and painted metal common in towns. When lizards from both habitats were raised under identical conditions, the differences persisted, suggesting that they are inherited rather than simply the result of each animal’s experience.

DMammals have adapted too. Red foxes became established in many British cities during the twentieth century, feeding on waste food and nesting under sheds and in gardens. Urban foxes often live in smaller territories than rural ones, reflecting the abundance of food. In North America, coyotes have colonised even large cities, largely avoiding people by becoming more active at night.

ENot all urban change is positive for wildlife. Artificial light disrupts the behaviour of insects and migrating birds, and roads divide populations, reducing their genetic diversity. Some biologists also warn that species which do well in cities tend to be the same everywhere, so that urban wildlife in different parts of the world is becoming increasingly similar.

FNevertheless, the study of urban evolution has practical value. Because changes in cities happen quickly and close to research institutions, they provide unusual opportunities to observe evolution in action. They may also help planners to design cities — with corridors of vegetation, darker areas at night and fewer barriers — that are better places for both wildlife and people.

The Value of Wetlands

Reading Passage 4 · Environment · 380 words

AFor centuries, wetlands were regarded as wasteland: damp, unproductive and often associated with disease. Across the world, marshes were drained for farmland, mangrove forests were cleared for aquaculture and development, and peat bogs were dug up for fuel. According to one global assessment, roughly a third of the world’s natural wetlands were lost between 1970 and 2015, a rate considerably faster than that of forest loss.

BOnly relatively recently has the true value of these ecosystems been widely recognised. Wetlands act as natural filters, trapping sediment and absorbing excess nutrients before they reach rivers and the sea. They also store water during periods of heavy rain and release it slowly, reducing the risk of flooding downstream and helping to maintain river flows in dry seasons.

CCoastal wetlands provide a further form of protection. Mangrove forests, which grow along tropical coastlines, can reduce the height and energy of waves, offering coastal communities a degree of protection from storms. Studies after major storms have suggested that villages behind intact mangroves often suffered less damage than those where mangroves had been removed, although the extent of protection depends on the width of the forest and the strength of the storm.

DPerhaps the most important function, in an era of climate change, is carbon storage. Peatlands — wetlands in which dead plant material accumulates over thousands of years because waterlogged conditions slow its decay — cover only a small fraction of the Earth’s land surface but are estimated to hold more carbon than all the world’s forests combined. When peatlands are drained, the stored material begins to decompose and releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

EInternational efforts to protect wetlands date back to a treaty signed in the Iranian city of Ramsar in 1971, one of the earliest global environmental agreements. Thousands of sites have since been designated as wetlands of international importance. Designation alone, however, does not guarantee protection, and many listed sites remain under pressure from agriculture, pollution and water extraction.

FRestoration is possible but slow and expensive. Blocking drainage ditches can allow a peat bog to become waterlogged again, and mangroves can be replanted, but recreating the complex structure of a mature wetland may take decades. The cheapest and most effective strategy, conservationists stress, is to protect the wetlands that remain.
