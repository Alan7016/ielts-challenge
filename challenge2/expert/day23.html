The Clock Inside Us

Science · Health · 438 words

Long before humans built mechanical clocks, they carried one inside them. Almost every cell in the human body follows a daily cycle of activity known as a circadian rhythm, from the Latin circa diem, 'about a day'. Body temperature, alertness, hormone levels and even the efficiency of digestion rise and fall in a regular pattern over roughly twenty-four hours. These rhythms are coordinated by a small cluster of nerve cells deep in the brain, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which acts as a master clock and keeps the many smaller clocks in the body's organs in step with one another.

Remarkably, this internal clock keeps running even when people are cut off from daylight. In experiments in which volunteers lived for weeks in caves or windowless rooms, they continued to sleep and wake in a regular cycle — but one that, in most people, was slightly longer than twenty-four hours. Left to itself, in other words, the body clock gradually drifts. What keeps it aligned with the outside world is light. Light entering the eyes, particularly in the morning, resets the master clock each day, while darkness allows the release of melatonin, a hormone that signals to the body that night has arrived.

In 2017 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded for discoveries about the genes and proteins that make this clock tick, research that began with studies of fruit flies. One of the more intriguing implications of the field is that the timing of an activity may matter as well as the activity itself, and there is growing interest in whether some medicines work better, or cause fewer side effects, when taken at particular times of day.

The clock's dependence on light also explains the misery of jet lag. When a traveller crosses several time zones in a few hours, the outside world jumps forward or backward, but the internal clock can shift only gradually — as a rough rule of thumb, by about one time zone a day. Most people find flying east harder than flying west, partly because a clock that naturally runs a little long finds it easier to lengthen the day than to shorten it.

Shift workers face a version of the same problem that never ends, and some research has linked long-term night work with certain health problems. For travellers, the advice is practical: seek daylight at the right time of day at the destination, and the body clock will catch up sooner. The lesson of circadian science is that the body has its own sense of time — and it does not always agree with the clock on the wall.

Railway Time: How Clocks Were Standardised

History · Technology · Society · 991 words

AFor most of human history, time was a local matter. Noon was the moment when the sun stood highest in the sky, and because the sun appears to travel westward, that moment arrives at slightly different times in different places: every degree of longitude corresponds to four minutes. A town lying to the west of another therefore reaches its noon a little later. Each community set its clocks first by a sundial and later by astronomical observation, keeping what came to be known as local mean time. Bristol, more than 150 kilometres west of London, ran about ten minutes behind the capital. For as long as travel was slow, such discrepancies were of no consequence; nobody making a two-day journey by coach from London to Bristol would notice that ten minutes had gone missing along the way.

BThe railways changed this. A train could cover in a few hours a distance that had once taken days, and timetables had to state when trains would depart from and arrive at stations that each kept their own time. The result was confusion and, more seriously, a threat to safety, since the running of trains along shared lines depended on staff at different points agreeing on exactly what time it was. In 1840 the Great Western Railway, whose line linked London with Bristol, began to use London time at all its stations, and other companies followed. In 1847 the Railway Clearing House, a body that coordinated the business of the various companies, recommended that all of them adopt Greenwich Mean Time — the time kept at the Royal Observatory at Greenwich — and within a year or so most British lines had done so.

CAdopting a single time was one thing; keeping thousands of clocks in agreement was another. The solution came from a technology that had grown up alongside the railways: the electric telegraph. From the early 1850s, time signals were sent from Greenwich along telegraph wires, allowing station clocks across the country to be checked against a common standard every day. Time, in effect, had become a kind of information that could be transmitted, rather than something each place read from its own sky.

DNot everyone welcomed the change. Railway time was the time of the station, and for years many towns kept two times at once: their own, which governed the church, the market and the town hall, and London's, which governed the trains. Some public clocks were fitted with two minute hands, one for each. The objections were partly practical — local time still matched the rhythm of daylight — but they were also a matter of identity, since to abandon local time was, for some, to accept that the pace of life was now set in the capital. Legal uncertainty added to the muddle, as it was not always clear which time applied to matters such as the opening hours of public houses or the deadlines of courts. Only in 1880 did Parliament settle the question, making Greenwich Mean Time the legal standard throughout Great Britain.

EIn North America the problem existed on a far larger scale. A continent spanning more than fifty degrees of longitude could not run on a single time, and before 1883 the railways of the United States used dozens of different standards, often based on the local time of a company's headquarters city. Various reformers had proposed dividing the continent into broad zones, each an hour apart. In the end it was the railway companies, rather than the government, that acted. On 18 November 1883 the railways of the United States and Canada switched to a system of standard time zones, and clocks across much of the continent were adjusted on a date that newspapers nicknamed 'the day of two noons'. The federal government did not give the zones legal force until 1918.

FThe railway zones solved a national problem, but they raised an international one: if time was to be measured from a fixed line of longitude, which line should it be? Many countries used their own prime meridian, running through their own capital or observatory. In October 1884, at the invitation of the United States government, delegates from twenty-five nations met in Washington for the International Meridian Conference, where they voted to adopt the meridian passing through Greenwich as the zero of longitude for the world. The choice owed less to sentiment than to practicality: by then most of the world's shipping already used charts based on Greenwich. The vote was not unanimous. San Domingo, now the Dominican Republic, voted against, while France and Brazil abstained, and France continued to use the Paris meridian for its own time until 1911.

GIt is a common misconception that the conference created the world's time zones. In fact, it did nothing of the kind. Its resolutions established a prime meridian and recommended a 'universal day' for purposes such as astronomy and navigation, but they explicitly left countries free to keep whatever local or standard time they wished. The global system of zones measured from Greenwich was adopted country by country over the following decades. Even today a few states use offsets of half an hour or three-quarters of an hour, and some very large countries — China among them — use a single time across territory that spans several natural zones.

HThe standardisation of time is easily seen as a purely technical achievement, but it changed far more than clocks. Once the time in one place could be related precisely to the time everywhere else, it became possible to coordinate trade, communication and, eventually, broadcasting across vast distances. At the same time, it loosened the ancient link between the clock and the sun. In the far west of a wide time zone, the sun may not reach its highest point until well after midday by the clock — a small reminder that the time we live by is a social agreement, not a natural fact.

Has Satellite Navigation Changed the Way We Find Our Way?

Psychology · Technology · 410 words

For most of history, finding the way in an unfamiliar place meant reading the landscape: noticing landmarks, remembering turns, asking strangers for directions or unfolding a paper map. Today, millions of people simply follow a blue dot on a phone screen. Satellite navigation has made getting lost far less common, but some researchers have begun to ask whether it has also changed the way our brains handle space.

Psychologists distinguish between two broad strategies. In a route-based strategy, a person learns a sequence of actions: turn left at the bakery, then right after the bridge. In a map-based strategy, a person builds a mental model of the area — often called a cognitive map — that represents how places relate to one another and therefore allows shortcuts and detours. The second strategy is more flexible, and it is closely associated with the hippocampus, a region of the brain that is also central to memory.

Studies of London taxi drivers, who must memorise a vast network of streets before they can obtain a licence, found that part of their hippocampus tended to be larger than in comparison groups, and that it changed as they trained — evidence that intensive spatial learning can reshape the adult brain. If active navigation can strengthen these circuits, the argument goes, then outsourcing it to a device might allow them to weaken.

There is some evidence for this view. In several experiments, people who followed turn-by-turn directions remembered less about the route and its surroundings than people who navigated with a map or relied on their own observations. One study reported that people who depended heavily on satellite navigation tended to have poorer spatial memory, and that the difference appeared to grow over time. Such findings, however, need careful interpretation. Many studies are small, and correlation is not causation: it is quite possible that people with a weaker sense of direction are simply more likely to rely on a device in the first place.

What the research does suggest is that the way a device is used matters. Following instructions passively, eyes fixed on the screen, appears to leave little trace in memory. Looking up, noticing landmarks and checking the overall map from time to time may help users keep their own sense of direction in working order. In that respect, humans are not so different from other animals: our ability to find our way depends on the attention we pay to the cues around us.

How Animals Find Their Way

Biology · Animal behaviour · 953 words

AEvery autumn, billions of birds leave their breeding grounds in the northern hemisphere and fly south, some crossing deserts and oceans to reach wintering areas thousands of kilometres away. Many return the following spring not merely to the same region but to the same wood, or even the same nest site, and the young of some species make their first migration alone, with no experienced adult to follow. Such feats have puzzled naturalists for centuries, and although a great deal has been learned since the mid-twentieth century, the question of how animals find their way remains one of the most stubborn problems in biology. Part of the difficulty is that navigation is not a single ability but several. To reach a goal, an animal needs a compass, which tells it which direction is which, and — if it is to correct for being blown off course or displaced to an unfamiliar place — something like a map, which tells it where it is in relation to its destination.

BThe most familiar compass is the sun. Using the sun for direction, however, presents an obvious problem, since its position in the sky changes throughout the day. Experiments with caged birds in the mid-twentieth century showed how this problem is solved: the birds combine the sun's position with an internal clock. If a bird's body clock is shifted by keeping it for several days under artificial light switched on and off at the wrong times, it misreads the sun and orients in a predictably wrong direction. Similar time-compensated sun compasses have since been found in insects such as ants and monarch butterflies, and in some fish and reptiles.

CMany songbirds, however, migrate at night, when the sun is unavailable. In a classic series of experiments, young birds were raised in a planetarium, where the artificial night sky could be controlled. The birds learned to identify the point around which the stars appeared to rotate, and later used it to orient themselves — even when the planetarium sky was made to rotate around a quite different star. In other words, they had not inherited a fixed map of the constellations; during their early weeks they had learned which part of the sky stayed still, and treated it as north. Such a star compass appears to be a feature of certain night-migrating species rather than of birds in general.

DThe most intriguing, and most contested, sense is the ability to detect the Earth's magnetic field. That many animals possess such a sense is no longer seriously disputed: it has been demonstrated in birds, sea turtles, salmon and a range of other creatures. How they do it is another matter. One leading hypothesis proposes that light-sensitive proteins in the eye called cryptochromes take part in chemical reactions whose outcome is influenced by the direction of the magnetic field — the so-called radical-pair mechanism. If this is correct, a bird might literally see the field as a pattern superimposed on its normal vision. The idea is supported by evidence that the bird compass depends on light and can be disrupted by weak radio-frequency noise, which should have no effect on a compass based on a simple magnetic material. 📄 The passage — How Animals Find Their Way

AEvery autumn, billions of birds leave their breeding grounds in the northern hemisphere and fly south, some crossing deserts and oceans to reach wintering areas thousands of kilometres away. Many return the following spring not merely to the same region but to the same wood, or even the same nest site, and the young of some species make their first migration alone, with no experienced adult to follow. Such feats have puzzled naturalists for centuries, and although a great deal has been learned since the mid-twentieth century, the question of how animals find their way remains one of the most stubborn problems in biology. Part of the difficulty is that navigation is not a single ability but several. To reach a goal, an animal needs a compass, which tells it which direction is which, and — if it is to correct for being blown off course or displaced to an unfamiliar place — something like a map, which tells it where it is in relation to its destination.

BThe most familiar compass is the sun. Using the sun for direction, however, presents an obvious problem, since its position in the sky changes throughout the day. If a bird's body clock is shifted by keeping it for several days under artificial light switched on and off at the wrong times, it misreads the sun and orients in a predictably wrong direction. Similar time-compensated sun compasses have since been found in insects such as ants and monarch butterflies, and in some fish and reptiles.

Keystone Species: Small Numbers, Large Effects

Ecology · Biology · 442 words

In a stone arch, the keystone is the wedge-shaped block at the very top that holds all the others in place: remove it, and the arch collapses. In the late 1960s, an ecologist studying rocky shores borrowed the term to describe species whose influence on their ecosystem is disproportionate to their numbers or size. The idea of a keystone species has since become one of the most influential concepts in ecology and conservation.

It grew out of a simple experiment. On a stretch of the Pacific coast of North America, a predatory starfish was repeatedly removed from one area of shore, while a nearby area was left untouched. Without the starfish, mussels — its main prey — spread rapidly and crowded out many other creatures, and the number of species in the experimental area fell sharply. The starfish, it turned out, had been keeping diversity high by preventing any single competitor from taking over.

The best-known example involves sea otters on the same coast. Sea otters eat sea urchins, which in turn graze on kelp, the large brown seaweed that forms underwater forests. When otters were hunted almost to extinction for their fur in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, urchin numbers rose in many areas, and the urchins stripped away the kelp, leaving bare rocky areas known as 'urchin barrens'. Where otters have returned, kelp forests have often recovered, and with them the fish and other animals that shelter among the fronds. Because such an effect passes down through several levels of a food chain — from predator to plant-eater to plant — ecologists describe it as a trophic cascade.

Not every keystone species is a predator. In some tropical forests, fig trees produce fruit at times of year when little other food is available, supporting monkeys, birds and bats through lean seasons. Elsewhere, large animals act as ecosystem engineers: by pushing over trees and trampling young plants, elephants help to keep parts of the African savanna open.

The concept has practical value, since protecting a keystone species may benefit many others at once. Ecologists caution, however, that the label is easier to apply than to prove. A species may play a central role in one place and a minor one in another, and few ecosystems have been studied closely enough to be sure. The return of otters, for instance, has not restored kelp everywhere, partly because other factors, such as warming seas, also affect its growth. Even so, the idea has changed how people think about nature: an ecosystem is not simply a list of species but a web of relationships, and pulling on one thread can move the whole web.

The Case for Rewilding

Ecology · Conservation · Society · 979 words

AFor most of the twentieth century, nature conservation in Europe and North America meant, above all, protection: fencing off valuable habitats, managing them carefully and preventing further losses. Rewilding begins from a different premise. Rather than holding a landscape in a particular state through constant intervention, it aims to restore the natural processes that once shaped it — flooding, grazing, predation, the growth and decay of forests — and then to step back and allow the ecosystem to regulate itself. Sometimes this involves reintroducing species that have been lost; sometimes it simply means removing drains, dams or fences and waiting. The idea has attracted enthusiastic supporters and fierce critics in roughly equal measure, and in my view both camps have tended to claim more certainty than the evidence allows.

BNo story has done more to popularise rewilding than that of the grey wolves of Yellowstone National Park. Wolves had been eliminated from the park by the 1920s, largely through deliberate hunting and poisoning, and in their absence elk numbers grew. In 1995 and 1996, wolves captured in Canada were released into the park. In the version of events that has since been retold in countless articles and videos, the wolves not only reduced elk numbers but also changed their behaviour: fearful of being caught in the open, elk began to avoid river valleys, allowing willow and aspen to grow back along the banks, which in turn stabilised the rivers and provided habitat for birds and beavers. Ecologists call such a chain of effects, passing down from predators through plant-eaters to plants, a trophic cascade.

CThe story is appealing, and parts of it are probably true. Subsequent research, however, has made it considerably more complicated. Some studies found that elk did not avoid risky areas as consistently as the so-called 'landscape of fear' idea suggested, and that the recovery of willow and aspen has been patchy rather than general. Other pressures were at work at the same time: hunting by people outside the park, growing numbers of bears and cougars, and a series of dry years all affected the elk. In some places willows have struggled to recover because, after decades without beavers, streams had cut deeper channels and the water table had fallen, leaving young trees out of reach of the moisture they need. Most ecologists would now agree that the wolves have had real effects, but that the cascade was weaker, slower and more uneven than the popular account suggests.

DTo my mind, this does not weaken the case for rewilding; if anything, it strengthens it. The lesson of Yellowstone is not that a single species can repair an ecosystem, but that ecosystems are shaped by many interacting processes, and that once these have been disrupted for long enough, restoring one element may not be sufficient to reverse the damage. That is an argument for acting earlier and more broadly, not for abandoning the attempt. What it does undermine is the habit of presenting rewilding as a simple, guaranteed solution — a habit that risks disappointing the public when results arrive slowly.

EA more modest, but in some ways more convincing, example is provided by the Eurasian beaver. Hunted to extinction in Britain several centuries ago, and reduced to small, scattered populations across much of Europe, beavers have returned to many rivers through both reintroduction and natural spread. By building dams, they create ponds and wetlands that hold back water, release it more slowly after heavy rain and trap sediment. Studies of beaver sites in Britain have reported reductions in peak flows downstream during storms, along with increases in the variety of plants, insects and amphibians. Beavers are not a replacement for flood defences, and their effects depend greatly on the landscape; but they offer something rare in conservation — a species that does useful work for nothing.

FThe objections to rewilding deserve to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. The most immediate concern those who live and work alongside reintroduced animals. Wolves kill sheep and cattle, and although compensation schemes exist in many countries, farmers often argue that payments are slow and incomplete, and fail to reflect the stress of repeated losses. Beavers can flood farmland and block drainage channels. It is easy for conservationists in cities to celebrate the return of a predator whose costs they will never bear, and I believe that rewilding projects which ignore local people have only themselves to blame when they meet resistance.

GA subtler objection concerns the goal itself. If rewilding means restoring nature, to which point in the past should it be restored? Landscapes have been shaped by people for thousands of years, and each generation tends to regard the nature it knew in childhood as normal — a phenomenon known as 'shifting baselines', through which the losses of earlier periods become invisible. Some advocates propose a return to conditions before intensive farming; others look back to a time before humans spread across the continents, when large animals now long extinct roamed Europe. There is no objectively correct answer. This is why I find the emphasis on processes rather than fixed targets the most persuasive feature of rewilding: it does not attempt to recreate a particular moment in history, but to allow ecosystems to change and adapt by themselves.

HUltimately, the success of rewilding will depend less on ecology than on public acceptance. Surveys in several European countries suggest broad support in principle for the return of species such as beavers and lynx, but that support tends to fall as projects come closer to people's own land. Rewilding will succeed, I suspect, only where communities are involved from the beginning and share in its benefits, whether through tourism, protection from floods or simply a sense of ownership. Nature, left to itself, can do remarkable things — but where people live alongside it, it cannot be left entirely to itself.

Surveys in several European countries suggest broad support in principle for the return of species such as beavers and lynx, but that support tends to fall as projects come closer to people's own land. Rewilding will succeed, I suspect, only where communities are involved from the beginning and share in its benefits, whether through tourism, protection from floods or simply a sense of ownership. Nature, left to itself, can do remarkable things — but where people live alongside it, it cannot be left entirely to itself.

In Passage 3, almost every answer depends on a stance marker: in my view, to my mind, I believe, I find, I suspect. Find them first — they are the map of the writer's opinions. Then notice how often the trap is the tone of a paragraph rather than its words.

27YESPara A, last line

Both supporters and opponents … have overstated how certain the evidence is

Evidence: in my view both camps have tended to claim more certainty than the evidence allows.

Supporters and opponents ↔ enthusiastic supporters and fierce critics → both camps; overstated how certain ↔ claim more certainty than the evidence allows. In my view confirms it is the writer's claim.

Trap: in roughly equal measure describes the number of supporters and critics, not their certainty — don't let it distract you from the second half of the sentence.

28NOPara C, line 1 + last line

entirely untrue

Evidence: The story is appealing, and parts of it are probably true; also Most ecologists would now agree that the wolves have had real effects.

The writer criticises the popular story as too simple, not as false. Entirely untrue contradicts parts of it are probably true.

Trap: the long paragraph of criticism (patchy recovery, other pressures) makes readers feel the writer rejects the story. Weaker, slower and more uneven ≠ untrue. Watch absolute words: entirely, always, never.

29NOPara D, lines 1–4

suggest that rewilding should be abandoned

Evidence: this does not weaken the case for rewilding; if anything, it strengthens it and That is an argument for acting earlier and more broadly, not for abandoning the attempt.

The writer explicitly rejects the conclusion in the statement — a direct contradiction → NO.

Grammar note: an argument for X, not for Y and if anything (= in fact, rather the opposite) are strong signals of the writer's position.

30NOPara E, last line

can serve as a substitute for conventional flood defences

Evidence: Beavers are not a replacement for flood defences.

Substitute ↔ replacement; the writer negates it.

Trap: the paragraph is very positive — reductions in peak flows downstream during storms, a species that does useful work for nothing. Readers carried along by that tone choose YES. Praise for a benefit is not a claim that it replaces something.

31NOT GIVENPara F, lines 2–3

should be paid by national governments rather than by conservation groups

The passage says only that compensation schemes exist in many countries and reports farmers' complaints that payments are slow and incomplete.

Nothing is said about who pays or should pay. The writer gives an opinion about ignoring local people, but not about funding.

Trap: the writer clearly sympathises with farmers (whose costs they will never bear), so it is tempting to infer YES. Sympathy for farmers is not a view on which institution should pay — NOT GIVEN.

32CPara C, lines 6–8

why have willows failed to recover in some places?

Evidence: streams had cut deeper channels and the water table had fallen, leaving young trees out of reach of the moisture they need.

Ground water has fallen ↔ the water table had fallen; below the reach of young trees ↔ out of reach of the moisture they need.

Trap: bears and cougars (option B) appear in the same paragraph, but as pressures on elk, not on trees. Option D reverses the popular story, in which elk avoided river valleys.

33BPara D, last line

what danger does the writer see …?

Evidence: the habit of presenting rewilding as a simple, guaranteed solution — a habit that risks disappointing the public when results arrive slowly.

Danger ↔ risks; people may be disappointed ↔ disappointing the public; when change takes a long time ↔ when results arrive slowly.

Note how the answer sits after a dash: writers often place the consequence of an idea in an appositive phrase after —.

34BPara E, last line

a species that does useful work for nothing

Context: beaver dams hold back water, release it more slowly after heavy rain and trap sediment — work that people would otherwise have to pay for.

For nothing = without payment. The writer is saying beavers provide free services (water storage, flood reduction), so option B is the correct inference.

Trap: option C is contradicted in the same sentence (Beavers are not a replacement for flood defences); option D ignores paragraph F, where beavers can flood farmland and block drainage channels.

35BPara G, lines 2–4

The term “shifting baselines” refers to

Evidence: each generation tends to regard the nature it knew in childhood as normal — a phenomenon known as ‘shifting baselines’.

Accept … as normal ↔ regard … as normal; the nature it grew up with ↔ the nature it knew in childhood.

Trap: option D borrows thousands of years and farming from the same paragraph, but those words describe the history of landscapes, not the meaning of the term.

36CPara A, line 5

Removing drains, dams or fences is one way in which rewilding can …

Evidence: Sometimes this involves reintroducing species that have been lost; sometimes it simply means removing drains, dams or fences and waiting.

The contrast sometimes … ; sometimes … shows that removing barriers is an alternative to reintroducing species. The aim, stated earlier, is to restore the natural processes that once shaped it.

Ending C: allow natural processes to resume without introducing any species.

37EPara C, lines 4–5

Changes in the elk population … cannot be attributed to wolves alone because …

Evidence: Other pressures were at work at the same time: hunting by people outside the park, growing numbers of bears and cougars, and a series of dry years all affected the elk.

Several other pressures affected the animals at the same time ↔ Other pressures were at work at the same time … all affected the elk.

Trap: ending B (have consistently restored rivers within a few years) echoes the popular story of rivers being stabilised — which the passage says was slower and more uneven.

38APara F, last line

rewilding projects which ignore local people …

Evidence: I believe that rewilding projects which ignore local people have only themselves to blame when they meet resistance.

Are themselves to blame for ↔ have only themselves to blame; the opposition they face ↔ when they meet resistance.

Vocabulary note: have only themselves to blame is an idiom meaning "the problem is their own fault". Idioms like this often carry the writer's judgement in Passage 3.

39DPara G, last three lines

prefers an emphasis on processes to fixed historical targets because …

Evidence: There is no objectively correct answer. This is why I find the emphasis on processes rather than fixed targets the most persuasive feature of rewilding.

This is why links the preference directly to the previous sentence: there is no objectively correct point in the past ↔ no single correct point in the past to aim for.

Trap: ending F (make the public less interested) sounds like a plausible worry, but public interest is discussed only in paragraph H, and not as a reason for this preference.
