Why Waiting in Line Feels So Long
Research into queues shows that our frustration has less to do with the clock than with the mind.

Reading Passage 1 · Psychology · 548 words

AMost of us spend a surprising amount of our lives waiting in queues — at supermarkets, airports, banks and government offices. One estimate suggests that the average person in a developed country spends around six months of their life standing in line. Yet research into queues has shown that the frustration we feel depends less on how long we actually wait than on how the wait is experienced.

BThe modern study of queuing began in Copenhagen in the early twentieth century, when a Danish engineer, Agner Krarup Erlang, worked for the city’s telephone company. Erlang wanted to calculate how many telephone lines and operators were needed so that callers would not have to wait too long to be connected. His mathematical work, now known as queuing theory, is still used today to plan everything from call centres to hospital emergency departments.

CMathematics, however, can only solve part of the problem. In the 1980s, David Maister, a business professor, argued that the psychology of waiting matters as much as its length. One of his best-known observations was that unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time. This is why many restaurants hand customers a menu while they wait for a table, and why lifts in tall buildings often have mirrors beside them: people who are busy looking at themselves are less likely to complain about the wait.

DA famous example comes from an airport in Houston, Texas. Passengers there complained constantly about the time they spent waiting for their luggage. The airport employed more baggage handlers, which reduced the average wait to eight minutes, but the complaints continued. Investigators then discovered that passengers walked for only one minute from the plane to the baggage area and then stood for seven minutes. The solution was surprisingly simple: the airport moved arrival gates further away from the main terminal and sent bags to the most distant carousel. Passengers now walked for six minutes and waited for two, and the complaints almost disappeared.

EUncertainty also makes waiting worse. People who do not know how long they will wait tend to become more anxious and impatient than those who are given an estimate. For this reason, many theme parks display expected waiting times at the entrance to each ride. Some deliberately overestimate the time, so that visitors are pleasantly surprised when they reach the front sooner than expected.

FPerhaps the strongest feeling of all, however, is a sense of unfairness. Research has found that people are more upset by someone pushing in ahead of them than by the length of the wait itself. Many banks and post offices have therefore replaced several separate lines with a single ‘serpentine’ line that feeds customers to the next free counter. Although this does not usually reduce the average waiting time, customers prefer it because everyone is served in order and nobody can choose a faster line.

GTechnology is now changing the experience again. In many places, customers can take a virtual ticket on their phone and receive a message when it is their turn, allowing them to wait in a café rather than in a line. Yet some researchers believe that a visible queue still has value. A long line outside a restaurant, they point out, can persuade passers-by that the food must be worth waiting for.

When a Language Dies
Hundreds of the world’s languages may fall silent this century. What is lost — and can anything be done?

Reading Passage 2 · Language and culture · 481 words

AOf the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, linguists estimate that around 40 per cent are endangered, and that one disappears on average every few months. Many of these languages have never been written down, so when their last speakers die, an entire system of words, sounds and grammar can vanish without leaving a trace. Half of the world’s population, meanwhile, speaks just 23 of the largest languages.

BLanguages rarely die suddenly. More often, the process takes place over three or four generations. Parents who speak a minority language at home decide, often for practical reasons, to raise their children in a national or global language that seems to offer better jobs and education. The children may understand their grandparents’ language but no longer speak it, and their own children may not understand it at all. By the time anyone notices, there may be only a handful of elderly speakers left.

CWhy should this matter to people who have never heard these languages? One answer is that each language contains knowledge that cannot easily be translated. Communities that have lived in one place for centuries often have detailed names for local plants, animals and weather patterns. Researchers working with speakers of indigenous languages in the Pacific, for instance, have recorded words that distinguish dozens of types of fish and the seasons when each can be caught. When the language is lost, much of this knowledge goes with it.

DLanguages also reveal the variety of ways in which the human mind can organise experience. Some languages, for example, have no words for ‘left’ and ‘right’, and their speakers describe position using compass directions instead, saying things like ‘the cup is to the north of the plate’. Speakers of such languages have been found to have an exceptional sense of direction, even in unfamiliar buildings. Each language that is lost reduces the evidence available to scientists who study how language and thought are connected.

ENot every story ends in loss, however. Hebrew, which for centuries was used mainly for religious and written purposes, was revived as an everyday spoken language in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries and now has millions of native speakers. In New Zealand, Māori ‘language nests’ — pre-schools in which young children are cared for entirely in Māori — have helped to increase the number of young speakers since they were introduced in the 1980s. In Wales, the number of Welsh speakers has stabilised after decades of decline, partly because of Welsh-language schools and television.

FTechnology offers new tools as well. Linguists can now record speakers on inexpensive digital equipment, and online dictionaries and language-learning apps allow scattered communities to use their language daily. Yet experts warn that recordings alone cannot save a language. A language survives, they argue, only if it is spoken by children, and that depends on whether families believe it has a future.

Should Robots Care for Older People?
As populations age and carers become scarce, machines are entering care homes. Not everyone is convinced.

Reading Passage 3 · Technology and society · 466 words

APopulations around the world are ageing rapidly. In Japan, almost 30 per cent of people are now over 65, and similar trends are visible across Europe and East Asia. At the same time, many countries face a serious shortage of care workers: Japan alone is expected to need hundreds of thousands more within the next decade. Faced with this gap, governments and companies are increasingly turning to robots.

BThe robots already in use vary widely. Some, such as robotic beds that turn into wheelchairs or machines that help carers lift patients, perform physical tasks that would otherwise cause back injuries among staff. Others are designed for company. The best known of these is Paro, a robotic baby seal developed by the Japanese engineer Takanori Shibata. Paro responds to touch and voice, moves its head and tail, and makes soft sounds. It has been used in care homes in more than 30 countries.

CSupporters claim that such companion robots can bring real benefits. A study led by the nursing researcher Wendy Moyle in Australia found that residents with dementia who spent time with Paro showed less agitation and were more engaged than those who did not. Carers also reported that the robot sometimes helped residents to start conversations with each other. Unlike live animals, robotic pets do not need feeding, cannot bite and do not trigger allergies.

DCritics, however, raise serious concerns. The sociologist Sherry Turkle has argued that offering robots as companions to lonely people is a form of deception, since the machine only appears to care. She worries that relying on robots will allow families and societies to feel less responsible for visiting and looking after older relatives. Other researchers point out that the benefits found in studies are often small and short-lived, and that the novelty of a robot may wear off after a few weeks.

ECost is another obstacle. A single Paro costs several thousand dollars, and more advanced robots are far more expensive. A large government programme in Japan, which subsidised care robots for hundreds of care homes, found that many machines were used only occasionally, or not at all, because staff lacked the time or training to operate them. In some cases, introducing robots actually created extra work for carers.

FMy own view is that the debate is often framed in the wrong way. The question is not whether robots should replace human carers, which almost no one in the field seriously proposes, but which tasks they can do well. Lifting and moving patients, reminding people to take medicine and monitoring for falls are all areas where machines can relieve exhausted staff. Companionship, on the other hand, should remain a human responsibility. Used in this way, robots could give carers more time, not less, for the personal contact that matters most.

The Unexpected Value of Boredom

Extra reading 1 · Psychology · 332 words

ABoredom has a bad reputation. We associate it with wasted time, and most of us will do almost anything to avoid it — in one well-known experiment, a quarter of the women and two-thirds of the men chose to give themselves a mild electric shock rather than sit alone with their thoughts for fifteen minutes. Smartphones have made escape easier than ever: the moment we have nothing to do, we can fill the gap with messages, videos or games.

BYet a growing number of psychologists argue that boredom has a useful function. It acts, they say, as a signal that our current activity is not satisfying and that we should look for something more meaningful. In this sense, boredom is similar to hunger or pain: unpleasant, but valuable because it pushes us to change our behaviour.

CThere is also evidence that boredom can encourage creativity. In a study at the University of Central Lancashire in the UK, participants were first asked to copy numbers from a telephone directory — a deliberately dull task — and then to think of as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups. They produced more ideas than a group who had not done the boring task first. The researchers suggested that a bored mind begins to daydream, and that daydreaming helps people to make new connections between ideas.

DThe difficulty is that constant digital stimulation may prevent this process from happening. If every quiet moment is filled with a screen, the mind never has the chance to wander. Some teachers and parents are therefore experimenting with ‘boredom time’ — periods without devices in which children must find their own ways to occupy themselves.

EBoredom is not always harmless, however. Chronic boredom, especially at work or school, has been linked to poor performance, unhealthy eating and risk-taking. The aim, psychologists say, is not to seek boredom for its own sake, but to stop treating every empty moment as a problem that must be solved immediately.

3. The Lancashire study found that bored participants made fewer mistakes when copying numbers.

Why Cities Are Painting Their Roofs White

Extra reading 2 · Cities and climate · 287 words

AOn a hot summer afternoon, the centre of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the countryside around it. This ‘urban heat island’ effect is caused largely by dark surfaces such as asphalt roads and tarred roofs, which absorb sunlight during the day and release the heat slowly at night. As heatwaves become more frequent, the effect is no longer just uncomfortable: it increases energy use for air conditioning and can be deadly for older people.

BOne of the simplest responses is to make roofs reflect sunlight instead of absorbing it. A conventional black roof can reach temperatures of more than 80°C on a sunny day, while a white or specially coated ‘cool roof’ may stay 30°C cooler. In New York, a programme launched in 2010 has coated more than ten million square feet of roofs, mainly using volunteers. A Short History of the Umbrella

Speed reading · 330 words · 228 words

AUmbrellas have existed for at least 3,000 years, but for most of their history they were used to protect people from the sun, not the rain. In ancient Egypt, Assyria and China, large sunshades were carried by servants above kings and nobles, and the umbrella became a symbol of power and high status. The Chinese are believed to have been the first to make waterproof umbrellas, by covering paper with wax and lacquer, possibly as early as the fourth century.

BIn Europe, umbrellas were rare until the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and for a long time they were seen as an accessory for women. Men who carried them were often laughed at. This began to change in London in the 1750s, when a traveller named Jonas Hanway carried an umbrella in public for some thirty years, ignoring the insults of coach drivers, who feared losing customers on rainy days.

CEarly umbrellas were heavy, with frames made of wood or whalebone. In 1852, the English manufacturer Samuel Fox designed a lighter frame using steel ribs, which became the basis of most modern umbrellas. The folding pocket umbrella followed in the twentieth century: a German engineer, Hans Haupt, received a patent for a compact design in 1928. Today, it is estimated that more than a billion umbrellas are sold worldwide every year, and the vast majority are produced in China.

Many countries are facing a growing gap between the number of older people who need care and the number of workers available to provide it, and some see robots as the answer. While I agree that technology can play a valuable role, I do not believe it is the best overall solution.

There is no doubt that machines can reduce the pressure on care staff. Lifting patients, moving them from bed to wheelchair and checking whether someone has fallen are physically demanding tasks, and robots can perform them reliably without injuring workers. Similarly, simple devices can remind older people to take their medicine or alert relatives in an emergency. In Japan, where almost a third of the population is over 65, such technology may be essential simply to keep care homes running.

However, care is not only a set of physical tasks. Many older people suffer from loneliness, and what they need most is conversation, attention and the feeling that someone values them. A robotic pet may calm a person for a while, but it cannot genuinely care, and there is a risk that families will visit less if they believe a machine is providing company. Moreover, experience in Japan shows that expensive robots are often left unused when staff lack training, which suggests that money might be better spent on recruiting and paying carers properly.

In conclusion, technology should be used to support human carers by taking over routine physical work, so that they have more time for personal contact. Treating robots as a replacement for people, however, would be a mistake.
