Reading — Rising Dough

Before you write about today's graph, read the story behind it. This article describes the same three bakeries you will write about later — notice how it describes changes over time. The bakeries come from a textbook exercise; the article and the reasons it gives are invented for practice.

Rising Dough: How Three London Bakeries Swapped Places in a Decade

Business · 450 words

In 2000, if you had asked people in this corner of London where to buy bread, most would have sent you to Lovely Loaves. With annual earnings of around £82,000, it was by far the most successful of the area's three independent bakeries, comfortably ahead of Robbie's Bakery (£54,000) and the tiny Bernie's Buns, which earned just £20,000. Ten years later, the picture had been turned upside down.

The three businesses could hardly have been more different. Lovely Loaves was an old family firm, famous for its traditional white bread. Robbie's Bakery was a quiet shop that relied almost entirely on regular customers, while Bernie's Buns had only just opened and sold little more than sweet buns and coffee.

Lovely Loaves enjoyed a strong start to the decade. Its income peaked at about £93,000 in 2001, and although it fluctuated over the next three years, it stayed close to £90,000 until 2004. Then, in 2005, a large supermarket opened a few streets away. Almost overnight, the bakery's earnings plunged to £62,000, and the decline continued: by 2008 they had fallen to around £41,000, where they levelled off for the rest of the period.

Robbie's Bakery, by contrast, was a picture of stability. For six years its income hardly changed at all, staying at roughly £54,000 a year. The turning point came in 2006, when the owner's son took over and introduced online ordering and a range of gluten-free products. Earnings edged up to £60,000 that year — just enough to overtake Lovely Loaves for the first time — and then rose dramatically, reaching almost £100,000 in 2008 and a peak of about £104,000 in 2010.

Perhaps the most remarkable story, however, belongs to Bernie's Buns. Starting from a very low base of £20,000, its income grew unevenly at first, rising to £25,000 in 2001 before dipping slightly the following year. A move to a bigger shop in 2003 pushed earnings up to £38,000, a level they held until 2005. After a small fall in 2006, the business took off: income jumped to £61,000 in 2008 and finished the decade at around £66,000 — more than three times its original figure, and well above Lovely Loaves.

What explains Bernie's success? Customers point to its friendly staff, its longer opening hours and a menu that changed with the seasons. Unlike Lovely Loaves, it never tried to compete with the supermarket. Instead, it offered things the supermarket could not: bread baked on site every few hours and a comfortable place to sit down.

Local business experts say the lesson is clear. "Size isn't everything," one commented. "The bakeries that adapted to what customers wanted grew. The one that stood still was left behind."

Summarize the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

How a Task 1 answer is built

  • Paragraph 1 — an introduction to the topic of the graph, in your own words
  • Paragraph 2 — an overview of the main ideas in the graph
  • Paragraphs 3 and 4 — the most important changes and numbers
  • At least 150 words · about 20 minutes in the exam
