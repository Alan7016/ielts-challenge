The Trap Lab — Sunday's Mock Under the Microscope

Day 21 is a reading day, and it is deliberately hard. First, four questions from your Day 20 mock that split the Advanced group almost in half. Work out exactly why the right answer is right.

Advanced group, Day 20 reading: 48% on Question 2, 46% on Question 28, 50% on Question 33 and only 37% on Question 38. Passage 3 — the abstract one — cost the group the most marks. Today has two passages of that kind.

1. Q2 (TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN). "Soap has enjoyed widespread popularity in Europe for over a thousand years." Text: "...finally managed to appeal to a wide European population in the 17th century." Half of you chose TRUE. What should have stopped you?

2. Q28 (writer's view). "According to the second paragraph, people are judged by..." The text: people whose semantic habits are "reasonably similar to those of most people around them" are seen as normal; different ones as "original" or "crazy". Correct answer: how conventional their responses are. Many chose "the closely-related people around them". Why is that wrong?

3. Q33 (YES / NO / NOT GIVEN). "Meaning that is unique to an individual is less worthy of study than shared meanings." The writer gives an example of a personal meaning (the manager who dislikes Harvard men) but never compares its value. Answer?

4. Q38 (sentence endings). "Bridgman..." — the text: "the true meaning of a term is to be found by observing what a man does with it." Which ending matches?

The pattern

In three of these four questions, the wrong option re-used words from the passage. In the hardest IELTS questions, the right answer almost never copies the text — it paraphrases it. When an option looks like a photocopy of the passage, slow down.

Reading 1 — The Prince Who Measured the Sky

Suggested time: 25 minutes for Tasks 3–5. Choose the correct heading for paragraphs B–I. Twelve headings, eight paragraphs — three headings are built to catch keyword-matchers.

The Prince Who Measured the Sky

History of science · Samarkand · 738 words

AIn 1908, a Russian archaeologist named Vasily Vyatkin began digging on a low hill on the edge of Samarkand. Guided by an old document describing the land, his team uncovered something remarkable: a deep trench cut into the rock and lined with marble, curving down into the hill like a giant slide. It was the lower part of the largest astronomical instrument of the fifteenth century — and the first physical proof that one of the great scientific institutions of the medieval world had really existed.

BThe man who built it was not, by training, a scientist. Muhammad Taraghay, better known as Ulugh Beg, was a grandson of Timur, the conqueror whose empire had stretched from Anatolia to northern India. He was given control of Samarkand in 1409, while still a teenager, and governed the city for almost forty years. Unlike his grandfather, however, he showed little enthusiasm for war. His passions were mathematics, poetry and, above all, the stars.

CIn 1420 he completed a madrasa on Samarkand's Registan square, a college where he himself is said to have taught. A few years later he turned to his most ambitious project. On a hill just outside the city he built a three-storey circular observatory. Its main instrument, known as the Fakhri sextant, was an enormous arc with a radius of about forty metres, laid exactly along the north–south line so that the Sun, the Moon and the planets passed over it as they crossed the meridian.

DSize mattered. In an age before telescopes, the accuracy of an observation depended largely on the scale of the instrument. On a small brass instrument, a single degree might be a few millimetres wide; on Ulugh Beg's arc, each degree was marked across roughly seventy centimetres, so observers could read positions far more precisely than with any portable device. The disadvantage, of course, was that such an instrument could never be moved, could not be repaired cheaply and could not be copied by anyone with less money than a king.

EUlugh Beg did not work alone. He gathered around him some of the finest mathematicians of the age, among them Qadi Zada al-Rumi and Jamshid al-Kashi, who in 1424 calculated the value of pi correctly to sixteen decimal places — a record that was not beaten for more than 170 years. The younger scholars included Ali Qushji, who would later play a crucial role in the observatory's survival — not of its walls, but of its ideas.

FThe team's greatest achievement was the Zij-i Sultani, a set of astronomical tables completed in the late 1430s. Its star catalogue recorded the positions of roughly a thousand stars, the most thorough catalogue made since Ptolemy's in the second century. The astronomers also measured the length of the sidereal year — the time the Earth takes to return to the same position relative to the stars — as 365 days, 6 hours, 10 minutes and 8 seconds, less than a minute longer than the value accepted today.

GYet the observatory's life was short. In 1447, after the death of his father, Ulugh Beg became ruler of the whole Timurid empire, a position for which his scholarly interests had not prepared him. Within two years he had been defeated by rivals, and in 1449 he was murdered on the orders of his own son, Abd al-Latif. Religious conservatives who had disapproved of his scholarly circle gained influence, the observatory was abandoned, and in the decades that followed it was stripped for building materials until almost nothing remained above ground.

HThe knowledge, however, survived — largely thanks to Ali Qushji, who carried copies of the tables away from Samarkand and eventually settled in Istanbul, where he taught mathematics and astronomy. Manuscripts of the Zij spread across the Islamic world, and in 1665 the English scholar Thomas Hyde published part of the star catalogue in Latin at Oxford, bringing Ulugh Beg's measurements to European astronomers more than two centuries after they had been made.

IToday, visitors can walk down beside the surviving section of the great arc, now protected by a modern building, and a small museum tells the observatory's story; a crater on the Moon also bears his name. For historians, though, the site raises a question that has little to do with stars: how much of the scientific past has been lost not because it was wrong, but because the people who protected it lost power?

Reading 2 — Should Old Cities Look Old? (YES / NO / NOT GIVEN)

Suggested time: 25 minutes for Tasks 6–8. This is an opinion article — exactly the kind of passage that cost marks on Sunday. Do the statements agree with the views of the writer?

Should Old Cities Look Old?

Opinion · Heritage · 607 words

Anyone who has visited the great monuments of Samarkand or Bukhara will remember the colour: turquoise domes, glittering tilework and gateways so bright they seem to have been finished last week. In many cases, they nearly were. Much of what visitors photograph today is the product of twentieth- and twenty-first-century reconstruction, and some of it is less than fifty years old. Tour guides rarely mention this, and most visitors never think to ask.

I do not say this to criticise the craftsmen involved, whose skill is extraordinary. Without their work, several of the region's most important buildings would by now be heaps of brick. Earthquakes, neglect and the simple passing of centuries had left many of them in ruins long before the first restorers arrived, and a building that collapses cannot teach anyone anything. The question is not whether we should repair the past, but how far repair should go.

Conservation specialists have argued about this for more than a century, and in 1964 an international meeting in Venice produced a short document that still guides the profession. The Venice Charter states that restoration "must stop at the point where conjecture begins" — in other words, where the evidence runs out, so should the rebuilding — and that any new work should be distinguishable from the original. A visitor, in principle, should be able to tell which parts of a wall a fifteenth-century builder laid and which were added last year.

Much recent restoration in Central Asia has ignored that principle, and the reasons are understandable. A complete, gleaming monument attracts more visitors than a half-ruined one, and tourism brings money, jobs and international attention to cities that need all three. The difficulty is that the most attractive solution is not always the most honest one. When domes that collapsed centuries ago are rebuilt from guesswork, the building stops being evidence of the past and becomes, at least partly, an illustration of what someone today imagines the past looked like.

There can also be a heavier price. In 2016, UNESCO placed the historic centre of Shakhrisabz, Timur's birthplace, on its List of World Heritage in Danger after old residential areas around the monuments were cleared to make room for parks and tourist facilities. The monuments survived; much of the ordinary city that had grown up around them over five centuries did not. It is a pattern seen far beyond Uzbekistan: we preserve the palace and demolish the street, forgetting that the street is often the more precious record, because it shows how ordinary people actually lived.

Defenders of ambitious restoration have a reasonable reply. Many of these buildings were never meant to be admired as ruins; they were built to be used and to impress, and their creators would have been horrified to see them crumbling. Returning them to something like their original appearance, the argument goes, is closer to the builders' intentions than preserving the damage. I find this argument stronger than many of my colleagues admit. But it works only where we genuinely know what the original looked like, and in many cases we do not.

What, then, should be done? I would suggest three modest rules. First, where evidence is missing, leave the gap visible rather than filling it with invention. Second, tell visitors clearly — on signs, in guidebooks, through guides — which parts are original and which are new; most people find the story of restoration fascinating, not disappointing. Third, protect the ordinary fabric of historic towns as carefully as their famous monuments. None of this would stop tourists coming. It would simply mean that what they came to see was true.
