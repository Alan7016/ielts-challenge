Reading Passage 1 — What Are Universities For?

An IELTS Academic passage. Spend about 20 minutes, then answer questions 1–19.

What Are Universities For?

Education · 537 words

AFor most of their history, universities had little to do with employment. The medieval universities of Bologna, Paris and Oxford trained a small number of priests, lawyers and doctors, but their central purpose was the pursuit of knowledge itself. In the nineteenth century, the German philosopher Wilhelm von Humboldt developed this idea into a model that is still influential today: a university, he argued, should unite teaching and research, and its students should be free to follow their intellectual curiosity wherever it led.

BThis model came under pressure in the second half of the twentieth century, as higher education expanded rapidly. In 1950, around 3 per cent of young people in Britain went to university; by 2020, the figure was approaching 50 per cent. A system designed for a small elite was now expected to educate half the population, and governments that paid for this expansion naturally wanted to see an economic return. Universities were increasingly described as engines of growth, and graduates as the skilled workforce that a modern economy needed.

CThe introduction of tuition fees in many countries has intensified this pressure. When students pay tens of thousands of dollars or pounds for a degree, it is hardly surprising that many of them see it as an investment and expect it to lead to a well-paid job. Universities, competing for these students, now publish detailed statistics on graduate salaries and employment rates, and some governments have threatened to reduce funding for courses with poor job outcomes.

DSupporters of a more vocational approach argue that this is entirely reasonable. They point out that a significant proportion of graduates work in jobs that do not require a degree, and that employers frequently complain that new graduates lack basic workplace skills such as teamwork and communication. Building work placements, industry projects and career training into degree programmes, they say, would benefit students, employers and taxpayers alike.

ECritics respond that this argument rests on a narrow understanding of what is useful. The specific technical knowledge taught in a highly job-focused course may become obsolete within a few years, whereas the ability to think critically, write clearly and analyse complex problems lasts a lifetime. Several large surveys of employers have found that these general abilities are valued more highly than subject-specific knowledge. Ironically, therefore, a degree in history or philosophy may prove more "useful" in the long run than one designed to match the current needs of the labour market.

FThere is also a wider social argument. Universities, the critics say, do not exist only to serve the economy: they preserve and extend human knowledge, and they educate citizens who can take part in democratic debate. Much of the research that has transformed modern life — from the internet to vaccines — began as curiosity-driven inquiry with no obvious practical purpose. A university system judged purely by graduate salaries, they warn, would gradually lose the very qualities that made it valuable in the first place.

GIn practice, most universities now try to combine both aims. Many offer modules in employability alongside traditional academic subjects, and an increasing number encourage students to take courses outside their main field. Whether this compromise will satisfy students, employers and governments remains to be seen.

Reading Passage 2 — Mind the Skills Gap

A shorter passage presenting three expert opinions. Spend about 15 minutes, then answer questions 20–29.

Mind the Skills Gap

Employment · 346 words

Every year, business surveys report that employers struggle to find graduates with the right skills. This so-called "skills gap" has become one of the most powerful arguments for making degrees more job-focused. But a growing number of researchers are questioning whether the gap is as large — or as simple — as it appears.

Dr Lena Fischer, a labour economist, believes the problem has been exaggerated. "When employers say graduates lack skills, they often mean that graduates lack experience," she explains. "No three-year degree can produce a person who is fully ready for a specific job on the first day. Training new staff has always been the employer's responsibility, and many companies have simply stopped investing in it." In her view, asking universities to fill this role risks shifting a cost from businesses to students and taxpayers.

Professor Ahmed Raza, who runs a large engineering faculty, takes a more practical position. His department introduced a compulsory year-long industrial placement in 2015. "Our graduates now find jobs faster, and they are more confident," he says. "But the placement only works because it sits on top of a very demanding theoretical course. Students need the theory to understand what they are doing in the workplace." He is sceptical of degrees that replace academic content with job training, which he describes as "teaching people to use today's tools without understanding them".

For Maria Santos, who has recruited graduates for an international bank for twenty years, the subject of a degree matters less than people think. "Some of our best analysts studied music or classics," she says. "What I look for is someone who can learn quickly, handle uncertainty and explain a complicated idea simply." However, she admits that graduates with relevant work experience have a clear advantage at interview, "because they can give real examples of what they have done".

All three agree on one point: the debate is often presented as a choice between two extremes. In reality, they suggest, the most valuable degrees may be those that combine rigorous academic study with genuine opportunities to apply it.

Writing Task 2 — The Discussion Essay

In a discussion essay you must cover both views fairly and give your own opinion. Missing any of these three parts limits your score for Task Response.

📘 How a discussion essay works

paragraphcontent
IntroductionParaphrase both views + state your opinion clearly (thesis)
Body 1View 1: why some people hold it — reasons + example
Body 2View 2: why others hold it — reasons + example (often the view you agree with)
ConclusionSummarise both views + restate your opinion

Common mistakes: discussing only one view · giving no opinion · giving your opinion only in the conclusion · a vague thesis ("both views have advantages and disadvantages").

1. Some people think children should start school at four; others believe seven is better. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

2. Some say museums should be free; others believe visitors should pay. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

3. Some believe that people should be allowed to work as long as they want; others think there should be a fixed retirement age. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

4. Some people think that newspapers are the best source of news; others prefer the internet. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

5. Some people believe that the main purpose of a university education is to prepare students for employment. Others think that universities should give students knowledge for its own sake, regardless of whether it is useful for a career. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

B. True or false? If a statement is false, a box appears — rewrite it so that it is correct.

1. In a discussion essay, you should explain both views, even the one you disagree with.

2. You only need to give your opinion in the conclusion.

3. The thesis "Both views have advantages and disadvantages" is a clear opinion.

4. A common structure is: introduction, view 1, view 2, conclusion.

5. It is a good idea to copy the question into your introduction.

6. You can partly agree with both views, as long as your position is clear.

✓ Correct versions

  1. In a discussion essay, you should explain both views, even the one you disagree with.
  2. You should give your opinion in the introduction and repeat it in the conclusion.
  3. The thesis "Both views have advantages and disadvantages" is not a clear opinion — it does not say which view you support.
  4. A common structure is: introduction, view 1, view 2, conclusion.
  5. It is a good idea to paraphrase the question in your introduction, using your own words.
  6. You can partly agree with both views, as long as your position is clear.

Discussion essays constantly balance two sides. Accurate contrast language is one of the quickest ways to raise your Grammatical Range score.

Key grammar

structuregrammarexample
although / even though+ clause (subject + verb)Although degrees are expensive, they often lead to better jobs.
despite / in spite of+ noun / -ingDespite the high cost, … · Despite being expensive, …
whereas / while+ clause — compares two factsSome value theory, whereas others want practical skills.
However, / Nevertheless,new sentence + commaFees are high. Nevertheless, demand keeps rising.
Admittedly, … but …accept a point, then disagreeAdmittedly, some courses are too theoretical, but …

Writing Task 2

Write at least 250 words.

As university education has expanded and tuition fees have risen, there has been growing debate about what a degree is actually for. While some people believe that universities should primarily prepare students for the workplace, others argue that they should offer knowledge for its own sake. In my view, although employability matters, the deeper purpose of a university is to develop knowledge and the ability to think, which ultimately serve students better than narrow job training.

Those who favour a career-focused approach make several reasonable points. Students who pay high fees understandably expect a return on their investment, and employers frequently complain that graduates lack practical skills such as teamwork and communication. Building work placements into degree programmes, as some engineering faculties have done, can help graduates find jobs more quickly and enter the labour market with greater confidence.

Nevertheless, I believe that defining a university's purpose mainly in terms of employment is short-sighted. The specific technical knowledge taught on job-focused courses can become obsolete within a few years, whereas critical thinking, clear writing and the ability to analyse complex problems are transferable skills that last a lifetime. Indeed, employers themselves often value these general abilities more highly than subject knowledge, and some of the most successful bank analysts, for example, studied subjects such as music or history. Furthermore, much of the research that has transformed modern life began as curiosity-driven inquiry with no obvious commercial purpose.

In conclusion, while universities should certainly help students prepare for work, I am convinced that their main role is to equip young people with knowledge and intellectual skills that go beyond any single job. The best approach may be to combine rigorous academic study with opportunities to apply it in practice. (about 300 words)
