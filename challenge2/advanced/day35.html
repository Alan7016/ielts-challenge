Why Teenagers Need More Sleep
Late nights and sleepy mornings may be more biological than many parents think.

Reading Passage 1 · Health and education · 329 words

AParents have long complained that teenagers stay up late and are impossible to wake in the morning. For years, this behaviour was blamed on laziness or too much screen time. However, research over the past three decades suggests that a large part of the explanation is biological.

BThe key factor is melatonin, a hormone that makes us feel sleepy. In adults and younger children, melatonin is released in the evening, typically around nine or ten o’clock. During adolescence, however, its release is delayed by up to two hours. As a result, many teenagers simply do not feel tired until eleven o’clock or later, even if they go to bed earlier.

CSleep scientists estimate that teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep a night. Yet in many countries, secondary school begins at around eight o’clock, and pupils who travel long distances may have to get up before six. Surveys in the United States suggest that around seventy per cent of high school students sleep less than eight hours on school nights.

DThe consequences of this ‘sleep debt’ are serious. Tired students find it harder to concentrate and remember information, and they are more likely to suffer from low mood. Lack of sleep has also been linked to car accidents among young drivers and to weight gain, because tiredness affects the hormones that control appetite.

ESome school districts have responded by starting the school day later. In Seattle, for instance, secondary schools moved their start time from 7.50 to 8.45 in 2016. A study by the University of Washington found that students slept around thirty-four minutes longer on average, their attendance improved, and their grades in biology rose by four and a half per cent.

FLater start times are not without difficulties. They can make it harder for parents to drop children off before work, and they push after-school sports and part-time jobs later into the evening. Nevertheless, many researchers now argue that the benefits clearly outweigh the costs.

About 20 minutes for Questions 14–26: headings, sentence endings and summary completion.

The Secret Life of Trees
Are forests made of competitors — or of partners?

Reading Passage 2 · Ecology · 288 words

AFor centuries, foresters regarded trees as solitary competitors, each fighting its neighbours for light, water and nutrients. Over the past thirty years, however, research has revealed a far more cooperative picture of forest life.

BMuch of this work began with the Canadian ecologist Suzanne Simard. In the 1990s, she injected birch and fir trees with different forms of carbon that could be traced, and found that sugars were moving underground from one tree to another. The connection was made by fungi, whose thread-like filaments wrap around tree roots and spread through the soil.

CThese fungal networks, sometimes called the ‘wood wide web’, benefit both partners. The fungi receive sugars that trees produce through photosynthesis, while the trees gain water and minerals such as nitrogen and phosphorus that the fungi collect from the soil.

DSimard and others have suggested that large, old trees — which she calls ‘mother trees’ — may support younger trees growing in their shade by passing them nutrients. Some studies even indicate that trees can send chemical warning signals through the network when attacked by insects, prompting neighbours to strengthen their defences.

ENot all scientists accept these claims. A review published in 2023 argued that the evidence for mother trees deliberately helping their offspring is weak, and that many popular descriptions have exaggerated what the research shows. Movement of nutrients through fungi, critics say, does not prove that trees are cooperating in any meaningful sense.

FWhatever the final verdict, the research has practical implications. Forestry practices such as clear-cutting, in which every tree in an area is removed, may destroy fungal networks and make it harder for new forests to grow. Some foresters now leave a number of older trees standing when they harvest timber.

The True Cost of Fast Fashion
Clothes have never been cheaper. Who pays the difference?

Reading Passage 3 · Consumers and environment · 330 words

AOver the past two decades, the way people buy clothes has changed dramatically. Retailers such as Zara and H&M pioneered what became known as ‘fast fashion’: copying catwalk styles quickly and cheaply, and delivering new designs to stores every few weeks rather than twice a year. More recently, online companies have accelerated the process even further, adding thousands of new items to their websites every day.

BThe result has been an explosion in consumption. According to a report by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, global clothing production roughly doubled between 2000 and 2015, while the average number of times a garment was worn before being thrown away fell by around 36 per cent. In some wealthy countries, many items are worn fewer than ten times.

CThe environmental costs are considerable. Producing a single cotton T-shirt can require around 2,700 litres of water, and dyeing and treating textiles is a major source of water pollution in producing countries. Synthetic fabrics such as polyester, which now make up the majority of clothing fibres, release tiny plastic fibres into the water every time they are washed.

DThere are human costs, too. Much clothing is made in countries such as Bangladesh, where wages are low. The collapse of the Rana Plaza factory building near Dhaka in 2013, which killed more than 1,100 workers, drew global attention to unsafe working conditions in the industry. Since then, a legally binding safety agreement signed by many international brands has improved inspections, but campaigners say that wages remain far too low.

EConsumers are not powerless. Buying fewer, better-quality clothes, repairing items and buying second-hand can all reduce the impact of a wardrobe. The second-hand market has grown rapidly, helped by apps that make reselling easy. Yet I believe it would be naive to expect individual shoppers alone to solve the problem. Real change will require governments to set rules — for example, making producers responsible for collecting and recycling the clothes they sell, as France has begun to do.

The Psychology of Colour in Marketing

Extra reading 2 · Business · 168 words

AColour is one of the first things consumers notice about a product, and companies spend large sums choosing it. Research suggests that people make a judgement about a product within about ninety seconds, and that much of this judgement is based on colour alone.

BCertain associations appear to be widespread. Blue is often linked with trust and reliability, which may explain why it is popular with banks and technology companies. CHowever, the meanings of colours are not universal. White is associated with weddings and purity in many Western countries, but with mourning in parts of East Asia. Companies that sell internationally must therefore research local meanings carefully.

DPsychologists also warn against exaggerating the power of colour. What matters most, studies suggest, is whether the colour seems appropriate for the product: a pink power drill or a brown perfume bottle may look strange, whatever the colour’s general associations.

The fashion industry is now one of the world’s major sources of pollution, and people disagree about who should take action. While consumers certainly have a role to play, I believe the main responsibility lies with clothing companies.

On the one hand, there are strong arguments for asking consumers to change their habits. Clothes are now worn far fewer times than twenty years ago, and much of this waste results from people buying items they do not really need. If shoppers bought fewer, better-quality garments, repaired them and used second-hand apps, the demand for cheap clothing would fall. In this sense, every purchase is a small vote for or against fast fashion.

On the other hand, it is unrealistic to expect individual shoppers to solve a global problem. Most consumers know little about how their clothes are made, and cheap prices are hard to resist, especially for families on low incomes. Companies, by contrast, decide which materials to use, how quickly to produce new designs and how factories are managed. Regulation has already shown its value: after the Rana Plaza disaster, a binding safety agreement improved factory inspections, and France now requires producers to help collect and recycle clothes.

In conclusion, although consumers can make a difference by buying more carefully, the greater responsibility belongs to companies, supported by government rules. Only regulation can change the practices of an entire industry.
