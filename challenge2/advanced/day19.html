Reading — Can Ecotourism Pay for Conservation?

An IELTS-style passage linking this week's environment and conservation topics. Spend about 20 minutes on questions 1–14.

Can Ecotourism Pay for Conservation?

Environment · 406 words

AWhen Costa Rica began promoting nature tourism in the 1980s, much of its rainforest had already been cleared for cattle farming. Today, around a quarter of the country is protected, and tourism has become one of its largest sources of foreign income. For many governments and conservation groups, Costa Rica has become the model for a simple idea: if visitors will pay to see wildlife, then protecting wildlife can become more profitable than destroying it.

BThe logic is attractive, particularly for poorer countries with rich ecosystems. Entrance fees to national parks can fund rangers and research, while local people who might otherwise hunt or cut down trees can find work as guides, cooks or drivers. In Rwanda, for example, permits to see mountain gorillas cost visitors well over a thousand dollars each, and a share of the revenue is passed directly to communities living near the parks. Gorilla numbers, which were once critically low, have risen steadily since the 1980s.

CYet the evidence is not uniformly positive. Researchers have found that the benefits of ecotourism are often unevenly distributed. In some destinations, most of the money is captured by international tour operators and hotel chains, and relatively little reaches the villages closest to the wildlife. Where local people see few rewards, support for conservation tends to be weak.

DThere is also the question of environmental impact. Large numbers of visitors can disturb animals, damage fragile habitats and increase waste and pollution. Coral reefs popular with divers have shown signs of damage from careless swimmers and boat anchors, and some wildlife has been observed changing its behaviour in response to constant human presence. The term "ecotourism" itself is not legally protected, which means that almost any company can use it, whether or not its activities are genuinely sustainable.

ESupporters argue that these problems are reasons to manage ecotourism better, not to abandon it. Strict limits on visitor numbers, as applied to Rwanda's gorilla treks, can reduce disturbance while keeping prices high. Independent certification schemes can help travellers identify responsible operators. Above all, they say, conservation projects work best when local communities have a genuine share in both the decisions and the profits.

FUltimately, ecotourism is unlikely to fund conservation on its own. Most protected areas attract few visitors, and tourism can collapse almost overnight, as the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated. It may be most valuable as one source of income among several, rather than as a single solution.
