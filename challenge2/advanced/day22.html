Task 2 of 10

Reading — The City Without Cars

Suggested time: 25 minutes for Tasks 2–5. This passage is long and argumentative, like a real Passage 3. Read it once for structure, then choose the correct heading for paragraphs A–H. There are three extra headings, and each one is a trap.

The City Without Cars

Urban planning · Society · 755 words

AIn 1999, Miguel Anxo Fernández Lores became mayor of Pontevedra, a city of about 80,000 people in north-west Spain. Within months, he had closed most of the historic centre to private cars. Delivery vans were allowed in only during limited hours, residents could drive to their own garages, and everyone else had to walk. The decision was deeply unpopular at first; shopkeepers predicted ruin and some residents talked of leaving. Two decades later, Pontevedra is regularly held up as a model by urban planners from around the world, and the mayor has been re-elected repeatedly.

BThe arguments for such policies are, on the surface, compelling. Cars in city centres generate congestion, noise and emissions, and they occupy an extraordinary amount of space: a parked car takes up roughly the same area as a small bedroom, yet sits unused for most of the day. Remove the cars, supporters argue, and streets can be returned to people — to children playing, to cafés and markets, to trees. Pontevedra's city council reports that its centre went for years without a single traffic death, and that the population of the old town, which had been falling, began to grow again.

COther cities have taken less radical routes. Oslo announced in 2015 that it would make its centre car-free, but after protests it chose a softer approach: rather than banning cars outright, it removed most of the on-street parking spaces in the centre and replaced them with cycle lanes, benches and small parks. Driving was still permitted, but there was little reason to do it. In 2019, not a single pedestrian or cyclist was killed in the city's traffic. Ghent, in Belgium, divided its centre into sectors in 2017; cars can still enter each sector, but they cannot drive directly from one to another and must return to a ring road instead. Within a short time, cycling had risen sharply and through-traffic had largely disappeared.

DThe most common objection comes from business. Shopkeepers tend to assume that their customers arrive by car and that removing parking will drive them away. The evidence, however, suggests that this assumption is often mistaken. One frequently cited survey from Bristol, in England, found that local traders believed around 40 per cent of their customers came by car; the real figure was closer to 20 per cent. Pedestrians and cyclists may spend less on each visit, but they tend to visit more often, and several studies have found that footfall increases once streets become pleasanter places to be.

EThis does not mean that the critics are simply wrong. A car-free centre works best in a compact city with a dense historic core, where most destinations are within walking distance. In sprawling cities with poor public transport, a ban can turn a short drive into a long and complicated journey. People with reduced mobility, parents with small children and workers who need to carry tools all depend on vehicles in ways that enthusiastic planners sometimes overlook. There is also the risk that traffic is not removed but merely displaced, moving congestion and pollution from wealthy central districts to the poorer neighbourhoods around them.

FA subtler concern is that success can itself create problems. When streets become quieter, greener and safer, they also become more desirable, and property prices tend to rise. Critics argue that car-free schemes can speed up gentrification, pushing out the very residents who might have benefited most from cleaner air. A policy designed to make the city fairer may, in the end, make it more inequitable.

GParis offers a reminder that these battles are often political as much as technical. When the city closed an expressway along the banks of the Seine to traffic in 2016, the decision provoked a fierce backlash from drivers and suburban politicians and was challenged in court; it was eventually upheld, and the road is now a riverside park. Supporters regard it as a symbol of a healthier city; opponents still see it as a decision made by central Parisians at the expense of commuters from outside.

HWhat these examples suggest is that the real question is not whether cars should be banned, but how, where and for whom. The most successful schemes have combined restrictions with genuine alternatives — frequent buses, safe cycle routes, exemptions for those who truly need a car — and have been introduced gradually enough for people to adjust. Every transport policy involves a trade-off. A city that pretends otherwise, in either direction, is unlikely to persuade the people who live in it.

Opponents of a ban, however, raise legitimate concerns about access and fairness. The most serious is that a car-free centre can disproportionately affect people who cannot easily walk or cycle, such as elderly residents, parents with young children and tradespeople who need to carry heavy equipment. For them, a journey that once took ten minutes by car may become long and exhausting, particularly in cities with poor public transport. A further concern is that a ban may not reduce traffic at all, but simply displace it; when vehicles are forced onto ring roads, congestion and pollution may move from wealthy central districts to the poorer neighbourhoods around them. In this sense, a policy intended to improve public health could end up being deeply inequitable.

Structure that works

  • Introduction: paraphrase both views + your thesis (your opinion and your main reason).
  • Body 1: the view you agree with less — explained fairly, with an example.
  • Body 2: the view you agree with more — explained, supported, and linked back to your thesis.
  • Conclusion: restate your position in new words; no new arguments.

3. Body 1 topic sentence + two supporting ideas (and one example).

It is sometimes argued that private vehicles should be prohibited in urban centres, whereas others believe that such a measure would do more harm than good. Although a total ban could cause genuine hardship for certain groups, I believe that city centres should be largely car-free, provided that reliable alternatives are in place.

Opponents of a ban raise legitimate concerns, particularly about access and fairness. Elderly residents, parents with young children and tradespeople who need to carry tools all depend on vehicles, and for them a short drive could become a long and exhausting journey, especially where public transport is poor. It is also argued that traffic would not disappear but simply be displaced, so that congestion and pollution move from wealthy central districts to the poorer neighbourhoods surrounding them. In this sense, a policy intended to benefit everyone could prove inequitable.

Nevertheless, I am convinced that the advantages are considerably greater. Not only do car-free streets dramatically improve air quality, but they also save lives: in 2019 Oslo, which removed most central parking, recorded no pedestrian or cyclist deaths at all. Moreover, the fear that local businesses will suffer financially appears to be exaggerated. Shopkeepers tend to overestimate how many customers arrive by car, and people on foot, while spending less per visit, come more frequently, so footfall often rises once streets become pleasant places to be.

In conclusion, however serious the objections may be, they are arguments for introducing car-free zones carefully rather than for rejecting them altogether. If cities invest in public transport first and grant exemptions to those who truly need to drive, removing cars from their centres will do far more good than harm.
