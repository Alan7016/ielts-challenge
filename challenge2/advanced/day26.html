Losing the Night

Catch-up passage · Environment and space · 337 words

AFor most of human history, a clear night sky offered a spectacular view: thousands of stars and the pale band of the Milky Way stretching from horizon to horizon. Today, a large proportion of the world’s population — and the great majority of people in Europe and North America — live under skies so bright that the Milky Way is no longer visible. The cause is light pollution: the artificial light from streets, buildings, vehicles and advertising that spills upwards and outwards and is scattered by the atmosphere into a dull orange glow.

BAstronomers were the first to complain. As cities expanded in the twentieth century, observatories built on the edges of towns found themselves increasingly unable to observe faint objects, and new telescopes have had to be built in remote mountains and deserts. Yet the effects of artificial light go well beyond science. Many animals depend on darkness. Newly hatched sea turtles, for example, find the sea by moving towards the brightest horizon, which on an undeveloped beach is the moonlit water, but on a developed coast may be a road or hotel. Migrating birds can become disoriented by brightly lit buildings, and night-flying insects are drawn to street lamps in vast numbers.

CThere may also be consequences for human health. Light at night can suppress the hormone melatonin, which helps to regulate sleep, and some researchers suspect that long-term exposure may be linked to other health problems, although the evidence on this point is still far from conclusive.

DUnlike most forms of pollution, light pollution can be reversed almost instantly. Shielded lamps that direct light downwards, lower brightness, warmer colours and simply switching lights off when they are not needed can all make a dramatic difference. A number of places have been recognised as ‘dark sky’ reserves or communities, where lighting is carefully controlled. The problem, in my view, is not technical but one of awareness: most people have simply never seen a truly dark sky, and so do not realise what has been lost.

1TRUEPara A

the great majority of people in Europe and North America — live under skies so bright that the Milky Way is no longer visible. Most = the great majority.

2TRUEPara B

new telescopes have had to be built in remote mountains and deserts. Forced ↔ have had to.

3FALSEPara B

moving towards the brightest horizon. Darkest is the opposite → FALSE.

4NOT GIVENPara B

Insects are drawn to street lamps in vast numbers, but there is no comparison with other structures.

Trap: superlatives (more than any other) need explicit evidence.

5NOPara C

the evidence on this point is still far from conclusive. Proved contradicts far from conclusive → NO.

6YESPara D

The problem, in my view, is not technical but one of awareness. The writer’s voice: in my view.

7shieldedPara D

Shielded lamps that direct light downwards. Point light downwards = direct light downwards.

8warmerPara D

warmer colours. Copy the comparative exactly.

9reserves / communitiesPara D

recognised as ‘dark sky’ reserves or communities. Either word is accepted.
