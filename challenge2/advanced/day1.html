Task 2 of 6

Article — Culture Shock: The Hidden Cost of a Big Move

Read the article. The highlighted phrases are defined below it.

Moving to a new country is often described as an exciting adventure, but for most people it also brings a surprising amount of stress. This experience even has its own name: culture shock. It refers to the confusion and anxiety a person feels when they suddenly find themselves surrounded by unfamiliar customs, language, and social rules.

Psychologists often describe culture shock as happening in stages. In the first few weeks, many newcomers feel a strong sense of excitement. Everything feels new and interesting, and small problems are easy to brush off. This early stage is sometimes called the honeymoon period, because — much like the beginning of a romantic relationship — everything looks better than it really is.

Unfortunately, this positive mood rarely lasts. As the initial thrill fades, people begin to notice the real differences between their home culture and their new environment. Simple daily tasks that once felt exciting can start to feel exhausting. It's common at this stage to feel irritable, tired, or even a little hostile toward the new culture. Some people withdraw from social situations altogether, spending more time alone or in contact with people from their own country.

Interestingly, one of the biggest sources of difficulty isn't language — it's the unwritten rules of a society. Every culture has expectations around things like personal space, how directly people speak, how punctual they are expected to be, and how much emotion is appropriate to show in public. When these expectations clash with what someone is used to, even a friendly interaction can feel awkward or confusing.

Fortunately, most people eventually adjust. Over time, they begin to pick up on the subtle signals that once went unnoticed, and they slowly develop coping strategies for daily challenges. This gradual process of settling in is what researchers call adaptation, and it usually leads to a much greater sense of comfort and confidence.

There's also a lesser-known twist to this story. When people eventually return to their home country after a long time abroad, they sometimes experience something similar all over again. This is often called reverse culture shock, and it can be just as disorienting as the original experience, since people often assume that going "home" should feel simple and familiar.

Understanding culture shock doesn't make it disappear, but it can make the experience feel less frightening. Knowing that the ups and downs are a completely normal part of adjusting to a new environment can help people be more patient with themselves — and with anyone else going through the same thing.

Highlighted phrases

brush off — to dismiss something as unimportant, without paying it much attention.

honeymoon period — an initial phase of a new situation when everything feels exciting and positive.

withdraw from social situations — to deliberately avoid spending time with other people.

unwritten rules — social expectations or norms that aren't officially stated but are commonly understood.

personal space — the physical distance people feel comfortable keeping between themselves and others.

coping strategies — practical methods a person uses to deal with a difficult situation.

reverse culture shock — the disorientation someone feels when readjusting to their home culture after living abroad.
