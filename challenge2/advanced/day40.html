Recap Reading — The Return of the Night Train

A short reading with this week’s words and topics — about 12 minutes, 8 questions.

The Return of the Night Train

Recap reading · Travel · 227 words

AFor decades, overnight sleeper trains in Europe seemed to be disappearing. Cheap flights made them uncompetitive, and many routes were closed in the early 2000s. In 2016, however, the Austrian railway company ÖBB took over several routes that German railways had abandoned and relaunched them under the name Nightjet. Today, night trains once again connect cities such as Vienna, Munich, Paris and Amsterdam.

BPart of the revival is driven by environmental concerns. Travelling by train produces far less carbon dioxide per passenger than flying, and in 2018 the Swedish word ‘flygskam’ — ‘flight shame’ — spread across Europe, describing the guilt some travellers feel about flying. Night trains also allow passengers to save the cost of a hotel and arrive in a city centre in the morning.

CThis growing interest is part of a wider trend sometimes called ‘slow tourism’: travelling less often but staying longer, using land transport and spending money with local businesses. Supporters argue that it reduces the pressure of overtourism, since visitors spread out over more places and seasons.

DYet night trains face serious obstacles. Sleeping carriages are expensive to build and carry far fewer passengers than seats, so tickets are often more expensive than flights. Different safety rules and track fees in each country also make cross-border routes complicated. Without government support, many operators argue, night trains will remain a niche choice.

Overall, rail travel became much more popular over the twenty-year period, reaching the same level as car and air travel, both of which declined. The share of coach travel remained unchanged.

In 2005, the car was the most common way to reach the city, accounting for 40% of journeys, followed by the plane at 35%. Only 15% of tourists arrived by train, and coaches made up the remaining 10%.

By 2025, the picture had changed considerably. The proportion of tourists arriving by train had doubled to 30%, while the share of car journeys fell by ten percentage points to 30%, and air travel declined slightly to the same figure. As a result, these three methods were equally popular. Coach travel, meanwhile, still accounted for one in ten journeys.
