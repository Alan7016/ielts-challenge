Reading Passage 1 — The Desert Locust

The Desert Locust
How a harmless grasshopper becomes one of the world’s most destructive pests.

Reading Passage 1 · Nature · 375 words

AFor most of its life, the desert locust is an unremarkable grasshopper. Living alone in the dry regions between West Africa and India, it avoids other locusts and does little damage. But under certain conditions it undergoes a dramatic transformation: it changes colour, becomes more active and begins to seek out other locusts. Scientists call these two forms the ‘solitary’ and ‘gregarious’ phases.

BThe trigger is crowding. After heavy rains, vegetation grows rapidly and locusts breed in large numbers. When the rain stops and plants dry out, the insects are forced together on the remaining patches of green. Research published in 2009 by scientists at the universities of Oxford and Cambridge showed that repeated contact between locusts’ back legs causes a rise in the chemical serotonin, which switches them into the gregarious phase within a few hours.

CGregarious locusts form swarms that can be enormous. A single swarm may cover hundreds of square kilometres, with up to 80 million locusts in each square kilometre. Carried by the wind, swarms can travel up to 150 kilometres a day, and each locust can eat roughly its own weight in fresh food daily. A swarm of one square kilometre can therefore eat as much food in a day as about 35,000 people.

DBetween 2019 and 2021, East Africa suffered its worst locust outbreak for decades. Unusually heavy cyclones had created ideal breeding conditions in the Arabian Peninsula, and swarms spread into Ethiopia, Somalia and Kenya, where it was the worst outbreak for 70 years. Millions of people already facing food shortages were put at further risk.

EControlling locusts is difficult because swarms move quickly across national borders, often into remote or politically unstable areas. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations runs a Desert Locust Information Service, which uses satellite data on rainfall and vegetation to predict where locusts are likely to breed, so that countries can act early.

FThe main method of control remains spraying chemical pesticides, which can harm other wildlife and people. An alternative is a biopesticide based on a natural fungus, Metarhizium, which kills locusts but is harmless to most other species. However, it acts slowly, taking up to two weeks, so it is most useful when outbreaks are detected early.

Reading Passage 2 — Why Do We Yawn?

Why Do We Yawn?
One of the most common human behaviours is still not fully understood.

Reading Passage 2 · Biology · 373 words

AEveryone yawns, often many times a day, and yet scientists are still not certain why. Yawning appears very early in life: ultrasound scans show that babies begin to yawn in the womb, around eleven weeks after conception. Most vertebrates, from fish to birds to dogs, yawn too, which suggests that the behaviour is ancient and serves some useful purpose.

BFor a long time, the most popular explanation was that we yawn to take in more oxygen when levels in the blood are low. In 1987, however, the psychologist Robert Provine tested this idea by asking volunteers to breathe air with extra oxygen or extra carbon dioxide. Their rate of yawning did not change, and the oxygen theory has since been largely abandoned.

CA more recent theory proposes that yawning cools the brain. When we yawn, the deep breath and the stretching of the jaw increase blood flow to the head, bringing cooler blood from the body. Studies by the psychologist Andrew Gallup found that people yawn more often when the air temperature is moderate, and less often when it is very hot or very cold — exactly as the theory predicts, since cooler air is most effective at cooling.

DPerhaps the most intriguing feature of yawning is that it is contagious. Seeing, hearing or even reading about someone yawning makes many people yawn themselves. Studies suggest that around half of adults catch yawns in this way. Interestingly, contagious yawning appears to develop only around the age of four or five, at about the same time that children begin to understand other people’s feelings.

EThis has led some researchers to link contagious yawning with empathy. People are more likely to catch a yawn from a family member than from a stranger, and chimpanzees and dogs have been observed catching yawns, especially from those they are close to. However, the link is debated: some large studies have found that age is a better predictor of contagious yawning than empathy, since older people catch yawns less often.

FWhatever its purpose, yawning is generally harmless. Frequent yawning can occasionally be a sign of tiredness or certain medical conditions, but for most people a yawn is simply a reminder of how much about the human body remains unexplained.

Too Many Tourists?
Popular destinations are trying to protect themselves from their own success.

Reading Passage 3 · Travel and society · 351 words

ATourism is one of the world’s largest industries, accounting for around one in ten jobs worldwide. For many cities and countries, visitors are a vital source of income. Yet in recent years, the word ‘overtourism’ has entered the language to describe places where the number of visitors has become so great that it damages the quality of life of residents and the experience of tourists themselves.

BVenice is perhaps the best-known example. The historic centre now has fewer than 50,000 permanent residents, while millions of visitors arrive each year, many on day trips. In 2024, the city became the first in the world to charge day-trippers an entry fee on its busiest days, in an attempt to spread visitors more evenly.

CIn Barcelona, frustration has been more visible. In the summer of 2024, thousands of residents marched through the city, some spraying tourists with water pistols, to protest against rising rents, which many blamed on short-term holiday rentals. The city government has announced that it intends to end all licences for tourist apartments by 2028.

DAmsterdam has taken a range of measures, including banning the construction of new hotels in the city and voting to move cruise ships away from the city centre. The city has also run advertising campaigns aimed at discouraging certain kinds of visitor, particularly young people coming mainly to party.

EA very different approach has been taken by the Himalayan kingdom of Bhutan, which has long followed a policy of ‘high value, low volume’ tourism. Most international visitors must pay a daily Sustainable Development Fee, which is used to fund healthcare, education and environmental protection. As a result, Bhutan receives relatively few visitors, but each contributes significantly to the local economy.

FIn my view, the problem is not tourism itself but how it is managed. Fees and limits can help, but they risk turning popular places into destinations only the wealthy can afford. The more effective solutions spread visitors across seasons and regions and ensure that local people share in the benefits — otherwise, residents will continue to feel that their homes have been taken from them.

The Four-Day Working Week

Extra reading 1 · Work · 216 words

AFor most of the past century, the standard working week in many countries has been five days. In recent years, however, a growing number of employers have experimented with a four-day week, in which employees work fewer hours for the same pay.

BAmong the largest experiments were two trials in Iceland between 2015 and 2019, involving around 2,500 public-sector workers. Their working week was reduced from 40 hours to 35 or 36 hours without any loss of pay. Researchers reported that productivity stayed the same or improved in most workplaces, while workers’ wellbeing increased considerably.

CIn 2022, a six-month trial in the United Kingdom involved 61 companies and around 2,900 employees. At the end of the trial, 56 of the companies decided to continue with the shorter week, and 18 made it permanent. Employees reported less stress and fewer sick days, and the number of people leaving their jobs fell sharply.

DCritics point out that a four-day week is easier to introduce in offices than in hospitals, schools or factories, where work cannot simply be done faster. Some also warn that squeezing five days of work into four could make each day more intense. Nevertheless, supporters argue that the trials show a long-held assumption — that more hours mean more output — deserves to be questioned.

AIn warehouses in cities from Singapore to New Jersey, lettuces, herbs and strawberries grow in stacked trays under pink and blue LED lights. BSupporters list several advantages. Because conditions are controlled, crops can be grown all year round, without pesticides, and close to the people who will eat them, which reduces transport. Vertical farms can also use up to 95% less water than traditional fields, since water is recycled.

DMost experts therefore see vertical farming as a useful addition to traditional agriculture rather than a replacement for it — particularly in crowded cities and in countries with little farmland or water.

A Short History of the Umbrella

Speed reading · 280 words · 195 words

AUmbrellas were first used not against rain but against the sun. More than three thousand years ago, sunshades appear in art from ancient Egypt, Assyria and China, where they were often a sign of rank: only kings and nobles were shaded by them. In China, oiled paper was used to make shades that could also keep off rain, and Chinese craftsmen were also among the first to make umbrellas that could fold.

BIn Europe, umbrellas were long seen as something for women. When the traveller Jonas Hanway began carrying one in London in the 1750s, he was laughed at in the street, and coach drivers, who earned money from people trying to stay dry, shouted insults at him. Within thirty years, however, umbrellas had become common among men too, and some were still called ‘Hanways’.

CEarly umbrellas were heavy, with frames of wood or whalebone. In 1852 the English manufacturer Samuel Fox introduced a frame with steel ribs, which was lighter and stronger, and the basic design has changed little since. The folding pocket umbrella followed in the twentieth century. Today, around one billion umbrellas are sold every year, and most are made in China.

International tourism has grown enormously in recent decades, and some popular destinations now struggle to cope with the number of visitors they receive. Although I accept that tourism can cause serious problems, I disagree that it does more harm than good, provided that it is well managed.

It is undeniable that mass tourism can damage the places people visit. In cities such as Venice and Barcelona, crowds of visitors make daily life difficult for residents, and the conversion of homes into holiday rentals has pushed up rents, forcing many local people out of their own neighbourhoods. Heavy visitor numbers can also damage historic buildings and natural environments, from ancient city centres to fragile coastlines.

However, these problems should be weighed against the considerable benefits. Tourism provides around one in ten jobs worldwide, and for many developing countries it is one of the few industries that bring in foreign currency. The income it generates can also fund public services: Bhutan, for example, uses its daily visitor fee to pay for healthcare, education and environmental protection. In addition, tourism encourages cultural exchange, helping people from different countries to understand one another.

Crucially, most of the harm caused by tourism results from poor management rather than from tourism itself. Measures such as limiting short-term rentals, charging fees on the busiest days and promoting less well-known regions can reduce overcrowding while preserving the economic benefits.

In conclusion, while international tourism can damage popular destinations, I believe that its economic and cultural benefits outweigh its drawbacks, as long as governments manage it carefully and ensure that local people share in its rewards.
