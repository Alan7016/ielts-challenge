Why Handmade Is Back

Culture · Crafts · 350 words

For most of the twentieth century, the story of everyday objects was a story of factories. Cups, cloth, furniture and paper were mass-produced in enormous quantities, and each item was identical to the next. Prices fell, and goods that had once been luxuries became available to almost everyone. Many traditional crafts, which could not compete on cost, slowly disappeared as the last generation of masters grew old without apprentices.

In recent decades, however, something unexpected has happened: handmade objects have become fashionable again. Part of the reason is simple boredom with sameness. A hand-thrown bowl or a hand-woven scarf is slightly different from every other one, and buyers increasingly value these small irregularities as signs of a human maker. Many people also want to know where their possessions come from, and a craft object usually comes with a story — a place, a family, a technique passed down over centuries.

Technology, ironically, has helped. Online shops and social media allow an artisan in a small village to sell directly to customers on the other side of the world, without depending on middlemen. Tourism plays a part too. Travellers increasingly want experiences rather than souvenirs, and a workshop where visitors can watch silk being dyed or pottery being painted offers both. In Uzbekistan, the ceramics of Rishtan and the silk of Margilan attract visitors partly because they can see the work being done.

Reviving a lost craft is not easy, however. When a tradition dies completely, there may be no one left who remembers how it was done, and modern craftspeople have to reconstruct the process from old books, surviving objects and patient trial and error. Even when they succeed, handmade goods are slow to produce and therefore expensive, so most revived crafts survive in a small, specialised market rather than in everyday use.

Supporters argue that this does not matter. A revived craft, they say, is valuable not because it can compete with factories but because it keeps alive skills and knowledge that would otherwise be lost — a link between the present and the people who first developed them.

From Rags to Pages: How Paper Travelled West

History · Technology · 801 words

APaper is so ordinary today that it is easy to forget it was once a rare and remarkable technology. For roughly a thousand years after it appeared in China, it was unknown in Europe, where important texts were written on parchment made from animal skin. Its slow journey westward — along the trade routes of Central Asia, through the great cities of the Islamic world and finally into Spain and Italy — is one of the least celebrated stories in the history of knowledge.

BChinese tradition gives the credit for paper to Cai Lun, an official at the Han imperial court, who in 105 CE is said to have presented the emperor with a new writing material made from tree bark, hemp, old rags and fishing nets. Archaeologists, however, have found fragments of paper in China that are at least two centuries older, so Cai Lun probably improved and standardised an existing process rather than inventing it. The basic method remained almost unchanged for centuries. Plant fibres were soaked and beaten into a pulp and mixed with water; a fine screen was dipped into the mixture and lifted out, leaving a thin layer of fibres that dried into a flexible sheet. Until then, Chinese writers had used strips of bamboo, which were heavy, or silk, which was too expensive for everyday use.

CHow the technique reached Central Asia is a matter of debate. The most famous answer involves the Battle of Talas in 751, when an army of the Abbasid caliphate defeated Chinese forces near the Talas River, in what is now the border region of Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. According to a story recorded by Arab writers some three centuries later, prisoners taken to Samarkand after the battle included craftsmen who knew how to make paper, and they passed on their skills to local people. The story is neat and memorable, and it is still repeated in many books. Many historians, however, now doubt it. Paper had been travelling along the Silk Road long before 751: letters written on paper by Sogdian merchants, the people of the Samarkand region, have been found in western China and dated to the early fourth century. It is quite possible that paper was already being made in Central Asia before the battle, and that the prisoner story gave a dramatic beginning to a gradual process.

DWhatever its origins, Samarkand paper soon became famous. Local makers adapted the Chinese method to the materials around them, using flax, cotton and, above all, the inner bark of the mulberry tree, which grew widely in the region and was also needed to feed silkworms. Once dry, the sheets were polished with a smooth stone until they shone, producing a durable surface that took ink cleanly. Writers elsewhere in the Islamic world praised the paper of Samarkand as among the finest available.

EFrom Central Asia the technology moved west, and by the end of the eighth century paper was being produced in Baghdad, the capital of the caliphate. Government offices adopted it quickly. One common explanation is that, unlike parchment, from which ink could be scraped away and replaced, paper absorbed ink into its fibres, making it difficult to alter a document without leaving a trace. Mills later appeared in Damascus, Cairo and across North Africa, and cheap paper encouraged the growth of libraries, booksellers' markets and translation projects.

FEurope received paper through Muslim Spain, where it was being made near Valencia by the twelfth century. At first, many Europeans regarded it with suspicion: parchment was considered more durable, and at least one ruler in southern Italy forbade the use of paper for official records. Attitudes changed in the thirteenth century, when mills in the Italian town of Fabriano introduced water-powered hammers to beat the fibres, coated their sheets with animal glue to make them less absorbent, and pressed watermarks into the paper to show which mill had made it. When the printing press arrived in the mid-fifteenth century, paper was ready for it. Printing books on parchment would have required the skins of huge numbers of animals; paper made the printed book affordable.

GIn Samarkand itself, the old craft faded away during the nineteenth century as cheap factory-made paper arrived. In the 1990s, however, craftsmen in the village of Konigil, just outside the city, set out to revive it. With no living master to learn from, they reconstructed the process from historical descriptions and years of experiment. Today, at the Meros workshop, wooden hammers driven by a water wheel beat the mulberry bark into pulp, and the finished sheets are still polished by hand. Visitors can watch every stage, and the paper is bought by artists, restorers and tourists. Its value lies less in its price than in its living connection to a technology that once changed the world.

How a Tree Cools a Street

Science · Cities · 365 words

Anyone who has waited for a bus on a hot afternoon knows the difference a tree can make. Stepping from the sun into the shade of a large tree can feel like entering a different climate. That feeling is not imagination: trees cool their surroundings in two quite different ways, and together these make them one of the most effective cooling systems a city can have.

The first is shade. A leafy canopy intercepts much of the sunlight before it reaches the ground. As a result, pavements, walls and parked cars beneath a tree stay far cooler than those in direct sun; on a summer afternoon, shaded asphalt can be many degrees cooler than asphalt a few metres away. Because shaded surfaces absorb less heat during the day, they also have less heat to give back in the evening.

The second method is less visible. Trees constantly draw water up from the soil through their roots and release it as vapour through tiny openings in their leaves. This process is called transpiration, and together with evaporation from the soil it is known as evapotranspiration. Turning liquid water into vapour requires energy, and that energy is taken from the surrounding air, which therefore becomes cooler. In effect, a tree sweats — and, like sweating, it works only when water is available. On a hot day, a single large tree may release hundreds of litres of water.

This explains why a tree growing in dry, compacted soil gives much less relief than one with plenty of water, and why a lawn that has dried out in summer provides little cooling at all. It also explains why the choice of species matters. Trees with broad, dense canopies offer more shade, while those that tolerate drought are better suited to hot, dry climates such as Central Asia's.

Where trees are planted is important too. A row of trees on the sunny side of a street, or over a car park, can do far more good than the same trees in the corner of a park. Many city planners now measure canopy cover — the share of a city's area shaded by trees — and set targets for increasing it.

The Urban Heat Island

Environment · Urban science · 763 words

AOn a calm summer evening, a thermometer in the centre of a large city can read several degrees higher than one in the fields a short drive away. This difference, known as the urban heat island effect, was first described in the early nineteenth century by Luke Howard, an English amateur scientist who noticed that London was regularly warmer than the surrounding countryside. Two centuries of measurements have confirmed his observation and added an important detail: the gap is usually largest not in the middle of the day but at night. After sunset, open countryside loses its heat quickly, while the city goes on releasing warmth it has stored since the morning.

BThe main reason lies in what cities are made of. Asphalt, concrete, brick and dark roofing materials absorb a large share of the sunlight that falls on them; fresh asphalt, for example, reflects only a small fraction of the light that reaches it. Scientists describe the reflectiveness of a surface as its albedo, and most city surfaces have a low one. These materials also store heat well, so they warm up during the day and release that heat slowly through the evening. The shape of the city adds to the problem. In narrow streets lined with tall buildings — sometimes called urban canyons — sunlight reflected from one wall is simply absorbed by the wall opposite, and the buildings block the winds that might otherwise carry warm air away.

CJust as important is what cities lack: vegetation and moisture. In the countryside, a large part of the sun's energy is used to evaporate water from soil and leaves, a process that cools the surface rather than heating the air. In a city, most of the ground is sealed under hard surfaces, and when it rains, the water is quickly carried away into drains. With little water left to evaporate, more of the incoming energy goes into raising the temperature.

DCities also produce heat of their own. Vehicles, factories and the machinery inside buildings all release waste heat, and in dense districts, especially those full of traffic, this can make a noticeable difference. Air conditioning creates a particularly awkward cycle. An air conditioner does not destroy heat; it moves it from inside a building to the street outside, while consuming electricity that is often generated by burning fossil fuels. The hotter the city becomes, the more people switch on air conditioners, and the more heat is pumped into the streets. A technology that protects individuals from the heat can therefore make the shared environment hotter.

EThe consequences are serious. Heatwaves are among the deadliest forms of extreme weather, and the European heatwave of 2003 is estimated to have caused tens of thousands of deaths, many of them among elderly people living alone in cities. Hot nights are especially dangerous, because the body needs cooler hours in which to recover from the stress of the day. Heat also raises demand for electricity just when power systems are under most pressure, and it speeds up the chemical reactions that form ground-level ozone, a pollutant that damages the lungs. The burden is not shared equally: in many cities, poorer neighbourhoods have less tree cover and more paved surfaces than wealthier ones, and are measurably hotter as a result.

FSome solutions focus on changing the surfaces themselves. Cool roofs, painted white or covered with reflective materials, stay far cooler than dark ones on a sunny afternoon, reducing the heat that enters the building below. Cities such as New York have run programmes in which volunteers paint roofs white. Green roofs, covered with soil and plants, cool through evaporation, provide insulation and hold back rainwater during storms, although they are heavier and more expensive to install. Lighter-coloured pavements are also being tested, though critics point out that reflected sunlight can make streets uncomfortable for pedestrians.

GThe most effective long-term measures, however, bring nature back into the city. Trees provide shade and release water vapour through their leaves, and parks and fountains create pockets of cooler air. But trees take decades to reach full size and need space both above and below ground, so planting must begin long before the benefits appear. Some cities have gone further and designed their layout around cooling. Stuttgart in Germany, which lies in a valley, has long restricted building on certain slopes so that cool air can flow down into the centre at night. No single measure will remove the heat island, but as climate change makes heatwaves more frequent, a combination of them is becoming a necessity rather than a luxury.

📄 The passage — The Urban Heat Island

AOn a calm summer evening, a thermometer in the centre of a large city can read several degrees higher than one in the fields a short drive away. This difference, known as the urban heat island effect, was first described in the early nineteenth century by Luke Howard, an English amateur scientist who noticed that London was regularly warmer than the surrounding countryside. Two centuries of measurements have confirmed his observation and added an important detail: the gap is usually largest not in the middle of the day but at night. After sunset, open countryside loses its heat quickly, while the city goes on releasing warmth it has stored since the morning.

BThe main reason lies in what cities are made of. Asphalt, concrete, brick and dark roofing materials absorb a large share of the sunlight that falls on them; fresh asphalt, for example, reflects only a small fraction of the light that reaches it. Scientists describe the reflectiveness of a surface as its albedo, and most city surfaces have a low one. These materials also store heat well, so they warm up during the day and release that heat slowly through the evening. The shape of the city adds to the problem. In narrow streets lined with tall buildings — sometimes called urban canyons — sunlight reflected from one wall is simply absorbed by the wall opposite, and the buildings block the winds that might otherwise carry warm air away.

CJust as important is what cities lack: vegetation and moisture. In the countryside, a large part of the sun's energy is used to evaporate water from soil and leaves, a process that cools the surface rather than heating the air. In a city, most of the ground is sealed under hard surfaces, and when it rains, the water is quickly carried away into drains. With little water left to evaporate, more of the incoming energy goes into raising the temperature.

DCities also produce heat of their own. Vehicles, factories and the machinery inside buildings all release waste heat, and in dense districts, especially those full of traffic, this can make a noticeable difference. Air conditioning creates a particularly awkward cycle. An air conditioner does not destroy heat; it moves it from inside a building to the street outside, while consuming electricity that is often generated by burning fossil fuels. The hotter the city becomes, the more people switch on air conditioners, and the more heat is pumped into the streets. A technology that protects individuals from the heat can therefore make the shared environment hotter.

EThe consequences are serious. Heatwaves are among the deadliest forms of extreme weather, and the European heatwave of 2003 is estimated to have caused tens of thousands of deaths, many of them among elderly people living alone in cities. Hot nights are especially dangerous, because the body needs cooler hours in which to recover from the stress of the day. Heat also raises demand for electricity just when power systems are under most pressure, and it speeds up the chemical reactions that form ground-level ozone, a pollutant that damages the lungs. The burden is not shared equally: in many cities, poorer neighbourhoods have less tree cover and more paved surfaces than wealthier ones, and are measurably hotter as a result.

FSome solutions focus on changing the surfaces themselves. Cool roofs, painted white or covered with reflective materials, stay far cooler than dark ones on a sunny afternoon, reducing the heat that enters the building below. Cities such as New York have run programmes in which volunteers paint roofs white. Green roofs, covered with soil and plants, cool through evaporation, provide insulation and hold back rainwater during storms, although they are heavier and more expensive to install. Lighter-coloured pavements are also being tested, though critics point out that reflected sunlight can make streets uncomfortable for pedestrians.

GThe most effective long-term measures, however, bring nature back into the city. Trees provide shade and release water vapour through their leaves, and parks and fountains create pockets of cooler air. But trees take decades to reach full size and need space both above and below ground, so planting must begin long before the benefits appear. Some cities have gone further and designed their layout around cooling. Stuttgart in Germany, which lies in a valley, has long restricted building on certain slopes so that cool air can flow down into the centre at night. No single measure will remove the heat island, but as climate change makes heatwaves more frequent, a combination of them is becoming a necessity rather than a luxury.

Is Younger Always Better?

Language · Psychology · 329 words

Most people believe that children learn languages more easily than adults. Watch a family that has moved to a new country, and the evidence seems obvious: within a year or two, the children are chatting with friends in the new language, while their parents still struggle to follow the news. One popular explanation is the idea of a critical period — a window early in life during which the brain is especially ready to acquire language.

Research supports part of this belief. People who begin learning a language in early childhood, and who hear it around them every day, are much more likely to end up with a native-like accent. They also tend to develop a more instinctive feel for grammar. Adults who start later can become highly proficient, but relatively few are mistaken for native speakers.

Yet adults have advantages of their own. In the early stages, they often learn faster than children: they can memorise vocabulary deliberately, understand grammatical explanations and use strategies such as note-taking and self-testing. Studies of immigrant families have found that older learners frequently make quicker progress at first, even though younger ones tend to overtake them in the long run.

The conditions of learning matter enormously. The child in the example above is immersed in the new language for many hours a day, at school and with friends. A child learning English in a classroom for three hours a week is in a very different situation, and research on school programmes suggests that starting a few years earlier brings smaller benefits than many parents expect. In the classroom, the amount and quality of practice may count for more than age.

None of this means that adults should give up. Motivation, regular exposure and a willingness to make mistakes help learners of any age. For most people, the realistic goal is not to sound exactly like a native speaker but to communicate clearly and confidently — and that goal is open to everyone.

Is Bilingualism Good for the Brain?

Psychology · Language · 754 words

AFor much of the twentieth century, growing up with two languages was widely regarded as a disadvantage. Several early studies reported that bilingual children scored lower on intelligence tests, and parents in many countries were advised to choose one language and abandon the other. Looking back, those studies were badly designed. They often compared the children of poor immigrant families with wealthier children who spoke only the majority language, and they tested the bilingual children in their weaker language. In 1962, a study of children in Montreal, which took more care to compare similar groups, found that the bilinguals actually performed better on many measures. Within a few decades, the pendulum had swung the other way.

BThe new view rested on an attractive idea. A bilingual person, it was argued, never completely switches off either language: both are active in the mind, and the speaker must constantly select one and suppress the other. If this is true, bilinguals are exercising, every day, the mental control system that psychologists call executive function — the abilities that allow us to focus attention, ignore distractions and move flexibly between tasks. In the 2000s, a series of experiments reported that bilingual children and adults were faster on laboratory tasks that measure these abilities. Newspaper headlines went considerably further than the evidence, promising parents that a second language would make their children smarter.

CThen the doubts began. When researchers tried to replicate the original experiments with larger groups, many failed to find any advantage at all. Several studies involving thousands of participants found no reliable difference between bilinguals and monolinguals on tests of executive function. Critics pointed out that many of the early studies had been small, and that small studies are more likely to produce striking results by chance. There was also evidence of publication bias: one analysis found that studies reporting a bilingual advantage had been more likely to be published than those that did not. Finally, bilinguals and monolinguals often differ in income, education and culture, and these differences, rather than language, may explain some of the results.

DNone of this proves that the bilingual advantage is a myth. Bilingualism is not a single experience: a child who speaks two languages at home, an adult who learned a second language at school and rarely uses it, and an interpreter who switches between languages all day are all, in some sense, bilingual. It is possible that certain kinds of bilingualism, such as frequent switching, do sharpen certain mental skills. But the most honest conclusion from the current evidence is that any such effect is smaller and less consistent than the early research suggested.

EA second, more dramatic claim concerns old age. Some studies found that bilingual patients developed the symptoms of dementia several years later than monolingual patients with the same disease. The usual explanation is the idea of cognitive reserve: a lifetime of managing two languages may give the brain extra resources, allowing it to compensate for damage for longer before problems become visible. This would not mean that bilingualism prevents the disease, only that it may delay its effects. Yet here, too, the findings have been mixed. Some later studies found no delay, and many of the early ones relied on relatives remembering when symptoms first appeared — a method that is far from precise.

FShould any of this matter to parents and teachers? In my view, the debate about brain benefits has distracted attention from the real reasons for learning languages. A second language opens doors to other cultures, to jobs and to friendships. These benefits do not depend on reaction times in a laboratory. There is also a danger in overselling: if families are told that languages are a form of brain training and then hear that the research has been challenged, they may wrongly conclude that bilingualism is pointless. At the same time, nothing in the recent research suggests that bilingualism harms children, and families who speak a heritage language at home should be encouraged to keep it.

GIt is worth remembering, finally, that much of this research has been carried out in countries where speaking one language is considered normal. In much of the world — including Central Asia, where many people move between two or three languages in a single day — multilingualism is the ordinary condition of life rather than an exception that needs explaining. Perhaps the more interesting question is not whether bilingualism improves the brain, but why we ever assumed that one language was the natural limit.
