Julian Treasure, a sound expert, explains how to speak in a way that makes people want to listen. He begins with seven “deadly sins” of speaking that stop people listening, including gossip, judging, negativity, complaining, making excuses, exaggerating or lying, and dogmatism — confusing facts with opinions. Instead, he suggests four foundations for powerful speech, summarised as HAIL: honesty, authenticity, integrity and love. He then describes a “vocal toolbox” of features we can control, such as register, timbre, prosody, pace, pitch and volume, and he recommends doing vocal warm-up exercises before an important talk. He ends by imagining a world in which people speak powerfully and listen consciously. I found the talk very practical, especially the reminder that how we say something matters as much as what we say.

As mass production replaces handmade goods, many traditional crafts are in danger of being lost. I largely agree that governments should help to preserve these skills, although I believe their support should be targeted rather than unlimited.

The strongest argument for government support is that traditional skills are part of a country’s cultural identity. Techniques such as Japanese sashiko stitching or Uzbek embroidery carry knowledge that has been passed down for generations, and once the last skilled workers disappear, that knowledge may be impossible to recover. Moreover, many traditional crafts are more sustainable than modern industry. Repairing and reusing clothes, for example, reduces waste at a time when the fashion industry is a major source of pollution. Supporting craftspeople can also bring economic benefits, since tourists are often willing to pay high prices for authentic handmade products.

On the other hand, public money is limited, and governments must decide between many priorities. Spending on hospitals, schools and modern technical training may seem more urgent than funding crafts that few people practise. In addition, if a skill only survives because of subsidies, it may not have a real future.

In my view, the best solution is for governments to provide focused support, such as funding apprenticeships, recording traditional techniques and including some crafts in school art programmes, rather than paying for whole industries. In this way, valuable skills can be kept alive while still encouraging them to find a place in the modern economy.
