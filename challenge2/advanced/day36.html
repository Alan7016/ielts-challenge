The Return of the Wolf
After a century of absence, wolves are back in much of Europe. Not everyone is pleased.

Reading Passage 1 · Wildlife · 378 words

AA century ago, wolves had been driven to extinction across most of western and central Europe. Hunted as a threat to livestock and people, they survived only in isolated pockets in countries such as Italy, Spain, Poland and Romania. Today, there are estimated to be more than 20,000 wolves in Europe, and they have returned to areas where they had not been seen for generations, including Germany, France and the Netherlands.

BThe recovery has several causes. In 1979, the Bern Convention gave wolves legal protection in many European countries, and the European Union’s Habitats Directive of 1992 strengthened this. At the same time, millions of people moved from the countryside to cities, leaving behind abandoned farmland that turned into forest and scrub. Populations of deer and wild boar — the wolves’ main prey — increased dramatically.

CWolves are remarkable travellers. Young wolves leave their family pack at around one or two years old to find territory of their own, and some cover hundreds of kilometres in a few weeks. One wolf fitted with a GPS collar travelled more than 1,500 kilometres from Germany to Belarus. This ability explains how wolves have been able to recolonise regions far from the populations they came from.

DTheir return has not been welcomed by everyone. Sheep farmers in particular have suffered losses: in France, wolves kill more than 10,000 sheep a year, and the government pays compensation to affected farmers. Many farmers argue that compensation does not reflect the stress and extra work involved, and that they should be allowed to shoot wolves more freely.

EConservationists point to measures that reduce attacks, such as electric fences and specially trained guard dogs, which have been used for centuries in regions where wolves never disappeared. Where such methods are adopted, losses can fall significantly. Some argue that the real challenge is not ecological but social: learning once again to live alongside a large predator.

FIn 2023, the European Commission proposed reducing the legal protection of wolves, citing growing numbers and conflicts with farmers, and in 2024 the Bern Convention downgraded their status from ‘strictly protected’ to ‘protected’. Supporters of the change say it will allow more flexible management; critics fear it will lead to unnecessary killing of a species that has only just recovered.

Reading Passage 2 — How Bilingualism Shapes the Brain

About 20 minutes for Questions 14–26.

How Bilingualism Shapes the Brain
Does speaking two languages make you think better? The answer has changed more than once.

Reading Passage 2 · Language and the brain · 313 words

AFor much of the twentieth century, raising children with two languages was widely thought to be harmful. Early studies, many of them conducted with immigrant children who were tested only in their second language, suggested that bilingual children had lower intelligence and confused their languages. Parents were often advised to speak only the majority language at home.

BThis view began to change in 1962, when Elizabeth Peal and Wallace Lambert in Montreal compared bilingual and monolingual children carefully matched for social background. To their surprise, the bilingual children performed better on a range of tests, particularly those requiring mental flexibility.

CLater research, led by the psychologist Ellen Bialystok in Toronto, proposed an explanation. Because both languages are active in a bilingual person’s mind, even when only one is being used, the brain must constantly select one and suppress the other. This regular practice, Bialystok argued, strengthens ‘executive control’ — the ability to focus attention, switch between tasks and ignore distractions.

DPerhaps the most striking claim concerns ageing. In a 2007 study, Bialystok and colleagues found that bilingual patients with dementia had developed symptoms around four years later, on average, than monolingual patients. Similar results have since been reported in India and elsewhere, though not in every study.

EIn the past decade, however, the ‘bilingual advantage’ has come under serious challenge. Several large studies have found no consistent difference in executive control between bilinguals and monolinguals, and some researchers have suggested that earlier positive findings were partly the result of small samples and a tendency for journals to publish positive results.

FWhat is not disputed is that speaking two languages brings obvious social, cultural and economic benefits, and that there is no evidence it harms children’s development. The current debate concerns whether bilingualism also improves general thinking skills — a question that may depend on how, when and how often both languages are used.

Reading Passage 3 — The Gig Economy

The Gig Economy
Apps have changed the way millions of people work. Is it freedom or insecurity?

Reading Passage 3 · Work and technology · 313 words

AOver the past fifteen years, digital platforms such as Uber, Deliveroo and Upwork have transformed the way millions of people work. Rather than being employed by a company, ‘gig workers’ accept individual tasks — a taxi ride, a food delivery, a design project — through an app, and are paid for each piece of work.

BEstimates of the size of the gig economy vary widely, partly because many people use platforms only occasionally to supplement other income. The International Labour Organization has estimated that the number of online platforms grew five-fold between 2010 and 2020, and surveys in Britain suggest that around one in seven adults has done some platform work.

CFor many workers, the main attraction is flexibility. Students, parents and people with other jobs can choose when and how much to work, and for some, platforms provide an income that would otherwise be unavailable. Platforms themselves argue that they create opportunities and make services cheaper and more convenient for customers.

DCritics, however, highlight a lack of security. Because gig workers are usually classified as self-employed, they are not entitled to minimum wages, sick pay or holiday pay in many countries, and their earnings can be unpredictable. Algorithms that allocate work and rate performance can also deactivate accounts with little explanation.

ECourts and governments have begun to respond. In 2021, the UK Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers should be treated as ‘workers’, entitled to the minimum wage and holiday pay, and in 2024 the European Union adopted a directive intended to clarify the employment status of platform workers and increase transparency about algorithms.

FIn my view, the gig economy is neither the liberation its promoters claim nor the exploitation its harshest critics describe. Flexibility is genuinely valuable, but it should not require workers to give up basic protections. The challenge for regulators is to preserve the first while guaranteeing the second.

How Long Does It Take to Form a Habit?

Extra reading 1 · Psychology · 216 words

AIt is often claimed that it takes twenty-one days to form a new habit. The figure appears to originate in the 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics by Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon, who observed that patients took ‘a minimum of about 21 days’ to adjust to changes in their appearance. Over time, ‘a minimum of’ disappeared from popular retellings.

BResearch suggests the reality is more varied. In a 2009 study at University College London, Phillippa Lally and colleagues asked 96 volunteers to adopt a new daily behaviour, such as drinking water after breakfast or running before dinner, and to report how automatic it felt. On average, it took 66 days for the behaviour to become automatic, but the range was enormous — from 18 to 254 days. Simpler behaviours became habits faster than more demanding ones.

CEncouragingly, missing a single day did not significantly affect the process. What mattered most was consistency in the same context: performing the behaviour at the same time and place, after the same cue. Psychologists call this ‘context-dependent repetition’.

DSome researchers recommend ‘habit stacking’ — attaching a new behaviour to an existing routine, such as doing stretching exercises while the kettle boils. Others emphasise reducing friction: making the desired behaviour as easy as possible, for example by leaving running shoes by the door.

The Story of the Pencil

AFor most of history, people wrote with brushes, quills or pieces of charcoal. The diagram illustrates the process by which plastic waste is used to build roads.

Overall, there are eight stages, beginning with the collection of plastic waste and ending with the laying of the finished road surface. The process combines recycled plastic with conventional road materials.

Apps now allow millions of people to earn money by delivering food, driving taxis or completing online projects, rather than holding permanent jobs. Although this development brings genuine benefits, I believe that, in its current form, its disadvantages outweigh its advantages.

The most important advantage of gig work is flexibility. Students, parents and people with other commitments can decide when and how much to work, which would be impossible in a traditional nine-to-five job. For some people, such as those who struggle to find permanent employment, platforms provide an income that would otherwise be unavailable. Customers also benefit, as services become cheaper and more convenient.

However, these benefits come at a considerable cost to workers. Because gig workers are usually classified as self-employed, they often have no right to a minimum wage, sick pay or holiday pay, and their earnings can vary greatly from week to week. If they become ill, they may lose their income completely. Moreover, their work is controlled by algorithms that can reduce their jobs or even close their accounts with little explanation, leaving them with no one to appeal to. In the long term, this insecurity makes it difficult for workers to plan their lives, rent a home or save money.

In conclusion, while the flexibility of the gig economy is valuable, I believe its disadvantages are greater at present. Governments should therefore follow the example of the UK courts and the European Union by ensuring that gig workers receive basic protections without losing their freedom to choose when they work.
