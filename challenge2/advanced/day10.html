Article — The Evolution of Mass Media

Read the article below, then answer the questions that follow.

Every generation has believed its own media environment was uniquely overwhelming, and every generation has been at least partly right. What has changed is not the basic desire to stay informed and entertained, but the speed, scale, and personalisation with which that desire is now met.

For most of the twentieth century, the newspaper was the dominant news medium, supplemented from the 1920s onward by radio, which offered something print never could: immediacy. A radio bulletin could reach a national audience within minutes of an event occurring, a genuine novelty at the time. Television, arriving in most households by the 1950s and '60s, added images to that immediacy, and with them, an entirely new kind of intimacy between broadcaster and audience. News anchors became familiar, trusted faces in people's living rooms, and television's dominance over public attention lasted, largely unchallenged, for roughly four decades.

The internet did not so much add a new medium to this line-up as dissolve the boundaries between all the others. A newspaper's website could now update as quickly as a radio bulletin. A streaming video could reach a global audience the way a national broadcast once reached a national one, only bigger. Perhaps more significantly, the internet also collapsed the distinction between media producer and media consumer: for the first time, ordinary people could publish something to a potentially enormous audience without needing to own a printing press or a broadcast licence.

This shift has had consequences that are still being argued over. On one hand, it has diversified whose voices get heard, breaking the near-monopoly that a small number of large organisations once held over public information. On the other, it has made verifying the credibility of any given source far harder for the average reader, and has been blamed, fairly or not, for the rapid spread of misinformation, growing political polarisation, and dramatically shortened public attention spans.

What seems clear is that each new medium rarely replaces its predecessor outright. Radio did not kill the newspaper, and television did not kill radio; each earlier format simply found a smaller, more specialised role. Whether the same pattern will hold as the internet continues to evolve, or whether social media and algorithm-driven feeds represent something genuinely new in the history of mass communication, remains one of the more contested questions in media studies today.

Grammar note — relative clauses

The passage uses several relative clauses to add extra information without starting a new sentence, e.g.:

  • "...a then little-known computer scientist called Tim Berners-Lee, who was working at CERN..."
  • "...a social networking website that allows people to post short messages..."
  • "...a successor which was intended to help astronomers understand how galaxies evolve..."

Notice that who is used for people, which for things, and that can often be used for either in less formal writing.

Grammar note — reporting verbs and hedging language

Academic and journalistic writing often avoids stating opinions as flat fact, using reporting verbs and hedging language instead, e.g.:

  • "...say the worriers, losing the ability to apply ourselves..."
  • "Carr suspects his ability to read deeply has been dented."
  • "...he admits that it provides a healthier environment..." (from yesterday's reading, same pattern)
  • "Buckingham concluded: 'the evidence... is weak and inconclusive.'"

This lets a writer present a claim without fully committing to it as their own established fact — useful for Writing Task 2 when presenting other people's views.
