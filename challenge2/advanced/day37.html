The Student Who Studied Less

Lead-in · Learning · 149 words

ADilnoza used to study for six hours every evening before an exam. She read her notes again and again and highlighted almost every line. By the end, the notes felt very familiar — but in the exam, she often could not remember the answers.

BLast year, a teacher suggested a different method. Instead of rereading, Dilnoza closed her notebook and tried to write down everything she could remember. Then she checked what she had missed. She also stopped studying everything the night before, and instead reviewed each topic a few times, with a few days between each review.

CAt first, it felt harder and slower. ‘I thought I was learning less, because I kept making mistakes,’ she says. But her results improved quickly, and she now studies for about three hours a day instead of six. ‘The mistakes were the learning,’ she explains. ‘Rereading only made me feel clever.’

Mini Reading Test — The Science of Remembering

A short check-up test: 10 questions, about 13 minutes.

The Science of Remembering

Mini reading test · Psychology · 375 words

AIn 1885, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus conducted one of the first scientific studies of memory, using himself as the only subject. To avoid the influence of meaning, he memorised lists of nonsense syllables such as ‘WID’ and ‘ZOF’ and measured how quickly he forgot them. More than half of each list was lost within an hour and around two-thirds within a day, after which forgetting slowed considerably. His ‘forgetting curve’ has proved remarkably durable: when researchers in Amsterdam repeated the experiment in 2015, they obtained a very similar result.

BEbbinghaus also observed that each review of a list made the curve less steep. This ‘spacing effect’ underlies the spaced-repetition apps now used by millions of language learners, which schedule reviews at gradually increasing intervals. Research suggests that the ideal gap between reviews grows with the length of time the information needs to be remembered.

CUnfortunately, the strategy most students prefer — rereading notes repeatedly shortly before an exam — exploits none of these findings. Rereading creates what psychologists call an ‘illusion of fluency’: material becomes familiar, and learners mistake this familiarity for mastery. Such ‘massed practice’ can produce good results in a test the following day, but the information is rapidly forgotten.

DA more effective approach is retrieval practice. In a 2006 study at Washington University, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found that students who read a text once and then attempted to recall it three times remembered far more after a week than students who read it four times. Strikingly, the rereaders predicted that they would perform better.

EA related technique is ‘interleaving’ — mixing different types of problems rather than practising one type at a time. Interleaving feels harder and leads to more errors during practice, yet it produces better long-term results. The psychologist Robert Bjork has described such techniques as ‘desirable difficulties’: obstacles that slow learning in the short term but strengthen it in the long term.

FSleep completes the picture, since memories are consolidated while we sleep. For teachers, the implications are clear: schedule reviews over weeks rather than days, and use frequent low-stakes quizzes. Yet many students still rely on rereading and highlighting, techniques that a major 2013 review led by John Dunlosky judged to be of ‘low utility’.
