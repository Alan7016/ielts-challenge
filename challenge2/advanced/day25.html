Ninety-Minute Days: Life Aboard the Space Station

Science · Space · 362 words

Around four hundred kilometres above our heads, a structure roughly the size of a football pitch circles the Earth about every ninety minutes. The International Space Station has been continuously occupied since the year 2000, which means that for more than two decades there has always been at least one human being living off the planet. Its crews, drawn from several countries, typically stay for around six months at a time.

Life on the station is shaped above all by weightlessness. Nothing stays where you put it, so tools, food packets and even pens must be attached to surfaces with straps or Velcro. Astronauts sleep in small bags fixed to the wall, since in the absence of gravity there is no difference between lying down and standing up. Food is designed not to produce crumbs, which could float into equipment or into someone’s eyes, and the toilets use a flow of air rather than gravity to carry waste away.

The working day is planned almost to the minute by teams on the ground. Much of it is devoted to scientific experiments — on materials, plants, the human body and much else — together with the constant maintenance that a complex machine requires. A surprisingly large share of each day, about two hours, goes on exercise. Without gravity to work against, muscles shrink and bones lose density, and the treadmill, exercise bike and resistance machine on board are the main defence against this.

There is a psychological side, too. Crew members live in a confined, noisy environment, far from their families, with sixteen sunrises and sunsets every twenty-four hours. Agencies select astronauts partly for their ability to cope with these conditions, and they keep a close watch on crew members’ mental well-being throughout a mission.

Much of what has been learned on the station will matter for longer journeys. A return trip to Mars would last far longer than a stay on the station, with no possibility of a quick return home, and every problem the station has solved — from recycling water to keeping a crew healthy and getting along — will have to be solved again, on a much larger scale.

Cleaning Up Orbit: The Problem of Space Junk
The space around our planet is filling up with rubbish travelling faster than a bullet. Can anything be done before it is too late?

Reading Passage 1 · Space technology · 745 words

AWhen the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 in 1957, the space around the Earth was empty. Since then, thousands of rockets have carried satellites into orbit, and much of what they left behind is still up there. Dead satellites, discarded rocket stages, flakes of paint and fragments from explosions now circle the planet alongside the working satellites on which modern life depends. Collectively, this material is known as space debris or, less formally, space junk.

BThe scale of the problem is difficult to picture. Tracking networks on the ground follow tens of thousands of objects larger than about ten centimetres. Below that size, objects are too small to track reliably, but statistical models suggest that there are roughly a million pieces between one and ten centimetres across, and well over a hundred million smaller ones. Most of this material is concentrated in low Earth orbit, the busy region within about two thousand kilometres of the surface where the International Space Station and most observation satellites operate.

CWhat makes even tiny fragments dangerous is their speed. An object in low Earth orbit travels at around 28,000 kilometres per hour — roughly ten times faster than a bullet — and when two objects collide, their combined speed can be higher still. At such velocities, a fragment the size of a marble can strike with the energy of a small car crash, and a flake of paint has been known to chip a window on a spacecraft. Satellites can be shielded against the smallest particles, but no practical shield can protect against a piece the size of a tennis ball.

DTwo events in particular made the dangers impossible to ignore. In 2007, a missile test deliberately destroyed an old weather satellite, creating thousands of trackable fragments in a single moment. Two years later, an active communications satellite and a defunct Russian satellite collided accidentally over Siberia — the first such collision between two whole satellites — scattering another cloud of debris. Most of the pieces produced by these two events are still in orbit today.

EScientists have long warned of a worse scenario. As early as the late 1970s, researchers suggested that once the amount of debris passed a certain point, collisions would produce fragments faster than natural processes could remove them, and each collision would make further collisions more likely. The result, in theory, would be a chain reaction that could make some orbits unusable for generations. Whether this point has already been reached in certain orbits is still a matter of debate among experts, but few dispute that the risk is growing.

FNature does provide some help. Objects in the lowest orbits are gradually slowed by the thin upper atmosphere, and eventually they fall and burn up, often within a few years. Higher up, however, the air is so thin that debris can remain in orbit for centuries. This is why international guidelines have for many years recommended that satellites in low orbit should be removed within twenty-five years of the end of their mission, either by lowering them so that they burn up or, for higher satellites, by moving them into a so-called graveyard orbit away from busy regions. Compliance with these guidelines, however, has been far from complete, and some regulators have recently demanded much shorter deadlines.

GMeanwhile, the number of satellites is rising faster than ever. Several companies are launching constellations of thousands of small satellites to provide internet access from space, and the number of active satellites has multiplied within a few years. Supporters argue that these satellites operate in low orbits, are designed to remove themselves at the end of their lives and can steer automatically to avoid collisions. Critics reply that even a small failure rate, multiplied across thousands of satellites, could add significantly to the problem.

HPreventing new debris is cheaper than removing old debris, but removal may eventually be unavoidable. Engineers have experimented with a range of methods for capturing dead objects, including nets, harpoons, robotic arms and magnetic docking plates fitted to satellites before launch. An experimental mission in 2018, for example, successfully tested both a net and a harpoon on targets it had released itself. None of these methods is yet cheap enough to use on a large scale, and there is a further obstacle that is legal rather than technical: under international law, every object in space still belongs to the country that launched it, and no one may remove another nation’s debris without permission.

Why Mars?

Science · Planetary exploration · 348 words

Of all the planets in the solar system, why has Mars attracted so much attention? Venus is closer to us, but its surface is hot enough to melt lead and lies beneath a crushing atmosphere of carbon dioxide and clouds of acid. The giant outer planets have no solid surface to land on at all. Mars, by contrast, is cold and dry, but it has a solid surface, a day only a little longer than ours, and seasons. Of all our neighbours, it is the one that looks most like a place where people might one day live.

It is also reachable. Depending on the positions of the two planets, a spacecraft can travel from Earth to Mars in roughly six to nine months. Because both planets are moving, the best opportunities to launch come only about once every two years, which is why missions to Mars tend to leave in groups.

The main scientific reason for interest, however, is water. Images from orbiting spacecraft show what look like dried-up river valleys and deltas, and rovers on the surface have found rocks that could only have formed in the presence of liquid water. Billions of years ago, scientists believe, Mars was warmer and wetter, with lakes and possibly even a shallow sea. Where there was liquid water, there may have been life.

That is why today’s rovers are equipped not just with cameras but with drills and chemical laboratories. They are searching for traces of the kinds of molecules that living things leave behind, and one rover has been collecting rock samples in sealed tubes so that a future mission might bring them back to Earth, where they can be studied with far more powerful instruments.

No one expects to find animals or plants. If life ever existed on Mars, it was almost certainly microscopic, and it may have died out long ago. But the discovery of even the simplest past life on another planet would be one of the most important findings in the history of science — evidence that life is not unique to the Earth.

Should Humans Go to Mars?
Sending people to another planet would be the greatest adventure in human history. But is it the right priority — and are we ready?

Reading Passage 2 · Space and society · 656 words

AFor more than half a century, a human landing on Mars has been described as being about twenty years away. Today, with several space agencies and private companies announcing ambitious plans, the goal seems closer than ever. Yet the question of whether we should go deserves more careful thought than it usually receives, because the case for a crewed mission is weaker in some respects, and stronger in others, than either its enthusiasts or its critics tend to admit.

BThe most popular argument for going is that humanity needs a ‘second home’. If an asteroid strike, a pandemic or a nuclear war were to make the Earth uninhabitable, a settlement on Mars would allow our species to survive. I find this argument unconvincing. Even after a catastrophe, the Earth would remain far more habitable than Mars, which has almost no atmosphere, no protection from radiation and an average surface temperature far below freezing. It would be easier, and much cheaper, to build shelters in Antarctica or under the sea than to create a self-sufficient city on another planet.

CThe scientific argument is more persuasive. Robotic rovers have achieved remarkable things, but they are slow and cautious. Because radio signals take up to about twenty minutes to travel between the planets in each direction, rovers cannot be driven in real time, and a task that a trained geologist could complete in an afternoon may take a rover weeks. Many scientists believe that if traces of ancient life exist on Mars, a human explorer, able to judge where to look and to react instantly, would be far more likely to find them.

DThe difficulties, however, are formidable. A round trip, including the time spent waiting on the surface for the planets to line up again for the journey home, would probably last between two and three years. During that time the crew would be exposed to cosmic radiation at levels well above those considered safe on Earth, and long periods in weightlessness would weaken their muscles, bones and possibly their eyesight. Unlike astronauts on the space station, they could not be rescued or sent home if something went wrong. Every piece of equipment, every medicine and every meal would have to be brought from Earth or produced on Mars.

EThen there is the cost. Estimates for a crewed Mars programme vary enormously, but all of them run into many tens of billions of dollars. Critics argue that this money would be better spent on urgent problems on Earth, such as climate change or poverty. This objection is understandable, but in my view it rests on a false choice. Space budgets are small compared with most areas of government spending, and the technologies developed for space — in materials, medicine, recycling and computing — have often brought benefits far beyond the space industry.

FA further concern is rarely discussed outside scientific circles: contamination. Humans carry enormous numbers of microbes, and no spacesuit or habitat is perfectly sealed. If Earth microbes were released on Mars, they might make it impossible to tell whether any life later discovered there was truly Martian. Some scientists therefore argue that humans should not land until robotic missions have studied the most promising sites thoroughly. I believe this caution is justified.

GWhat, then, should we do? My own conclusion is that a human mission to Mars is worth attempting, but not yet, and not for the reasons usually given. The idea of Mars as a lifeboat for humanity is a distraction. The real prize is knowledge: the chance to answer whether life has ever arisen anywhere other than on Earth. That prize is best pursued by first sending more capable robots, returning rock samples for study, and solving the problems of radiation and long-term health. When — and only when — those steps have been taken, the first human footprint on Mars will be not a stunt, but the beginning of a serious scientific undertaking.
