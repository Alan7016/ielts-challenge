The Shopping Brain

Psychology · Consumer behaviour · 354 words

Most of us like to think that we shop rationally: we notice a need, compare options and buy the best one. Yet anyone who has come home from a quick trip for milk carrying a bag of things they never intended to buy knows that the reality is rather different. A substantial share of what people buy is bought on impulse, and the reasons have more to do with psychology than with the products themselves.

Part of the explanation lies in how the brain handles reward. Research using brain scans suggests that the pleasure of shopping is often strongest not when we own something but when we are about to get it. The anticipation itself is rewarding, which is why browsing can feel so enjoyable and why the excitement of a new purchase so often fades once it is home.

Retailers have become skilled at triggering this feeling. One of the oldest techniques is the crossed-out price: a jacket marked '£120 £60' feels like a bargain, even if it was never sold at the higher price for long. The first number acts as an anchor, and every later number is judged against it. A second technique is scarcity. Messages such as 'only two left in stock' or 'offer ends at midnight' create a fear of missing out that pushes people to decide quickly rather than carefully. A third is what psychologists call social proof: when we learn that thousands of others have bought something, we assume it must be worth having.

Online shops use all of these methods, and they can do so with far greater precision than any physical store. Because they record every click, they can test which colour of button or which wording of a message produces the most sales, and then show it to millions of customers within hours.

None of this means that shoppers are helpless. The most effective defence, according to many consumer experts, is simply to recognise these techniques when they appear. A shopper who notices the anchor, questions the countdown clock and ignores the crowd is far more likely to buy what they actually came for.

Designed to Make You Spend
Behind the friendly aisles of a modern supermarket lies a carefully engineered plan to shape what — and how much — we buy.

Reading Passage · Retail psychology · 727 words

AThe average shopper walks into a supermarket believing that their choices are their own. Industry surveys, however, regularly suggest that a large proportion of supermarket purchases are not planned before the shopper enters the store. This is no accident. Over several decades, retailers have studied customers in remarkable detail — filming them, tracking their routes and analysing their receipts — and the layout of a typical store now reflects what they have learned.

BThe process begins before shoppers have taken more than a few steps. The first area inside the door is often called the transition zone, because people entering it are still adjusting to the change in light, noise and temperature. While they adjust, they tend to ignore what is around them, so experienced retailers rarely place important products there. Instead, the area is kept relatively open or used for large seasonal displays that set the mood. Observers who film shoppers also report that most people turn to the right after entering, which is why many stores put their most attractive displays along the right-hand wall.

CPerhaps the best-known feature of supermarket design is the position of everyday essentials such as milk, eggs and bread. These are almost always found at the back of the store. Since nearly every customer needs at least one of them, placing them as far as possible from the entrance forces shoppers to walk past hundreds of other products on the way. Every extra minute spent in the store — what the industry calls dwell time — increases the chance of an unplanned purchase.

DWithin each aisle, position matters just as much. Products placed at eye level sell far better than those on the top or bottom shelves, so this space is usually given to the brands that earn the store the most money. Products aimed at children, by contrast, are frequently placed on lower shelves, at a child's eye level. The shelves at the end of each aisle, known as end caps, are especially valuable: goods displayed there tend to sell well even when their price has not been reduced, because many shoppers simply assume that anything placed there must be a bargain.

EStores also appeal to the senses. In a well-known study carried out in an American supermarket in the 1980s, researchers changed the speed of the background music. When the music was slow, customers moved through the store more slowly and spent more money than when it was fast. The smell of freshly baked bread is used to stimulate appetite, and lighting is chosen with care: warm light can make fruit and vegetables look fresher than they really are.

FSurprisingly, offering more products does not always lead to more sales. In a famous experiment, researchers set up a tasting table in an upmarket food store, displaying either twenty-four varieties of jam or only six. The larger display attracted more passing shoppers, but only about 3 per cent of those who stopped went on to make a purchase. With the smaller display, around 30 per cent bought a jar. The researchers concluded that too much choice can make decisions harder rather than easier, and that shoppers faced with too many options may choose nothing at all.

GRetailers have learned to steer choices in another way too. When customers must choose between two similar products, adding a third option that is clearly worse than one of them — a so-called decoy — makes that product look more attractive by comparison. In one experiment described by a behavioural economist, students were offered three magazine subscriptions: online only, print only, and print plus online for the same price as print alone. Almost nobody chose the print-only option, yet its presence persuaded most students to choose the more expensive combined package. When it was removed, most chose the cheaper online subscription instead.

HDoes this mean supermarkets are deceiving their customers? Not necessarily. Many of these techniques simply make shopping more pleasant, and nobody is forced to buy anything. But shoppers who want to stay in control can take some simple steps: writing a list and sticking to it, avoiding shopping when hungry, and pausing before reaching for anything placed at eye level or at the end of an aisle. A store may be designed to make you spend, but knowing how it works is the first step towards spending only what you intended.
