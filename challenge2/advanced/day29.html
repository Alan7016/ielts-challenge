This episode of 6 Minute English explores lying and whether we can detect it. The presenters distinguish between small, polite untruths, known as white lies, and deception, which involves hiding something in order to gain an advantage. A neuroscientist explains that ordinary polite conversation often depends on not saying exactly what we think. A psychologist then describes some signs that may reveal a lie: liars tend to say less, take longer before they reply — a delay called response latency — and refer to themselves less often. Reading Passage 1 — The Changing Shape of the Family

Question types: TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN, matching information and sentence completion. Suggested time: 15 minutes. Answers are checked when you press Next.

The Changing Shape of the Family

Reading Passage 1 · Society · 338 words

AFor much of the twentieth century, the typical household in many industrialised countries was imagined as a married couple living with their children. Today that picture describes a shrinking minority of homes. Across Europe, North America and much of East Asia, households have become smaller, more varied and more likely to change over a person’s lifetime.

BOne of the most striking trends is the rise of people living alone. In some northern European cities, single-person households now make up close to half of all homes. This is not simply a sign of loneliness: many of those living alone are young professionals who value independence, or older people who have outlived a partner but wish to remain in their own homes.

CAt the same time, people are forming couples and having children later. In many countries the average age at first marriage has risen by several years since the 1970s, and a growing proportion of couples live together without marrying at all. Smaller families are also the norm: in large parts of the world, the average number of children per woman has fallen below the level needed to keep the population stable.

DYet one older pattern is making a surprising comeback. In several countries, the number of people living in multi-generational households — with grandparents, parents and adult children under one roof — has grown in recent years. Researchers point mainly to economic pressures, particularly the high cost of housing, which makes it difficult for young adults to leave home, together with the practical value of grandparents helping with childcare.

EFamilies created through remarriage, often called blended families or stepfamilies, are also increasingly common. Their members may have to negotiate new roles and loyalties, and research suggests that, with time and good communication, many such families function as well as any other.

FWhat do these changes mean? Some commentators warn that the decline of the traditional family weakens society. Others argue that the family is not disappearing but adapting, as it always has, to new economic and social conditions.

Reading Passage 2 — The Hidden Childcare Army

An opinion passage. Question types: YES / NO / NOT GIVEN, matching headings and summary completion. Suggested time: 15 minutes.

The Hidden Childcare Army

Reading Passage 2 · Family · 327 words

AIn many countries, grandparents have quietly become one of the most important sources of childcare. When both parents work, and when professional childcare is expensive or hard to find, it is often grandmothers and grandfathers who collect children from school, look after them during holidays and step in when a child is ill. Surveys in several European countries suggest that a large proportion of grandparents provide some form of regular care for their grandchildren.

BThe benefits for families are obvious. Parents save money, children are cared for by people who love them, and close relationships between generations can develop. For children, a strong bond with a grandparent can provide stability, especially during difficult periods such as a parents’ divorce. In my view, this contribution is seriously undervalued by governments, which rarely include it in their calculations of how childcare is provided.

CThere may also be benefits for the grandparents themselves. Some studies suggest that older people who look after grandchildren for a moderate amount of time tend to stay mentally sharper than those who do not, perhaps because caring keeps them active and socially involved. However, the same research indicates that very heavy caring responsibilities can have the opposite effect, leading to exhaustion and stress.

DThis is where I believe the current situation is unfair. Many grandparents feel unable to refuse when asked to help, and some have reduced their working hours or given up jobs entirely to provide care, with consequences for their own income and pensions. Unlike paid childminders, they receive no training, no holidays and, in most countries, no financial support.

ENone of this means that grandparents should stop helping. But if societies depend on this hidden army of carers, they should at least recognise it — for example, by allowing grandparents to share parental leave or by offering credits towards their pensions. Such measures would not only be fair; they would make it more likely that this valuable form of care can continue.
