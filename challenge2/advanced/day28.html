The Truth About Lying

Reading Passage 1 · Psychology · 503 words

AMost of us like to think of ourselves as honest, yet lying appears to be a normal part of everyday life. When researchers have asked volunteers to keep a diary of their conversations, many participants recorded at least one lie a day. The majority of these are so-called white lies — small untruths told to protect someone’s feelings or to avoid an awkward moment, such as praising a meal we did not enjoy. Serious, damaging lies are far less common.

BLying is also something we learn surprisingly early. Studies of young children suggest that many begin to tell simple lies at around the age of two or three, for example denying that they have eaten a forbidden sweet. Rather than being a cause for alarm, psychologists regard this as a sign of normal development: to lie successfully, a child must understand that other people have minds of their own and do not automatically know what the child knows.

CDeception is not limited to our own species. Some birds give false alarm calls, warning other animals of a predator that is not there, and then steal the food the frightened animals leave behind. Certain primates have been observed hiding food from more powerful members of their group. Whether these animals ‘know’ they are lying in the way humans do remains an open question, but the behaviour is undeniably deceptive.

DAsk people how to spot a liar and you will hear remarkably similar answers. In one international survey carried out in dozens of countries, the most common belief by far was that liars avoid eye contact; many people also mentioned nervous movements of the hands and shifting about in a chair. The problem is that careful comparisons of filmed liars and truth-tellers have found little support for these ideas. Liars do not reliably look away, and an honest person who is nervous about being disbelieved may fidget just as much as a liar.

ENot surprisingly, then, most of us are poor lie detectors. When the results of many laboratory experiments are combined, people turn out to identify lies correctly only slightly more often than they would by tossing a coin. Perhaps more surprisingly, police officers, customs staff and other professionals whose job involves judging honesty generally perform little better than ordinary members of the public, although they tend to be far more confident in their judgements.

FSo are there any clues worth looking for? The most promising evidence suggests that we should pay attention to what people say rather than how they look. Liars often give shorter accounts with fewer details, perhaps because every extra detail is another thing that could be checked, and they tend to make fewer references to themselves. Some researchers have also found that lying becomes harder when a speaker’s mind is busy: asking someone to tell their story in reverse order, for instance, can make an invented account fall apart. In short, if you want to catch a liar, it may be better to listen carefully than to watch closely.

An opinion passage. Question types: YES / NO / NOT GIVEN, summary completion with a word bank and multiple choice. Suggested time: 15 minutes.

Why Friendship Is Good for You

Reading Passage 2 · Society and health · 407 words

AFor most of human history, survival depended on belonging to a group, and our need for friends may be a legacy of that past. Today, a growing body of research suggests that friendship is not merely pleasant but has a measurable effect on health. People with strong social ties tend to recover more quickly from illness and to live longer, while loneliness has been linked to higher blood pressure, poorer sleep and a weaker immune system. Some researchers have even compared the health risks of long-term loneliness to those of smoking. Such comparisons are open to debate, but in my view the general message is hard to ignore: friendship is a health issue, not just a social one.

BHow many friends can a person have? One influential idea, proposed by an anthropologist in the 1990s, is that the human brain can manage only around 150 meaningful relationships. Within this number, he argued, relationships form layers: perhaps five very close friends, around fifteen good friends, and larger circles of people we know less well. The exact figures are disputed, and some studies have found much wider variation between individuals, but the idea that our closest circle is small seems well supported.

CFriendships also take time to build. One study that followed adults who had recently moved to a new city estimated that it took around fifty hours spent together to turn an acquaintance into a casual friend, and over two hundred hours to become close friends. This helps to explain why adults often find it harder than children to make new friends: work and family leave far less free time, and opportunities to meet the same people repeatedly are rarer.

DWhat about social media? It is often claimed that online contact has made us lonelier, but I think the reality is more complicated. Messaging apps clearly help people to stay in touch with friends who live far away, and for some isolated groups they are a lifeline. However, I am not convinced that a stream of likes and comments can replace the shared experiences that deepen a friendship. Time spent face to face, it seems, still matters most.

EIf friendship is so valuable, should we treat it as seriously as diet or exercise? I believe we should. Making time for friends, answering that message, or accepting an invitation when we would rather stay at home may be among the best investments we can make in our own wellbeing.

Question types: TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN, matching information and short-answer questions. Suggested time: 15 minutes.

Does Birth Order Shape Who We Are?

Reading Passage 3 · Family · 348 words

AFew ideas about family life are as popular as the belief that our position among our brothers and sisters shapes our personality. According to this view, first-born children are responsible and ambitious, youngest children are charming and rebellious, and those in the middle are diplomatic peacemakers. The idea was made famous in the early twentieth century by a psychologist who argued that each position in the family brings its own challenges, and it has remained a favourite topic of magazines and parenting books ever since.

BEarly research seemed to support it. However, many of these studies had a serious weakness: they compared children from different families, and families differ in many ways besides birth order, such as size and income. Larger families, for example, are more common in some social groups than others, so any difference between first-born and last-born children might have been caused by family background rather than by birth order itself.

CMore recent studies have tried to avoid this problem by using very large samples, sometimes involving tens of thousands of people, and by comparing brothers and sisters within the same family. Their conclusion is striking: once family background is taken into account, birth order has little or no measurable effect on personality traits such as confidence, emotional stability or imagination.

DThere is one area where a small difference does appear. On average, first-born children score slightly higher on intelligence tests than their younger siblings. The gap is small — a few points — and far too small to predict anything about an individual. Researchers suggest it may arise because first-borns enjoy their parents’ undivided attention for a time, or because they benefit from explaining things to younger brothers and sisters.

EWhy, then, does the belief remain so strong? One explanation is that within a family, older children are always older: they are bigger, more capable and given more responsibility than their siblings at any given moment. Parents may mistake these differences of age for differences of personality. Popular beliefs, once established, are also easily confirmed by memorable examples and rarely tested against the evidence.
